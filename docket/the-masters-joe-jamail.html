Joe Jamail

Every plaintiff’s trial lawyer in Houston is working in a house that Joe Jamail helped build. He was the King of Torts, the most successful trial lawyer this city ever produced, and he made his name on a faith worth stealing: that in a courtroom, and almost nowhere else, an ordinary person stands equal to the most powerful company in the world.

Jamail was Houston to the bone. Born in 1925, St. Thomas High School, then the University of Texas for college and for law school, and then more than sixty years trying cases in this city and this state. He was a plaintiff’s lawyer, which meant he spent his career on the side most of the profession’s money is not on, representing injured people and their families against corporations and insurers with limitless resources. He did it so well that he became, for a time, the wealthiest practicing lawyer in America, and he did it the hard way, one jury at a time.

What drove him was a conviction about what a courtroom is for. Outside its doors, a working family and a multinational company are not remotely equal; one has the money, the lawyers, the time. Inside, in front of twelve citizens, they are. The jury is the one institution built to make the powerful answer to the ordinary on even terms, and Jamail treated that as close to sacred. He took the cases where the little guy was outgunned, and he made the size of the opponent the point, not the problem.

The case that made him a legend was not a personal injury case at all. In the 1980s he represented Pennzoil against Texaco in a fight over a broken deal, and a Texas jury, persuaded by a Houston trial lawyer, returned one of the most consequential verdicts in the history of American corporate law, one that ultimately drove one of the largest oil companies in the world into bankruptcy. Lawyers still study it. But the thing to take from it is not its scale. It is that the same craft Jamail used to win a widow her due from an insurance company is the craft that brought a corporate giant to account. The work is the same. Only the stakes change.

Behind the folksy Texas charm and the fearsome reputation was a man who outworked everyone. Jamail’s power in front of a jury was not improvisation; it was preparation so total that it looked like ease. He knew the file better than the witnesses knew their own stories. He knew the documents cold, the medicine cold, the other side’s case better than the other side did. The confidence a jury felt coming off him was not a performance. It was the confidence of a man who had already done the work, and there is no shortcut to it and no substitute for it.

And for all his ferocity, his real gift with a jury was simplicity. He did not lecture twelve Texans; he talked to them, in plain words, about a wrong a decent person could feel. He understood that a jury does not decide on the law first. It decides whether something was fair, and then it looks for the law that lets it do what it already wants to do. So he made them feel the unfairness, cleanly and without clutter, and trusted them to carry it the rest of the way. That is not a trick. It is respect for the jury, and it is the whole game.

There is a particular reason to put him in this series, and it is personal to anyone who tries cases in this city. Jamail proved, on the biggest stage there is, that a Houston plaintiff’s lawyer with a jury and the truth can beat anyone. Not a firm with a thousand associates. Not a corporation with unlimited money. Him, a jury, and the work. That is the inheritance every trial lawyer in this town is working with, whether he knows it or not, and it is worth remembering on the nights when the other side looks too big to beat.

He died in 2015, and the University of Texas he loved is covered in his name, the chairs and the buildings and the statues. But his real monument is not carved. It is the standing assumption, in every Texas courtroom, that an ordinary person gets a fair fight against a powerful one. Joe Jamail did more than anyone to make that assumption feel true. The least the rest of us can do is keep proving it.

Joseph Dahr Jamail Jr. (1925 to 2015), the Houston trial lawyer known as the King of Torts; a graduate of the University of Texas and its law school, he built a landmark plaintiff’s practice representing injured people and their families, and won one of the most consequential jury verdicts in American corporate history in Pennzoil’s case against Texaco. A major benefactor of the University of Texas, his name marks its law school, its swimming center, and much of its campus. Considered here for the craft, and for what a Houston trial lawyer inherits from him. This is general information, not legal advice.
