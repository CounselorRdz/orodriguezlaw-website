Two Cars, One Gone

Leer este parte en español →

Late Tuesday night a man crossing a Houston freeway was struck by a vehicle that kept going, and the impact put him in the path of a second car, whose driver stopped. He died at the scene. One driver is a witness. The other is a question.

What was reported. Houston police said the crash happened at about 10:20 p.m. on November 17 in the 5400 block of the North Freeway. The man was trying to cross when an unknown vehicle hit him, and that impact caused him to be hit by a second car. “HPD said the first driver fled without stopping to help,” and the second driver “stayed at the scene.” Police asked anyone with information to call the department’s Hit and Run Unit, and the man had not been identified (ABC13, November 18). This page names no one and decides nothing about fault.

What the law asked of both drivers. A Texas driver in a collision that results, or is reasonably likely to result, in injury or death must “immediately stop the vehicle at the scene of the collision or as close to the scene as possible,” must go back if he did not stop, must “immediately determine whether a person is involved in the collision” and whether that person needs aid, and must stay until he has given his information and rendered aid as Section 550.023 requires. When the collision results in death, failing to do so is “a felony of the second degree” (Section 550.021; Render Aid). The driver who stayed did the first thing the statute requires: stop. That says nothing, one way or the other, about whether either driver was careless.

Two impacts, one body. When two vehicles strike the same person, the case has to sort out which impact did what. That is work for the medical examiner and a crash reconstructionist, and the answer comes from the autopsy, the marks on each vehicle and the evidence on the road (Inquest; No Witnesses). Texas then has the jury assign a percentage of responsibility to each claimant, each defendant, each settling person and each responsible third party designated under Section 33.004, even one who is not a party to the suit (Section 33.003; Comparative Fault).

The driver who was never found. The law has a place even for him. A court must let a defendant designate an unknown person as a responsible third party if, within 60 days after filing its original answer, it alleges that “an unknown person committed a criminal act that was a cause of the loss or injury,” pleads facts that give the court “a reasonable probability” the act was criminal, and states every identifying characteristic of that person it knew when it answered. The unknown person “is denominated as ‘Jane Doe’ or ‘John Doe’ until the person’s identity is known” (Section 33.004(j), (k); Responsible Third Party). The rule asks for a criminal act that helped cause the harm, not only a crime committed afterward, so whether a hit-and-run driver fits it depends on the facts of what that driver did before leaving.

Where a family’s own coverage fits. Uninsured motorist coverage can answer for a driver who is never identified, but the Insurance Code requires that “actual physical contact must have occurred” between the unknown driver’s vehicle and the insured or the insured’s property (Section 1952.104). As reported, the first vehicle struck the man. Whether a particular policy covers a particular person, on foot or in a car, is a question for that policy (Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims; Contact).

What lasts. The second car and its damage, pieces of the first vehicle left on the road, any freeway camera, the 911 calls, the autopsy, and the account of the driver who stayed, who may be the only person who saw what came first. Each of them can be lost (Spoliation).

The law asked the same thing of both drivers: stop. One did. Any case that follows will ask what each one did before that, and the answer starts with what the road and the two vehicles still hold.
