The Fifth CircuitDecided · No. 26-50184

Berg v. Rivas

COURT: U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, on appeal from the Western District of Texas (San Antonio).
DECIDED: November 24, 2026, No. 26-50184 (per curiam; Ho, Engelhardt and Wilson, JJ.). Unpublished, and so not precedent under the court’s Rule 47.5. Affirmed.
WHY IT MATTERS: Filming the police is protected. The question in a retaliation case is whether what the officers did would have stopped an ordinary person from filming, and here the court said the fact that he kept filming “at least suggests” the spotlight would not have stopped an ordinary person.

A man stands by his car at a gas pump at night, filming a traffic stop on his phone. An officer turns a spotlight on his camera for about six seconds. Another tells him he will go to jail if he gets near the stop. He keeps recording. The court held that neither act would have stopped an ordinary person from filming, and said the fact that he kept recording at least suggested as much.

The scene

“On April 11, 2024, at approximately 10:36 p.m.,” Berg was recording a traffic stop “on his cell phone from beside his parked car at a gas pump” in San Antonio. “The area was well lit.” One officer aimed a spotlight “at Berg’s camera for around six seconds,” and Berg moved out of its path almost at once. A second officer told him, “if you get near this traffic stop, you’re gonna go to jail,” and repeated the warning. Berg sued the two officers and the City of San Antonio without a lawyer, claiming retaliation for exercising his First Amendment rights (Berg v. Rivas). The district court dismissed the complaint, so the facts on appeal are the ones Berg alleged.

The right

The right to record is settled in the Fifth Circuit. In 2017 the court held that “a First Amendment right to record the police does exist, subject only to reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions” (Turner v. Driver). In the same opinion it held that “there was no clearly established First Amendment right to record the police at the time of Turner’s activities” in 2015, so the officers there were immune. Since that decision, the right has been on the books. Berg’s recording, the panel agreed, was protected activity.

The test

A retaliation claim has three parts, and the court decided this one on the second: whether the officers’ conduct “would chill a person of ordinary firmness from continuing to engage in that activity.” The panel quoted an older case for the rule that “a retaliation claim requires some showing that the plaintiffs’ exercise of free speech has been curtailed.” On the spotlight, it held that “[the officer’s] spotlight curtailed Berg’s speech, but ‘trivial[ly]’ so”: it was turned on, and he moved aside. On the warning, it held that the perimeter the second officer set “did not curtail Berg’s speech at all.” And on the recording itself, the panel said Berg was “partially correct” that his persistence could not be held against him, because he did not have to stop recording altogether, but that “his completely uninterrupted recording following the alleged adverse conduct at least suggests” that the spotlight would not have chilled a person of ordinary firmness.

With no constitutional violation, the officers were entitled to qualified immunity, and the claim against the city failed with it: “without a predicate constitutional violation, there can be no Monell liability.”

What it settles, and what it does not

The opinion is unpublished, so it binds no other court. It does not narrow the right to record. What it shows is how the retaliation test is applied: the question is not whether officers were rude or the light was annoying, but whether what they did would stop an ordinary person, and the court looked at what the person filming actually did next. A case like this is built from the recording itself, second by second, and from what the officers’ own cameras show. For the qualified immunity framework the court applied, see Immunity; for a published decision on the same two questions in a force case, see Searles v. City of Houston.
