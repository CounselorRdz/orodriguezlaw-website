Stable

Leer este parte en español →

Stable is the word a family holds onto in the first hours. After a hard side impact it can also be the most misleading word in the room, because the injury that decides the case is often the one that has not finished happening yet.

The pattern. At an intersection, one vehicle is struck in the side by another. On the side of a car there is only a door; the blow reaches the person on the struck side almost at once, through a door, a window and a pillar a few inches away (Broadside). The person is pulled out talking. A doctor says the word everyone wants to hear: stable. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The chest. A broken rib is one of the causes of a traumatic pneumothorax, air in the space around the lung that lets the lung collapse. A reference for doctors explains why that can turn from quiet to dangerous: “Traumatic pneumothoraces can result from blunt or penetrating trauma; these often create a 1-way valve in the pleural space (letting the airflow in but not flow out) and hence hemodynamic compromise,” and “A tension pneumothorax can cause severe hypotension (obstructive shock) and even death” (StatPearls, Pneumothorax). In plain words, air gets in with each breath and cannot get out, and as the pressure builds, the blood pressure can fall dangerously.

The older chest. For older people the arithmetic is harder. A trauma-center study compared 277 patients sixty-five and older who had broken ribs with a control group of 187 randomly selected patients aged 18 to 64. It found that “Mortality and pneumonia rates increased as the number of rib fxs increased,” with an “odds ratio for death of 1.19 and for pneumonia of 1.16 per each additional rib fracture”; the “mortality was 22% for the elderly versus 10% for the young,” and “Pneumonia occurred in 31% of elderly versus 17% of young.” The study’s conclusion: “Elderly patients who sustain blunt chest trauma with rib fxs have twice the mortality and thoracic morbidity of younger patients with similar injuries” (Journal of Trauma, 2000). A 2024 study opens by citing that research: older patients with rib fractures from blunt chest trauma face a higher risk of death and lung complications, “and the risk increases with the number of fractured ribs” (Geriatric Orthopaedic Surgery and Rehabilitation, 2024). Stable on the first day does not settle the last.

What the law asks. Having the green light does not end the case. Every driver, with the light or without it, may not drive “at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing,” and the same section names approaching and crossing an intersection among the times that call for a reduced speed (Transportation Code Section 545.351). Texas then divides responsibility by percentages among the people whose conduct caused the harm (Comparative Fault). And the person who caused the crash takes the injured person as found: a fragile body is not a defense, though the injury must still be proved to have come from the crash (Eggshell).

The record. The proof of who had the light is not only the witnesses. It is the timing of the signal and the data each car keeps, and both can be gone while the family is still at the hospital. Ask for them to be preserved now (Spoliation). And a relative called stable is a person to keep watching, not a case that is closed.

Broadside said that on the side of a car there is only a door. The Third Collision said the injury that decides the case is often inside the body, where the photographs do not reach (The Third Collision). Smoke and Hours Later said the worst of an injury can show itself after everyone has been sent home. The chest belongs on that list.
