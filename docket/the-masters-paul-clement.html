Paul Clement

Leer esta página en español →

Paul Clement treats an oral argument as a conversation. Writing about an argument he gave in January 2026, the legal journalist David Lat said Clement sees each one as “a conversation with nine justices.” He is here for what a conversation takes: listening, a plan for when the bench says no, and honesty about the weak spot.

He studied at Georgetown’s School of Foreign Service, at Cambridge and at Harvard Law School, and he clerked for Judge Laurence H. Silberman of the federal appeals court in Washington and for Justice Antonin Scalia. He was Principal Deputy Solicitor General for more than three years and Acting Solicitor General for nearly a year, and from June 2005 until June 2008 he was the 43rd Solicitor General of the United States. He is a partner at Clement & Murphy. The American Academy of Arts and Sciences said in 2025 that he had argued more than 100 cases in the Supreme Court, “more Supreme Court cases since 2000 than any lawyer in or out of government”; by May 2026 he had “notched 128 arguments before the court by his count,” Bloomberg Government reported.

The preparation starts with other people. His preferred method is the moot court, a practice argument before a panel playing the judges, and he is particular about the panel: “Your moot courts are only going to be as good as the people you get to participate in them.” He wants people “who are picking up the briefs fresh, like the justices will.” And he does not pretend the nerves go away. “You’re also exceptionally prepared,” he told Harvard Law Today in 2023, “precisely because you’re so nervous.”

Then the argument. Lat drew three lessons from it. Listen, listen, listen: Clement took the questions and comments the justices had put to the lawyer who argued before him and “integrated them seamlessly into his own argument.” Stay nimble: when the bench was skeptical, he moved to a “backup argument,” then to “a backup to the backup.” And keep it conversational and candid: Lat called him “unfailingly honest with the justices,” willing to name a weak point out loud rather than hope no one noticed it. In the Harvard interview, Clement singled out one of his own answers to Justice Scalia as the kind that works, one that was “totally responsive to the question.”

Why he is here. Because a trial lawyer argues to people too, a judge at a hearing, a jury at the close, and the same habits carry. Listen to the question that was asked and answer that one. Have the second theory ready before the court rejects the first, as when a judge will not treat a broken traffic rule as negligence by itself and the case has to stand on ordinary care (Negligence Per Se). Practice the opening on people who have never seen the file, because that is who the jury will be. And say the weak fact before the other side does. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside another former Solicitor General, Ted Olson, and Lisa Blatt. And because he learned the Court from the inside, as a clerk to Scalia.

Most lawyers prepare a speech. Clement prepares for a conversation, and he expects to lose the first plan.
