Cold Shock

Leer esta página en español →

Cold water does not wait to make a person cold. It takes the breath first. The danger is in the first minutes, long before the body’s core has time to chill.

The gasp. Physiologists call it the cold shock response. A review in The Journal of Physiology describes it as “sympathetically mediated tachycardia, a respiratory gasp, uncontrollable hyperventilation,” along with “peripheral vasoconstriction and hypertension” (Shattock and Tipton, 2012). In plain words: a racing heart, a reflex gasp, breathing that will not take orders, and blood pressure climbing as the vessels in the skin clamp down. A study of the response measured the gasp “within the first 10 s of immersion” (Eglin and others, 2015).

The minutes. The National Weather Service says the gasping and rapid breathing occur “during the first 2-3 minutes or more,” and that “This uncontrolled rapid breathing can quickly create a drowning emergency if you inhale water and cannot stay afloat” (NWS). A gasp taken with the face under water is water, not air.

Not that cold. The water does not have to be near freezing. The Weather Service says the gasp can be “triggered by water as warm as 77°F (25°C),” and that cold shock “can be just as severe and dangerous from water temperatures of 50-60°F (10-15°C) as it is from water at 35°F (2°C)” (NWS). Water a person would call cool, not cold, is enough to start it.

The strong swimmer. The 2012 review cites a 1977 British Home Office report that “67% of drownings occur in strong swimmers and 55% of these are within 3 m of a safe haven,” and it notes that these early deaths are usually put down to drowning, or sometimes hypothermia, “even though the period of immersion is too short (<30 min) for dangerous levels of hypothermia to occur.” The authors offer a hypothesis for some of those early deaths: the body’s cold shock response and its diving response firing at once, a clash they call “autonomic conflict” that can throw off the heart’s rhythm (Shattock and Tipton, 2012). Whatever the mechanism in a given death, the point stands: in cold water, the first minutes can kill people who know how to swim.

What it means for a case. When someone ends up in cold water, from a car, a boat, a dock or a work platform over the water, the other side may say the person should have swum to safety. The physiology answers that the first minutes are not a swimming test. The questions that matter are the ones every case asks: how the person came to be in the water, and what should have kept them out (High Water). And if the person was conscious in those minutes, what they went through is part of the claim, but it has to be proved, not assumed (Conscious).

Cold water’s first weapon is not the cold. It is the breath. Any account of what someone should have done in the water has to begin with the minute when no one decides how to breathe.
