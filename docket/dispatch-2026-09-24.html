The Wrong Lane

Leer este parte en español →

Almost every crash this strand writes about happens on one side of a painted line. The worst ones happen when a car crosses it. A vehicle that drifts or veers into the oncoming lane does not simply have a wreck; it turns the other driver’s lane into the crash, and the other driver’s lawful speed into part of the impact. That is the quiet arithmetic of a head-on. The two speeds do not average. They add.

The count. Head-on crashes killed 555 people on Texas roads in 2025, in a year when 3,769 people died on those roads, one every 2 hours 19 minutes, and not a single day passed without a death. Another 1,201 died when a single vehicle ran off the road. Leaving the lane to the right puts a car in a ditch or against a tree. Leaving it to the left puts it in front of somebody.

What the pattern says. The physics is why a head-on at ordinary highway speeds is so violent. Two cars at highway speed meet at the sum of their speeds, and the energy the crash has to spend climbs with the square of that sum, so a head-on at a combined hundred miles an hour carries about four times the energy of one at fifty, not twice. The bodies inside keep their speed until belt, airbag, and folding metal spend it in the inches and hundredths of a second there are, which is what Closing Speed and The Third Collision are about. The driver who stayed in his lane brought his own speed. The driver who crossed the line brought the collision.

What the law does with it. Texas law is not vague about which half of the road is yours. On a roadway of sufficient width a driver “shall drive on the right half of the roadway” (Transportation Code Section 545.051), with narrow exceptions for passing, an obstruction, three marked lanes, or a one-way road, and a driver meeting oncoming traffic must keep to the right (Section 545.052). A rule that specific is the kind Negligence Per Se is built on, and the excuses Texas accepts for breaking it are few; Centerline walks through them, including the emergency a driver makes himself. The harder part is proof. The car that crossed is often destroyed, and its driver does not always live to explain it. The record is physical: the gouge marks that fix where the lanes were crossed, and the event data recorder that logged the last seconds, which is where Delta-V is read. That is why the car accidents Before You Call starts with preserving the vehicles. One honest caution: crossing the line is powerful evidence, not a verdict. Texas still asks whether anything the other driver did played a part (Comparative Fault). Driving the limit in your own lane is not, by itself, fault; the question is whether the speed was reasonable for the conditions, which is what Squared is about.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the numbers say about Texas roads, next to the pages that explain why the line down the middle of the road is a rule.
