The most common crash in a work zone is not a car hitting the work. It is a car hitting the back of the line of traffic the work created, and the case turns on whether anyone warned the driver the line was there.

The count. “Rear-end collisions are the most common kind of work zone crashes,” the Texas Department of Transportation tells drivers, and it counted more than 28,000 crashes and 203 deaths in Texas work zones in 2025. The Work Zone reported who died in them. This one is about the crash that happens most.

What the pattern says. When a lane closes and more cars arrive than the narrowed road can pass, a line forms and grows backward, toward traffic still moving at highway speed. The federal traffic-control manual warns that the stopped or slow line “might extend past the normal location of the signs” (Federal Highway Administration). On a freeway the same manual already pushes the warning signs out as far as half a mile or more, because “drivers are conditioned to uninterrupted flow.” A line that outgrows its signs puts stopped cars ahead of the first warning a driver sees. At seventy, the back of that line behaves almost like The Fixed Object, and the time it takes to see it and react, which The Reaction measures, comes out of room the driver no longer has.

What the law does with it. Two duties meet at the back of the line. The driver behind owes an “assured clear distance,” enough room to stop (Transportation Code Section 545.062), a duty of reasonable care that a jury measures, as The Gap explains, not the automatic breach Negligence Per Se describes. A speed over a posted work-zone limit is prima facie evidence against the driver (Section 545.352), evidence and not a verdict. The second duty is the warning, and it belongs to the people who set up the zone. Texas shields a contractor building or repairing a road for the Texas Department of Transportation if, when the crash happened, it was “in compliance with contract documents material to the condition or defect that was the proximate cause” of the harm (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 97.002). That makes the traffic-control plan, and whether the zone on the road matched it, one of the first records a work-zone case needs. A contractor that was following the plan on the point that caused the crash has that shield; one that was not, does not.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A crash into stopped traffic is decided before the brakes: by the room the driver behind was required to keep, and by the warning the zone was supposed to give.
