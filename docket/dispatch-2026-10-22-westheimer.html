Summer 2028

A road that kills ten people on foot in a year is not an accident scene. It is a pattern with an address, and this week the state put a date on the fix: summer 2028. Between now and then the law on that road does not change, and it is worth knowing exactly what it already asks.

The road. KPRC reported Wednesday that Westheimer Road “was among the deadliest roads in the United States for pedestrians in 2022,” according to federal and state crash data cited in the station’s own investigation, that “Ten pedestrians were killed along Westheimer Road in a single year,” and that the data point to “the deadly 3.8-mile stretch between the West Loop and Fondren Road” (KPRC). The story is built around one family’s loss; the person is named there and is not named here. The Docket was on this road two weeks ago, in The Pole, for a crash at Westheimer and Hillcroft, inside that stretch, and the statewide count, 695 people on foot in 2025, is carried by Stranded.

The fix. The state highway department told the station it is “enhancing pedestrian safety along Westheimer Road with the installation of enhanced crosswalks at 23 intersections between Loop 610 and SH 6,” markings “designed to draw drivers’ attention to pedestrian crossings,” improvements “intended to shorten crossing distances, reduce vehicle speeds and significantly increase driver compliance at crosswalks.” Then the calendar: “The project is scheduled to let in fall 2027, with construction expected to begin shortly thereafter. The public can expect the improvements to be completed by summer 2028.” One intersection near Memorial Park, at Westcott and Blossom, has already been rebuilt with “brighter signs, a wide median, and new road paint” (KPRC). Paint and light sound like small things. The federal highway agency has measured them. On its list of proven countermeasures, “High-visibility crosswalks can reduce pedestrian injury crashes up to 40%,” “Intersection lighting can reduce pedestrian crashes up to 42%,” and “Advance yield or stop markings and signs can reduce pedestrian crashes up to 25%,” with a warning that reads like a description of a wide city arterial: “For multilane roadway crossings where vehicle volumes are in excess of 10,000 Average Annual Daily Traffic (AADT), a marked crosswalk alone is typically not sufficient” (FHWA, Crosswalk Visibility Enhancements). A leading pedestrian interval, a walk signal that “gives pedestrians the opportunity to enter the crosswalk at an intersection 3-7 seconds before vehicles are given a green indication,” is credited with a 13 percent “reduction in pedestrian-vehicle crashes at intersections” (FHWA, Leading Pedestrian Interval). Those are the numbers behind the 2028 date, and they are also a description of what a crossing can lack: visibility, light and time.

The seventy percent. A member of the state’s pedestrian safety task force told the station, “Approximately 70% is, it’s not the driver’s fault” (KPRC). Whatever that number measures, Texas law does not work in that currency. It asks, crash by crash, what each person did. The driver’s duty is written so that it survives every argument about where the person was standing: “Notwithstanding another provision of this chapter, the operator of a vehicle shall: (1) exercise due care to avoid colliding with a pedestrian on a roadway; (2) give warning by sounding the horn when necessary; and (3) exercise proper precaution on observing a child or an obviously confused or incapacitated person on a roadway” (Transportation Code Section 552.008). The person on foot has duties of his own. In a crosswalk with no signal, a driver “shall stop and yield the right-of-way to a pedestrian crossing a roadway,” but “a pedestrian may not suddenly leave a curb or other place of safety and proceed into a crosswalk in the path of a vehicle so close that it is impossible for the vehicle operator to stop and yield” (Section 552.003); outside a crosswalk, “A pedestrian shall yield the right-of-way to a vehicle on the highway,” and “Between adjacent intersections at which traffic control signals are in operation, a pedestrian may cross only in a marked crosswalk” (Section 552.005). When both have broken a rule, Chapter 33 divides the responsibility by percentage, and a share above fifty bars the claim; that is Comparative Fault, and “No Crosswalk” Is Not the End of the Sentence is about what goes on the driver’s side of the scale when the person was not between the lines: the speed, the lookout, the lighting, the headlights that Past the Light measures against the distance a car needs to stop. “Not the driver’s fault” is a percentage waiting to be argued, not a verdict.

The road itself. Can a family sue over the road? The Tort Claims Act, the one door The One Door describes, has a door for roads too, and most of the road is on the far side of it. The Act does not apply to a claim based on “a governmental unit’s decision not to perform an act or on its failure to make a decision on the performance or nonperformance of an act if the law leaves the performance or nonperformance of the act to the discretion of the governmental unit” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.056). The Supreme Court of Texas applied that to a road in a 1999 case from Corpus Christi, where a truck driver was killed when his tractor-trailer failed to complete a ninety-degree turn in a highway detour, went onto the shoulder and sideswiped a bridge abutment, and the rig rolled over; his widow and children sued the state and the city “alleging that the detour was unreasonably dangerous and had inadequate warning signs.” The court held that “Design of any public work, such as a roadway, is a discretionary function involving many policy decisions, and the governmental entity responsible may not be sued for such decisions,” that the detour’s design “involved the same type of policy-level decisions as that of designing a roadway and as such, is a discretionary act,” and that the state “retained its immunity for warning sign placement because it also was a discretionary act and because the detour was not a special defect,” and it rendered judgment that the family take nothing (State v. Rodriguez). Where to put a crosswalk, and when, is that kind of decision. The 2028 date is a calendar, not a confession. The door that stays open is narrower and specific. The Act keeps its immunity for “the absence, condition, or malfunction of a traffic or road sign, signal, or warning device” except where that absence, condition or malfunction “is not corrected by the responsible governmental unit within a reasonable time after notice” (Section 101.060), and keeps none for the duty to warn of “special defects such as excavations or obstructions on highways, roads, or streets” (Section 101.022), which the same court described as conditions that “unexpectedly and physically impair a car’s ability to travel on the road,” the subject of Barricade and Single Vehicle. A corridor built with too few crosswalks is a policy. A pedestrian signal that has been dark for a month after someone reported it is a claim.

What the investigation asks. Where the person crossed, and where the nearest marked crosswalk was, which is the first thing the defense will measure and the first thing the Redline says is not the end of the sentence. What the lighting was at that spot that night, whether the streetlights were working, and who had been told if they were not, because notice is the hinge of Section 101.060. What the signal was doing, and whether the walk phase led the green or ran with it. The driver’s speed, from the car’s own recorder, and the phone. The line of sight: parked cars, a curve, a turning vehicle that hid the crossing. The time of night and the clothing, which decide what the headlights could reach, and the moment the eye could have found a figure it was not looking for, which is Looking, Not Seeing. And the box the officer checked on the crash report, which is a conclusion drawn on the shoulder, not evidence of anything but what the officer believed that night.

No one is named here. The fix on Westheimer comes in summer 2028, by the state’s own calendar. Until then every driver on it owes due care to every person on foot, every person on foot owes the road the same care back, and the road answers for itself only where the law has left a door open. A dispatch keeps the record of which is which.
