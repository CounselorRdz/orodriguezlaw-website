Backing

Leer esta página en español →

A truck does most of its damage going forward, and the law is written for that. But a rig that backs across a road at night is doing the one thing the commercial driver’s manual tells a driver to avoid whenever he can, and Texas writes the rule for it in two sentences: not unless it can be done safely, and never on a controlled-access highway.

Backing. Driving a vehicle in reverse. The word matters because the statute puts the whole duty on the one who chooses to do it. “An operator may not back the vehicle unless the movement can be made safely and without interference with other traffic,” and “An operator may not back the vehicle on a shoulder or roadway of a limited-access or controlled-access highway” (Transportation Code Section 545.415). Two sentences, two rules. The first is conditional: anywhere else, the move is lawful only if it is safe and interferes with no one. The second is flat: on a freeway’s shoulder or roadway it is not lawful at all. Neither sentence asks what the other driver did.

What the manual says. The model commercial driver’s manual the states reprint opens its backing section with the reason: “Because you cannot see everything behind your vehicle, backing is always dangerous.” Then the rules. “Avoid backing whenever you can.” “Look at your line of travel before you begin.” “Check the outside mirrors on both sides frequently.” “Always back as slowly as possible.” “Backing toward the right side is very dangerous because you cannot see as well.” And “Use a helper when you can” (CDL Manual, subsection 2.2.4). A rig backing across a two-lane road at night is every one of those warnings at once: the driver cannot see what is coming, the trailer is broadside to it, and the maneuver is slow by instruction while the traffic it blocks is not.

What the other driver sees. The side of a trailer across a dark road is the hardest thing on the road to see in time, and the Docket has the pieces. Past the Light sets out how far low beams reach, about the next two hundred feet, and why a car at highway speed needs more than that to stop. The manual’s own arithmetic says how the two hundred feet get spent: an alert driver’s perception time is “1¾ seconds,” and “At 55 mph this accounts for 142 feet traveled”; reaction is “¾ second to 1 second,” and “At 55 mph this accounts for 61 feet traveled,” so a car at 55 has covered about two hundred feet before the brakes are touched, which is about where the low beams end (CDL Manual, subsection 2.6.1). “You should always be able to stop within the distance you can see ahead,” the manual says, and “When you must use low beams, slow down” (subsection 2.6.4). Looking, Not Seeing explains why a dark shape across the lane registers late. Conspicuity explains the red and white tape a big trailer must carry so that it registers at all, and why a trailer running dark or untaped is a trailer the law has already judged. Underride explains what happens when a car meets a trailer above its bumper: a floor at the height of the hood, a windshield and roof pillars taking a load they were never built for. The Insurance Institute says of the rear guards the law requires that they “work well when passenger vehicles strike the center of the trailer’s rear” but “don’t necessarily prevent underride in crashes involving only a small portion of the truck’s rear,” and that “There is no requirement for front or side underride guards in the United States” (IIHS, Large trucks). A trailer backing across a road presents its side.

The phrase to watch. In the first hours after a crash like this, an agency release will often say the car’s driver failed to observe the truck. That is a preliminary statement, attributed to whoever made it, and it is not a finding; the report that carries it usually says the crash is still under investigation. In the civil case the question is divided, not settled. Texas has the jury assign “the percentage of responsibility, stated in whole numbers,” to “each claimant,” “each defendant,” “each settling person” and each designated responsible third party, with respect to each person’s “causing or contributing to cause in any way the harm for which recovery of damages is sought” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003), and “a claimant may not recover damages if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Section 33.001). The car’s speed and attention go on one side of the ledger, the question Comparative Fault walks through. The decision to back a loaded rig across a road at night, the lamps and the tape it carried, whether anyone was flagging traffic, go on the other, and where the rule is flat, as the freeway sentence is, a safety statute broken is, unless excused, negligence in itself, the rule Negligence Per Se explains; where the rule asks only that the move be made safely, it states the ordinary-care question the jury already has. Behind the driver stands the company that sent him, on the terms Trucking Company Liability sets out.

The evidence. The truck’s engine computer and any camera it carried. The trailer’s lamps and tape, photographed before anything is cleaned or repaired. Whether a helper was spotting, and where he stood. The car’s own event data recorder, which says its speed and braking in the last seconds. And the dispatch records that say why a rig was crossing that road at that hour at all. Every one of them belongs to someone else, which is why the first letter asks for them (Spoliation; the black box).

Why the word matters. Backing moves the question from the car’s speedometer to the truck’s decision. A rig crossing a road in reverse is a choice, made by a driver and a company, and the statute puts the burden of making it safely on the one who made it. Nothing here is about any one crash; the pattern is the same on every dark road, and what happened on a particular one is for its record to say.
