More Than One Driver

Leer este parte en español →

A truck and several cars meet on a dark highway, and a family is told the crash was someone else’s fault, as if fault were one thing to hand to one driver. Texas does not divide it that way. After a crash with more than one vehicle, the law splits the responsibility into percentages, and the split is where the case is.

The split. When the case is tried, the jury, or the judge if there is no jury, assigns a percentage of responsibility to each person claiming damages, to each defendant, to each person who has settled, and to each responsible third party the court has allowed to be named (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003). A claimant recovers nothing only if the claimant’s own share is greater than 50 percent (Section 33.001). Each defendant is liable only for its own percentage of the damages, unless its share is greater than 50 percent, or in narrow cases it acted with others to commit certain intentional crimes, and then it can be held for all the damages the claimant can recover (Section 33.013). That is Comparative Fault at work. A family told that the other side blames their own driver has been handed the start of the argument, not the end of it.

Behind the truck. When one of the vehicles is a commercial truck, the list does not stop at the cab. A company generally answers for an employee driver whose negligence caused harm while he was doing its work (Respondeat Superior), and whether the companies that arranged the load can be reached as well is the question Who Hired Them takes up.

The one who left. A driver who helped cause the crash and drove away is part of what happened, but the law counts his share only in narrow ways. A defendant can ask the court to name a Responsible Third Party, any person “alleged to have caused or contributed to causing in any way the harm” (Section 33.011(6)). Naming him lets the jury assign him a share; it does not by itself make him liable (Section 33.004(i)). When that person is unknown, the statute provides a narrow path: within 60 days after filing its original answer, the defendant alleges that an unknown person committed a criminal act that was a cause of the injury, pleads facts showing a reasonable probability that the act was criminal, and states every identifying characteristic then known (Section 33.004(j)). And when the owner or driver of the vehicle that caused the harm is unknown, the uninsured motorist coverage that protects the injured person can answer only if there was actual physical contact between that vehicle and the insured or the insured’s property (Insurance Code Section 1952.104).

The order of the impacts. In a crash with several vehicles, the sequence is the case. Which vehicle struck first, which injury came from which blow, who could have stopped and who could not: the drivers who lived each saw a piece of it, from inside a moving car. The rest is read from the vehicles where they came to rest, the damage on each one, the marks on the road, and any electronic record the truck kept of its speed and braking. The Second Hit explains why saying that three vehicles were involved can hide the one fact that matters, and Spoliation explains why the first job is to keep that record from being lost.

So after a crash with more than one vehicle, the family’s question is not only whose fault it was. It is everyone whose fault it was, and how much. The answer is a list of percentages, and it is read from the road before it is read from anyone’s account.
