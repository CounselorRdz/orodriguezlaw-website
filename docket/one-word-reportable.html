Reportable

Leer esta página en español →

When a trucking company’s federal record shows its crashes, the number counts crashes, not fault. Read it the way the agency that keeps it says to read it, and it says something narrower, and more useful, than it seems to.

What counts. The federal rules define the event. An accident is an occurrence involving a commercial motor vehicle operating on a highway in interstate or intrastate commerce that results in a fatality; in bodily injury to a person who “immediately receives medical treatment away from the scene”; or in disabling damage that requires a vehicle to be towed away. Two kinds of occurrence are left out: one involving only getting on or off a stationary vehicle, and one involving only loading or unloading cargo (49 CFR 390.5T, the text in effect while 390.5 is suspended). The federal motor carrier agency’s safety measurement system puts the threshold in one sentence: crashes are reportable “if they result in a fatality or injury, or require a vehicle to be transported from the crash scene, regardless of the carrier or driver’s role in the crash” (FMCSA, SMS methodology).

What it does not say. Regardless of role. The count does not say who caused the crash. The agency’s own help page says the carrier’s role, “i.e. preventability,” “is not automatically determined or considered,” and it gives the reason it counts them anyway: “past crashes are a good predictor of future crashes” (FMCSA, CSA help center). The agency now reviews, on request, 21 kinds of crashes, among them a truck struck from behind, and crashes caused by another motorist driving the wrong way or falling asleep, or by another person under the influence. A crash it finds not preventable is removed from the carrier’s crash measure, but it is “still listed on the website” (FMCSA, Crash Preventability Determination Program). The crash measure itself is not public: the agency shows it only to a carrier logged into its own profile and to enforcement (SMS methodology). A count also says nothing, by itself, about how many trucks and miles stand behind it.

What the carrier must keep. The number on the website is a summary, built from crash reports the states send to the agency: the crash measure collects “State-reported crashes from the last two years” (SMS methodology). The carrier keeps its own record as well. Every motor carrier “must maintain an accident register for 3 years after the date of each accident,” listing each accident as the rules define it, with the date, the place, the driver, the number of injuries and deaths, and whether hazardous materials were released, along with copies of every accident report required by a state, another government or an insurer (49 CFR 390.15).

In a case. The count is a reason to ask questions, not an answer, and it cuts both ways. It does not prove that this driver was careless this time, and a carrier does not erase its history by pointing out that the count says nothing about fault. The useful questions start with the register. How many of the accidents involved the same driver, the same kind of trip, the same hours of the day? Which ones did the agency find not preventable, and which were never sent for review? What did the carrier change after each one? Those answers come from the register, the reports kept with it, and the carrier’s own files, which is why they are asked for early (Spoliation). Who hired the driver and who answers for him are the subjects of Negligent Entrustment and Who Hired Them.

Reportable is a threshold, not a verdict. A crash crosses it when someone dies, when someone is hurt and taken for treatment away from the scene, or when a vehicle has to be towed. Who caused it is a different question, and the register is where the answer starts.

49 CFR 390.5T and 49 CFR 390.15 (Cornell LII); FMCSA, Safety Measurement System methodology; FMCSA, CSA help center, frequently asked questions; FMCSA, Crash Preventability Determination Program (last updated December 10, 2024).
