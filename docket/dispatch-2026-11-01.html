The Passenger

Leer este parte en español →

The person in the passenger seat did nothing. They did not pick the road, or the speed, or the moment to pull into the intersection. They could not brake, could not steer, could not see what the driver saw. Of everyone in a crash, the passenger is the one with no hand in it, and Texas law treats them that way. The fault that gets divided among the people who caused the wreck is not divided onto the passenger, and the search for who pays runs through everyone else’s insurance before it ever reaches what is theirs.

The pattern. Just after midnight on Saturday, as News 4 San Antonio reported it from police, a white SUV heading north on Quintana Road on San Antonio’s Southwest Side at a high rate of speed lost control and hit a utility pole, and officers pronounced both the driver and another person inside the vehicle dead at the scene. Neither had been identified. This page names no one and decides nothing about that crash. It is here because of the second person in that SUV, the one who was only riding, and because a passenger, or a passenger’s family, almost never knows where a claim begins.

What the passenger is not charged with. Texas divides responsibility for an injury among the people who caused it, and the statute’s definition is exact: a percentage of responsibility is the share “attributed by the trier of fact to each claimant, each defendant, each settling person, or each responsible third party with respect to causing or contributing to cause in any way” the injury or death. A passenger who did nothing to cause the crash has nothing to be assigned, so the rule that cuts a recovery by the injured person’s own share, and bars it when that share “is greater than 50 percent,” has nothing to take hold of; Comparative Fault explains the arithmetic for everyone else in the car. The thing the other side raises most often is the seat belt. Since 2015, when the Supreme Court of Texas decided Nabors Well Services v. Romero, “relevant evidence of use or nonuse of seat belts is admissible for the purpose of apportioning responsibility in civil lawsuits,” but only “with evidence that nonuse caused or contributed to cause the plaintiff’s injuries,” because, as the court put it, “the question is not simply who caused the car accident, but who caused the plaintiff’s injuries.” The Belt lays that out. Riding unbelted is itself an offense for anyone fifteen or older in a seat that has a belt, which is why the belt is the one question a passenger will always be asked.

Where the money is. The at-fault driver’s liability coverage answers first; Texas requires at least thirty thousand dollars for the injury or death of one person in one collision and sixty thousand for two or more. A Texas auto policy also carries personal injury protection unless an insured named in the policy rejected it in writing, and that coverage pays “the named insured in the policy, members of the insured’s household, and any authorized operator or passenger of the named insured’s motor vehicle, including a guest occupant,” for necessary medical, hospital and funeral expenses and, for an income producer, “replacement of income lost as the result of the accident,” incurred within three years of the crash, and it pays “without regard to” “the fault or nonfault of the named insured or recipient.” The law requires only two thousand five hundred dollars of it per person; many policies carry more, and it is often the first money a passenger sees. And when the driver who caused the crash had too little insurance or none, uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, which every Texas auto policy must include unless it was rejected in writing, “protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from owners or operators of uninsured or underinsured motor vehicles.” A passenger with a policy of their own has that door; whether the policy on the car they rode in opens it too is a question of that policy’s terms, and the uninsured and underinsured motorist page explains how the claim is made. This is the companion to Single-Vehicle, which asked who answers when there was no other driver. Read from the passenger seat, the answer is plainer: the crash was never theirs to prevent, and the coverage is theirs to find.
