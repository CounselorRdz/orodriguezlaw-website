The Card

Leer este parte en español →

When a loaded truck crosses a median or drifts off the road and the first account calls the cause unclear, the list is almost always the same: it could have been inattention, sleep, or a medical problem. That is a list of possibilities, not a finding. In a commercial case the answer is rarely a mystery for long, because the law made the driver prove he was fit for that seat, on a schedule, in writing, before the crash.

The pattern. A commercial driver’s fitness is not taken on faith. Before anyone hands him the keys to eighty thousand pounds, a medical examiner has to examine him and certify that he is physically qualified to drive, and the certificate, the card drivers still call it, has a clock on it. This page names no one, is pegged to no crash, and decides nothing about any of them. It is here because when a cause is called unclear at the scene, the record that would settle it usually already exists.

The card. The federal rule is written as a prohibition: a driver “must not operate a commercial motor vehicle unless he or she is medically certified as physically qualified to do so” (49 CFR 391.41). The examination “must be performed by a medical examiner listed on the National Registry of Certified Medical Examiners” (49 CFR 391.43), a registry the government keeps. The agency that runs it says “A DOT physical exam is valid for up to 24 months,” and that “The medical examiner may also issue a medical examiner’s certificate for less than 24 months” “when it is desirable to monitor a condition, such as high blood pressure” (FMCSA, DOT Medical Exam and Commercial Motor Vehicle Certification). The regulation puts the same clock the other way around: a driver “who has not been medically examined and certified as qualified to operate a commercial motor vehicle” “during the preceding 24 months” has to be examined again before he drives, and so does “Any driver whose ability to perform his or her normal duties has been impaired by a physical or mental injury or disease” (49 CFR 391.45). The standard the examiner applies is a list, and two items on it are the ones the first account reaches for. A person is physically qualified only if that person “Has no established medical history or clinical diagnosis of epilepsy or any other condition which is likely to cause” loss of consciousness or loss of the ability to control the vehicle, and only if that person “Has no current clinical diagnosis of myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, coronary insufficiency, thrombosis” or another cardiovascular disease of the kind the rule goes on to describe (391.41). Age is not on the list. The qualification rule sets a floor, a driver “Is at least 21 years old” (49 CFR 391.11), and names no ceiling; what it asks for after that is the card.

Why the card is the case. The card does not stay in the driver’s wallet, and for the driver of an eighteen-wheeler it is no longer a card at all. Since June 23, 2025 the paper certificate is furnished only when the examiner “identifies that the person examined will not be operating” “a commercial motor vehicle that requires a commercial driver’s license or a commercial learner’s permit”; for every driver the examiner sees, “by midnight (local time) of the next calendar day” “the medical examiner must electronically transmit to FMCSA, via a secure FMCSA-designated website,” “a completed CMV Driver Medical Examination Results Form, MCSA-5850,” and the examiner’s own office keeps “Each original (paper or electronic) completed Medical Examination Report” “for at least 3 years from the date of examination” (49 CFR 391.43). The result is posted to the driver’s state license record, and the company has to keep a qualification file on each driver that holds it: “For CDL holders,” “if the CDLIS motor vehicle record contains” “medical certification status information,” “the motor carrier employer must meet this requirement by obtaining the CDLIS motor vehicle record,” and “That record must be obtained from the current licensing State and placed in the driver qualification file”; a commercial driver with no certification on that record “is designated ‘not-certified’ to operate a CMV in interstate commerce.” The file “shall be retained for as long as a driver is employed by that motor carrier” “and for three years thereafter,” though the certificate or record itself is among the papers that “may be removed from a driver’s qualification file three years after the date of execution” (49 CFR 391.51). So each possibility read out at the scene leaves a different mark, and the marks are already written down. A medical event turns on what the examiner saw, or missed, months before, and on whether the card was issued for two years or for a shorter watch. Falling asleep turns on the hours-of-service log (Logbook) and on the truck’s own computer, which can show a drift with no brake applied (The Other Clock). Inattention shows in the same data. The driver’s account of the moment can change. The card, the examiner’s notes and the log do not.

What the law asks. Behind the driver stands the company that put him in the cab, and a carrier that qualified or kept a driver it knew or should have known was unfit answers for its own negligence, not only his (Negligent Entrustment; Trucking Company Liability). A federal safety rule broken is evidence of what care required, and the jury still decides the breach, the point Negligence Per Se makes about safety rules. And if the defense is that the driver simply collapsed, Texas treats a truly unforeseeable loss of consciousness as a complete defense, as The Blackout and Sudden Incapacity set out, which is the very reason the card decides it: foreseeable is only another word for written down in advance, and the examination the law required is where it was or was not written. The fault is then divided in whole-number percentages among everyone who had a hand in the crash (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003). On a truck the brakes carry an inspection sticker and can be put out of service (Inspection; Out of Service). The driver carries a certificate, on paper or on his license record. Fitness is not assumed. It is certified, on a clock, and the paper made before the crash answers the question the crash asks, if someone asks for the paper before it is gone (Spoliation).

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any crash. A dispatch keeps the record of the fact a family is rarely told when the cause is called unclear: unclear is a statement about the first hour, and the file was written long before it.
