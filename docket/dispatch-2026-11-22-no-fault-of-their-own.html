No Fault of Their Own

Leer este parte en español →

A child does not choose the seat, the speed or whether the door is locked. When a crash, or a fall through a door that came open, puts a child in the hospital, the law starts from a rule no adult’s case gets to use. Below a certain age, a child cannot be at fault.

The duties. The main duties in a car belong to the adults: the driver whose conduct caused the harm, whoever was supposed to buckle the child in, and the company that built a door or a latch that did not hold. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

A child’s standard. Texas does not measure a child against a reasonable adult. It asks only what “an ordinary prudent child of the same age, experience, intelligence, and capacity” would have done. And “at some age a child is too young to be negligent as that term is used in law.” In 1971 the Supreme Court of Texas held that a child of four years and ten months was incapable of negligence as a matter of law. It followed the guideline, suggested in an earlier Texas case, that a child beneath the age of five is incapable of negligence as a matter of law, and it cited a treatise that put the line “somewhere in the neighborhood of four years of age” (Yarborough v. Berner). An older child can be found negligent, but measured against a child of the same age, experience, intelligence, and capacity, not against a grown-up.

Retention. One job of a seat belt and of a door lock is the same: keeping a body inside the space built to protect it (see Thrown). Texas requires a driver carrying a child younger than eight, unless the child is taller than four feet, nine inches, to keep the child secured in a child passenger safety seat system according to the instructions of its manufacturer (Transportation Code Section 545.412). The federal standard for doors is written “to minimize the likelihood of occupants being ejected from a vehicle as a result of impact”; it requires door latches to hold under specified load and inertia tests, and it requires each rear side door’s lock, when engaged, to keep the inside handle from opening the door (FMVSS No. 206, S1, S4.1.1 and S4.3.1). Whether a car had that lock engaged, or that seat in place, is a question about the adults and the car.

The belt in court. Since 2015, relevant evidence of whether seat belts were used can come into a Texas case to apportion responsibility, when not using one caused or helped cause the injuries (Nabors Well Services v. Romero; see The Belt and Comparative Fault). For a child too young to be negligent, there is no share of fault to put on the child. The question that evidence can raise instead is what the adults did.

The body. A fall from a moving car is mostly not a fall. The drop from a seat is short, but a body that leaves a moving car keeps the car’s forward speed, so it does not simply land; it tumbles and slides, and every turn is another blow. A straight blow to the head can break the skull and bleed over the brain. The twisting of the head as the body tumbles can shear nerve fibers deep inside the brain (see The Third Collision).

Who answers. It starts with the driver whose negligence caused the crash and, if a part of the car failed, the company that made it (see Crashworthiness). If the driver who caused it carried no insurance or too little, the family’s own policy may still answer: uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from the owner or operator of an uninsured or underinsured motor vehicle (Insurance Code Section 1952.101; see Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims). Who counts as an insured is written in the policy.

A child in the back seat is the one person in the car who did nothing to be there. For a young child, the question is never what the child should have done. For every child, it is what the adults around the child were supposed to do, and whether they did it.
