Ted Wells

Leer esta página en español →

Ted Wells says a trial lawyer’s job is to tell a story. “Trial lawyers are storytellers,” he told Super Lawyers in 2006. “A good trial lawyer has the ability to construct a story line.” He is here for that sentence, and for the work he says it takes.

He was a star lineman in high school and went to the College of the Holy Cross on a football scholarship, graduating in 1972. At Harvard he earned a law degree and an M.B.A., both in 1976, and he was an editor of the Harvard Civil Rights-Civil Liberties Law Review. He clerked for Judge John J. Gibbons of the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit. After a brief stop at a firm in California, he spent 22 years at Lowenstein Sandler in New Jersey, where he worked under Matthew Boylan. “I learned everything about how to try cases from him,” he says. Paul, Weiss then brought him to New York to co-chair its litigation department, and the firm’s profile still lists him as a partner and co-chair. He is a Fellow of the American College of Trial Lawyers and a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.

The story first. “Trials are all about competing storylines,” he said, “and it’s important to get a jury to accept your version.” He frames every case as a story, the magazine says. When he joined a Practising Law Institute panel on opening statements in 2022, its description promised to cover how to “develop a theme and tell a story,” how to “win a jury over” and how to “handle damaging evidence.”

Then the work. “There’s no quick and easy way to do trial work,” he said. “There’s always more reading to do, more writing to do, more thinking.” The story does not arrive on schedule: “You can sit there waiting for months for the theory of your defense to crystallize.” And he wants the other side to know he is ready. “I hate to settle cases,” he said. “I want to fight. Everybody knows I will try the case.”

Then the jury. Some say he makes jurors feel smart; his answer, the magazine reports, is “That’s because juries are smart.” “Be yourself when it comes time to face the jurors,” he says, “and make sure to look them in the eye.” Coaching a high school mock trial team, he put it this way: “The most important thing I can tell you today,” he said, “is don’t use notes. Even in the closing.” Get the story on paper, he told them, outline it, cut it to bullet points, and keep practicing the delivery. His firm’s profile says he has taught in Harvard Law School’s Trial Advocacy Workshop and has served on the faculty of the Practising Law Institute’s Trial Advocacy Program.

Why he is here. Because an injury case is a contest of story lines too. The defense will tell the jury the crash was a moment no one could have prevented; the injured person’s lawyer has to build the version that explains every fact, the hours in the logbook, the inspection nobody did, the second and a half when the brake could have gone down (Show the Second and a Half). Because a lawyer who has done the reading, and waited for the theory to come clear, can put the notes down and look the jury in the eye. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Brendan Sullivan and David Boies. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Build the story line before the exhibits. Respect the jury, because juries are smart. And know the case well enough to leave the notes at the table.

Every trial has two stories. Wells’s lesson is to tell the one that explains everything, and to tell it looking at the jury.
