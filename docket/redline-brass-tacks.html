Brass Tacks

Before and after. Today, the opening that tells the reader everything except what the case is about.

Openings tend to start with procedure. The names are invented. The habit is not:

“Plaintiff Luis Ortega, by and through his attorneys of record, files this Motion to Compel and would respectfully show the Court as follows. This is a personal injury action arising out of a motor vehicle collision. Plaintiff served his Second Request for Production on Defendant. Defendant served objections. The parties conferred but were unable to resolve the dispute. Plaintiff therefore moves the Court for relief.”

Pass One: Interview Yourself.

Six sentences, and the judge still does not know who did what, what is being withheld, or why it matters. Ross Guberman, whose book Point Made studies how the country’s best advocates write, teaches a fix he calls the Brass Tacks approach, the first of his four lenses for an introduction: begin with “a paragraph or two that covers what many attorneys never explain at all.” His tip: “Interview yourself.” Who are the parties; when, where and how did the dispute arise; what question must the court answer; and why should you win (Guberman, BriefCatch). Answer those before anything else.

Pass Two: Put the Answers First.

“On March 3, a tractor-trailer ran a red light at a busy intersection on the east side of town and crushed the driver’s door of Luis Ortega’s pickup. The carrier’s own dispatcher wrote that the driver had been on duty since before dawn. The question is whether the carrier must produce the electronic logging data for the week before the crash. It must, because the data is the only complete record of the driver’s hours that week, and federal rules required the carrier to keep it.”

Same case. The judge now knows who, what, where, when, the question and the answer, in four sentences. The procedural history can come next, in one paragraph, because the reader now knows why it matters.

Pass Three: The Sixty-Second List.

Guberman’s second lens, the Logical Lens, is a short list: “List three or four specific points you would make to a judge who gave you just 60 seconds,” and “Make your reasons factual, not circular” (BriefCatch). Not “because the discovery is relevant,” which only restates the rule, but “because the logs show whether the driver had slept.” A reason that names a fact persuades. A reason that names a standard only repeats it.

Two More Lenses.

He offers two more, the Pragmatic Lens and the Contrasting Lens. Give the court “a reason to feel good about ruling in your favor”: what the ruling protects, and for whom. And draw “a line in the sand between the two competing views” of the dispute, so the judge sees the choice in one sentence (BriefCatch). A review of the book describes the same four moves as a checklist for writing an introduction (CABA review, 2016).

Why It Matters.

A judge decides how to read a motion in its first paragraph. So does an adjuster reading a demand letter, and so does a juror listening to the first minute of an opening statement. The Buried Lede moved the best fact to the front of a fact section. The Brass Tacks approach does the same for the whole document: the case first, the procedure after. The Mechanism Paragraph then shows the force behind the injury. Each one asks the same thing of the writer: tell the reader what the case is about before you tell them anything else.

If the reader cannot say what the case is about after your first paragraph, the first paragraph is not done.

Ross Guberman, Preliminary Statements and Introductions: Checklists and Models (BriefCatch, May 26, 2023); Ross Guberman, Point Made: How to Write Like the Nation’s Top Advocates (2d ed., Oxford University Press, 2014); Vicki Lowery, review of Point Made (December 17, 2016). The names and facts in the examples are invented. Writing guidance, not legal advice.
