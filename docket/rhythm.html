Rhythm

After a violent crash, the injury is not only to the body. Post-traumatic stress is a real, documented harm, and one common treatment for it looks strange from the outside: recall the worst moment while moving your eyes side to side. It is called EMDR, and the honest story about it is more interesting than either its champions or its critics admit.

What it is

EMDR, eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, has a patient hold a traumatic memory in mind while following a rhythmic back and forth cue, usually the therapist’s hand. Major bodies, the World Health Organization, the Department of Veterans Affairs, and the American Psychological Association, recognize it as an effective treatment for post-traumatic stress.

What the evidence shows

In head to head studies, EMDR works about as well as the best trauma-focused talk therapies. People get better. That part is not seriously in doubt.

The honest argument

What is in doubt is the rhythm itself. When researchers strip the eye movements out and keep everything else, the results barely change. Many scientists conclude the moving finger adds little or nothing, and that the real work is the exposure, facing the memory in a safe place. A competing theory holds that the eye movements occupy working memory and make the memory feel less vivid and less overwhelming. The field genuinely disagrees.

Why this matters

A person who was traumatized in a crash and helped by this therapy is not exaggerating, and a defense that mocks the eye movements is aiming at the wrong target. The outcome is well supported even where the mechanism is debated. Being honest about that distinction is not a weakness in a claim. It is what makes the claim credible.

On EMDR’s endorsements and effectiveness for post-traumatic stress, see the World Health Organization, VA/DoD, and American Psychological Association guidelines; on the debate over whether the bilateral eye movements add anything beyond exposure, see the dismantling studies and meta-analyses in the general EMDR literature.
