Stranded

Leer este parte en español →

A car that dies on a freeway turns its driver, for a few minutes, into the one thing a freeway is not built for: a person on foot. Late Saturday night in Houston, a man whose truck had run out of gas crossed the East Freeway for fuel and was struck and killed walking back to it. A few hours earlier, a man walking in the lanes of the Gulf Freeway near Baybrook Mall was hit and killed. And while police had the East Freeway closed to work that crash, a driver struck a patrol car, and the officer inside went to the hospital.

The count. Texas lost 695 people on foot in 2025, about 10 percent fewer than the 772 of the year before, in a year when 3,769 people died on its roads. In the state’s figures for 2024, people on foot were involved in about one percent of Texas crashes and accounted for about 19 percent of the deaths. They are rarely the crash, and far too often the death.

What the pattern says. A freeway is engineered on the assumption that no one will ever be standing in it. At 65 miles an hour a car covers about 95 feet every second, so the last two seconds before an impact, where The Reaction says everything that matters happens, are nearly 200 feet of road. A person in a dark lane is also the one thing a driver on a freeway is not looking for, and the eye can land on what the brain is not searching for without registering it, which is what Looking, Not Seeing is about. When the car arrives, the body meets it the way The Wrap and the Ground describes, bumper, hood, then pavement, and on a freeway the next car may be only seconds behind; police said the man on the Gulf Freeway appeared to have been struck more than once. A patrol car parked across the lanes with its lights on is in a version of the same danger: a stopped car in a live lane is only as safe as the attention of the driver coming up behind it.

What the law does with it. Texas does not excuse a driver because the person on foot should not have been there. Every driver must “exercise due care to avoid colliding with a pedestrian on a roadway,” warn with the horn when necessary, and take “proper precaution” on seeing a child or an obviously confused or incapacitated person in the road (Transportation Code Section 552.008). The person on foot has rules too. Where there is no sidewalk, a pedestrian walking along a highway must, if possible, keep to the left side of the road or to the shoulder facing oncoming traffic (Section 552.006), and someone crossing freeway lanes at night will be asked to carry a share of the fault. Texas divides fault by percentage, and a share above fifty percent bars recovery, which is Comparative Fault. That is a real fight, not a foregone conclusion, and “No Crosswalk” Is Not the End of the Sentence explains why: speed, lookout and lighting all go on the scale. The officer was protected by a rule of his own. A driver approaching a stopped police car, ambulance, tow truck or highway department vehicle with its lights on must move out of the lane next to it or slow to 20 miles per hour below the limit, and breaking that rule is an offense, a more serious one when someone is hurt (Section 545.157). And a driver who hits a person and drives away has broken a separate law, the one Gone is about.

If your car dies on a freeway. The Department of Public Safety’s advice is to park as far off the traveled lanes as you can, turn on the flashers, get out on the passenger side, away from traffic, and, “if possible, stay with the vehicle until uniformed law enforcement arrives, especially at night or during bad weather”; its helpline for stranded motorists is 1-800-525-5555 (DPS). In Harris County, the sheriff’s Motorist Assistance Program brings fuel, changes flat tires, jump-starts batteries and moves stranded cars off area freeways for free, around the clock on weekdays except county holidays; the number is 713-CALL-MAP, or 713-225-5627 (HCSO). And Houston’s Tow and Go program tows a stalled car off a city freeway to a safe spot nearby at no cost, as long as the car has not been left unattended (City of Houston). None of that helps after the fact. All of it is worth knowing before the tank runs dry.
