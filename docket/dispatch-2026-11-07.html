Contact

Leer este parte en español →

A piece of a truck works loose on the freeway, crosses a windshield, and comes to rest in the back seat. The truck does not stop. By the time anyone looks, it is gone. What happens next turns on two questions, and the second one can come down to a single word: contact.

The pattern. A strap, a bracket, a wheel, a length of steel comes free from a trailer and becomes a projectile at highway speed. It is not a crash in the usual sense. Nothing struck the truck, and the truck struck nothing. A part of it simply left and arrived somewhere else. This page names no one, is pegged to no one incident, and decides nothing about any of them. It is here because the law answers this kind of harm twice, and the two answers do not open the same door.

The first door. The first question is what came off, and whose it was. If it was the load or what held the load, a strap, a length of steel, a piece of the freight, the federal securement rules carry the case, and they do not leave securing a load to a driver’s judgment. The system that holds the cargo has to be built and kept so that “the maximum forces acting on the devices or systems do not exceed the manufacturer’s breaking strength rating” under a load thrown forward with “0.8 g deceleration in the forward direction” or sideways with “0.5 g acceleration in a lateral direction,” each applied on its own, that is, a force of eight tenths of the load’s weight forward and half its weight to the side (49 CFR 393.102). The driver must “Inspect the cargo and the devices used to secure the cargo within the first 50 miles after beginning a trip” and “Reexamine the commercial motor vehicle’s cargo and its load securement devices during the course of transportation,” at a change of duty status, after three hours or after 150 miles, whichever comes first; and the rule is written for the company too, since “a motor carrier may not require or permit a driver to operate” unless the cargo “is properly distributed and adequately secured” (49 CFR 392.9). A load that came off is a securement that failed, and the duty to secure runs past the driver to the carrier that dispatched the load and to whoever loaded it, the point Shed made about the load that becomes a fixed object in the next lane and Securement sets out rule by rule. If what came off was the truck itself, a wheel, an axle, a bracket, the arm of a mud flap, the rule is the carrier’s duty to maintain: “Every motor carrier and intermodal equipment provider must systematically inspect, repair, and maintain,” “or cause to be systematically inspected, repaired, and maintained, all motor vehicles and intermodal equipment” “subject to its control,” and “Parts and accessories shall be in safe and proper operating condition at all times,” a list the rule says includes “frame and frame assemblies, suspension systems, axles and attaching parts,” “wheels and rims, and steering systems” (49 CFR 396.3; Inspection). A broken rule is evidence of what care required, and the jury still decides the breach (Negligence Per Se). All of that reaches a truck you can name.

The second door. When the truck is gone and the owner is never found, Texas sends the injured person to their own policy. Every auto liability policy sold in the state must carry uninsured motorist coverage unless the named insured “rejects the coverage in writing” (Insurance Code Section 1952.101), and for the unknown driver the statute asks for one thing: the policy must “require that, for the insured to recover under the uninsured motorist coverage” when the owner or operator of the vehicle “is unknown, actual physical contact must have occurred” “between the motor vehicle owned or operated by the unknown person” and the person or property of the insured (Section 1952.104). A piece that came off the truck and struck the car looks like contact, and in plain sense it is. The Texas Supreme Court has read the statute the other way. In a 2008 case an axle and its wheels came off an eighteen-wheeler; “Momentum carried the axle-wheel assembly across the dividing median where it struck” the insured’s car, and “The unidentified truck, which was being driven in the opposite direction on a divided highway, did not stop.” The court of appeals had sided with the family under an integral-part test; the Supreme Court reversed and rendered judgment for the insurer, concluding “that physical contact with a detached axle and tandem wheels” “is not actual physical contact with a motor vehicle under the unidentified motor vehicle provision.” “We decline, however, to adopt an integral part test not present in the text of the statute,” “and inconsistent with the relatively bright line established by the Legislature,” the court wrote, and “The Legislature drew a relatively bright line, and we decline to fuzz it up”; requiring contact with the vehicle itself, it said, “enforces the legislative purposes of protecting insured motorists and preventing fraud” (Nationwide Insurance Co. v. Elchehimi). The court did not disturb an older court of appeals decision for a chain collision, where the unknown vehicle strikes a second car that is driven into the insured, but it distinguished that case rather than adopting it, noting that no Texas court had relied on it to find contact “where there was no” intermediary “Car B” and that the detached axle, not being a motor vehicle, could not fill that role. So the line runs like this. A truck that hits you and leaves, contact. A truck that knocks another car into you and leaves, contact under the court of appeals’ rule, which the Supreme Court left alone without adopting. A piece that falls off a truck and hits you while the truck drives on, not contact, and the policy the family paid for does not answer, unless the truck is found.

What the law asks. Which is the whole reason the first hour matters more than it seems. The record that identifies the truck is the case, because it is the only thing that moves the claim from the second door to the first. The part itself, kept and not thrown away, with its markings. A dash camera, the other drivers’, a doorbell, a gas station, canvassed before the files roll over. A plate, a company name on a door, a description good enough to find a carrier, a timestamp good enough to pull a toll record. Secure that, and the claim rests on a duty the law spells out, against a company with a policy, rather than on a word the Texas Supreme Court has already read narrowly (Spoliation; Gone; Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims). A piece of steel that passes through a windshield and stops against a child’s seat is not a near miss. It is a rule that failed, of securement or of maintenance, and happened to spare everyone, and the time to prove whose it was is now, while the evidence is still on the road.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any one incident. A dispatch keeps the record of a rule most families learn only after the truck is gone: the duty to tie down a load reaches only a defendant you can name, and the policy in your own glove box asks for contact with the vehicle, not with what fell off it.
