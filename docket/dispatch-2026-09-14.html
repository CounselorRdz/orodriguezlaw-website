When a car and a train meet at a crossing, the story tends to write itself the same way every time: the driver should have stopped. It is true as far as it goes, because the driver is the only one who could have stopped. A loaded freight cannot brake in under a mile and cannot steer at all. But it is only half the story. This week a review of federal railroad records put Texas numbers to the other half, and the state has put money behind it.

The review. A CBS News Texas investigation matched the federal government’s list of railroad crossings used by school buses against decades of Federal Railroad Administration crash data. Even along the routes the state works hardest to protect, it counted 704 crossings with no gates on record. Nearly three hundred of those already have a crash history. Across the ones the review could tie to reported wrecks, the total came to 550 crashes, 249 people hurt, and 36 killed. Those are the routes with the most eyes on them; the crossings no bus travels are on no one’s list.

The backdrop. The national picture is why those numbers matter. Grade crossings are the second leading cause of rail-related death in the country, with more than two thousand collisions and roughly two hundred deaths in a typical year, by the Federal Railroad Administration’s count. A loaded freight cannot stop and cannot turn, so the burden of avoiding the collision sits, every time, on the person in the vehicle. That much was settled by physics before anyone woke up that morning.

What the pattern says. What is not settled is the crossing. A large thing far off and a small thing up close look alike to the eye, and a train is very large, so it reads as farther and slower than it is; a driver glances, judges there is time, and rolls onto the tracks on a misread, which is what Looking, Not Seeing is about. The warning devices exist to take that judgment out of the driver’s hands. A passive crossing offers only a crossbuck, the bare wooden X, and leaves the driver to decide; an active crossing adds the lights, the bells, and the gates that drop when a train is detected. The Grade Crossing walks through why that difference can be the difference between a survivable morning and a fatal one.

What the law does with it. So driver error is where the inquiry starts, not where it ends. Texas law tells a driver to stop for an activated signal, a lowered gate, or a train close enough to be a hazard (Transportation Code Section 545.251), and a driver who ignores it has handed the case a breach, which is what Negligence Per Se is about, while Failure to Yield explains why the right of way was never his to keep. But the driver had one path. The people who decided how much warning that crossing would give had many, and their choices are evidence: whether a busy, fast crossing was left to a bare crossbuck, whether the gates worked and were timed to warn, whether brush swallowed the sight line, and whether the crossing was already flagged as dangerous with money on hand to fix it. That last question is no longer hypothetical. The Legislature put 250 million dollars into a new state fund to separate roads from tracks (Senate Bill 1555), and the Texas Department of Transportation drew down 756 million dollars in federal money for twelve grade-separation projects this summer. A crossing left bare while the money sits there is a choice someone made. The car accidents and serious personal injury pages, with their Before You Call companion, cover how a crossing case is built.
