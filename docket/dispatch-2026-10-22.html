The Long Grade

Leer este parte en español →

Many truck wrecks happen on the flat, where a driver had room to stop and did not use it. A few happen on a long hill, where the room to stop ran out before the hill did. The second kind is the one a road has to be built to forgive, because by the time the brakes quit there is nothing left to stop the truck but the hill itself.

Monday morning, about 10:38, a semi came down Loop 375, Transmountain Road, southbound toward North Desert Boulevard at the road’s overpass at Interstate 10 on El Paso’s west side. Citing a police news release, the station reported that eight vehicles were involved, “including a semi-trailer with failed brakes,” and that “The driver said the semi’s brakes failed, according to police.” One man died at the scene. One person was flown to a hospital, and four others went to hospitals, the fire department said, which assessed ten people as patients. Police counted it as the city’s 55th traffic death of 2026, against 51 at the same point last year (KVIA). At a press conference the mayor opened with a question, “how many more?”, and said commercial vehicle inspections would start on both sides of the road; a council member put an item on the October 27 agenda to have the city evaluate restrictions on heavy truck traffic, on a stretch of highway the state owns (KVIA). By Wednesday the inspections had begun (KVIA). No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on the hill. The pattern is older than the crash, and the law answered it years ago.

The heat. A brake does one thing. It turns motion into heat and gives the heat to the air. On level ground the drums cool between stops. On a long downgrade the truck never stops needing them, and the heat arrives faster than the steel can shed it. The commercial driver’s manual, in the copy Georgia’s licensing agency posts, says it without ornament: “You must go slowly enough so your brakes can hold you back without getting too hot,” because “brakes can fade or fail from excessive heat caused by using them too much and not relying on the engine braking effect” (CDL manual, Section 2.16). Fade is the word for the moment the pedal that held the truck at the top holds less and less of it, and the driver’s instinct, pressing harder, makes more heat. The Federal Highway Administration built a signing system on that arithmetic: brake temperature at the bottom of a hill “is mainly dependent on grade length and steepness, truck weight, and truck speed,” and the engineers behind it, “After observing the onset of brake fade for a number of brake linings with typical amounts of imbalance,” set 500 degrees Fahrenheit as “the maximum allowed brake temperature” (FHWA, 1989). The physics, with the numbers, is the subject of Brake Fade. What matters on the road is one consequence of it: once the brakes are hot, there is no gear low enough to get them back.

The gear. The law answers the heat before the descent, not during it. The manual’s instruction is to choose a safe speed at the top, from the weight of the truck and its cargo, the length and steepness of the grade, the road and the weather, and then: “Shift the transmission to a low gear before starting down the grade,” because “You must use the braking effect of the engine as the principal way of controlling your speed,” and “Do not try to downshift after your speed has already built up” (CDL manual, Section 2.16). Texas writes the same duty for every driver in one sentence: “An operator may not drive at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing,” and it adds that a driver shall “drive at an appropriate reduced speed if” he “is approaching a hill crest” or “a special hazard exists with regard to traffic, including pedestrians, or weather or highway conditions” (Transportation Code Section 545.351). A mountain grade is a circumstance. The duty is the one Reasonable and Prudent explains, and on a hill it is met or failed at the summit.

The company. The driver’s duty runs beside the carrier’s, and the carrier’s is written in the federal rules Texas adopts as its own (37 Tex. Admin. Code Section 4.11). Every motor carrier “must systematically inspect, repair, and maintain” the vehicles under its control, and “Parts and accessories shall be in safe and proper operating condition at all times” (49 CFR 396.3). With limited exceptions, “all brakes with which a motor vehicle is equipped must at all times be capable of operating” (49 CFR 393.48). “A motor vehicle shall not be operated in such a condition as to likely cause an accident or a breakdown of the vehicle” (49 CFR 396.7). And before driving, the driver must “Be satisfied that the motor vehicle is in safe operating condition” (49 CFR 396.13). The manual ties the two duties together in a sentence that belongs in every one of these cases: “Brake fade is also affected by adjustment.” Brakes out of adjustment quit first, the ones still working take the heat for all of them, and so, the manual says, “brake adjustment must be checked frequently” (CDL manual, Section 2.16). A hill does not create a bad brake. It finds one. That is the question The Jackknife asked and Inspection asks, and it is why, after a wreck on a grade, the police did not wait. The inspections that began Wednesday check “brakes, lights, seatbelts and couplers” and the logbook, with the officer underneath the truck, by a department the station reported has six certified inspectors and can write a Class C citation of up to five hundred dollars or, if the problem cannot be fixed at the roadside, have the truck hauled off (KVIA). The authority for that is Texas law: a certified city police officer “may stop, enter, or detain on a highway or at a port of entry within the territory of the municipality a motor vehicle that is subject to this chapter,” and “may prohibit the further operation of the vehicle on a highway if the vehicle or operator of the vehicle is in violation of a federal safety regulation or a rule adopted under this chapter” (Transportation Code Section 644.103). The three words that follow are the subject of Out of Service.

The catch. Where a grade has taken enough trucks, the state stops trusting the brakes at all. The Federal Highway Administration’s review of escape ramps describes them as “used by vehicles which have lost their braking capabilities and are out of control,” and counts six designs, from the sandpile to the arrester bed, where “the rolling resistance offered by the material is the predominant means of decelerating the vehicle” (FHWA, 1982). Transmountain Road has four. Three are gravel, “one westbound and two eastbound”; the fourth, opened this summer, is “the first of its kind installed in El Paso and in Texas,” a mechanical system “designed to safely stop a 90,000-pound truck traveling 90 miles an hour using a series of eight energy absorbing net stations bolted into reinforced concrete walls,” placed for “vehicles that lose their brakes closer to I-10 via the left lane of Loop 375, westbound,” with a reset time “as short as four hours,” beside electronic signs “developed to advise trucks to use lower gear due to the steep grades” (TxDOT, July 1, 2026). After Monday’s crash TxDOT said the mechanical ramp “is fully operational and available for use” (KVIA). The reports do not say whether the truck reached a ramp, passed one, or could have. A ramp is an admission, built in concrete and gravel, that on this road the brakes may already be gone by the time a driver needs them, and a sign that tells a truck to gear down is the state’s own statement of what a prudent driver does at the top.

What the investigation asks. What gear the truck was in and how fast it was going when the grade began, which the engine’s own recorder kept (the black box) and which the manual says is decided before the descent, not during it. What the brakes were: their adjustment, their linings and drums, read by an expert for the heat they took, and whether every one of them was working, because the rule requires all of them. What the maintenance file shows, what the last roadside inspection found, and whether the truck had ever been placed out of service and for what. How much the load weighed, since the 1989 report, summarizing one study’s estimate of 2,450 runaways a year, found that “Some 73 percent of the trucks involved in those runaway events carried loads heavier than 60,000 lb” (FHWA, 1989). Whether the driver knew the road, which the manual tells him to learn before he takes it, and what his hours were, which is the Logbook. And whether the brakes failed all at once or faded, because “it came out of nowhere” is the defense Sudden Emergency explains, and the instruction does not reach an emergency the driver’s own negligence created. Each of those answers lives in a record that can be gone within weeks, which is why the first letter in a case like this one asks that nothing be repaired, sold or overwritten (Spoliation).

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on Transmountain Road. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a truck comes down a long hill and cannot stop: what the driver chose at the top, what the company gave him to choose with, what the state had already built to catch him, and what the records say about all three.
