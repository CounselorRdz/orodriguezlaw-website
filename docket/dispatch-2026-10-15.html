The Roof and the Tank

Leer este parte en español →

A car that rolls once and comes to rest can leave the people inside alive. A car that rolls and then crushes down on them, or burns before they can get out, is a different event. When there was no other driver, the question is not only why the car rolled. It is whether the car was built to come through a roll.

On Wednesday night, at about 9:59, a single vehicle on the southbound service road of Ed Bluestein Boulevard in East Austin left the road, struck the concrete barrier between the service road and the main lanes, rolled and caught fire. Two adults were pronounced dead at the scene, and a juvenile in the back seat was taken to Dell Seton Medical Center, where, as of the station’s Thursday afternoon update, the juvenile remained in intensive care. Police said their preliminary investigation found the vehicle traveling at a high rate of speed when it left the service road “for unknown reasons,” and that the investigation remains ongoing (CBS Austin). No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides that crash. It is here for a pattern the Docket has treated once as physics and once as law but never as one question: the single-vehicle rollover with a fire, and who answers for it when no other driver was involved.

No other car. When a single vehicle leaves the road and rolls, the instinct is that there is no one to blame but the road or the driver, and that instinct ends a lot of cases before they start. It should not. A crash and the injury it produces are two different things. The Texas Supreme Court said so in 1979, in a case about a sedan that “rolled over once and the roof caved in at the driver’s corner when it contacted the ground”; the belted driver “was struck on his head and suffered a crushed vertebra resulting in paralysis,” and the Court adopted, in a federal court’s words, the rule that a person hurt by a defect in a vehicle “should be protected under the doctrine of strict liability even though the defect was not the cause of the collision which precipitated the injury” (Turner v. General Motors). That is the whole of Crashworthiness in one sentence. A roll a body could survive becomes a death when the roof folds into the space where the head was, or when fuel finds a spark. Those are not acts of the road. They are questions about the vehicle, and the maker answers them beside the driver, not instead of him.

What the roof is for. The federal roof-crush standard exists “to reduce deaths and injuries due to the crushing of the roof into the occupant compartment in rollover crashes.” In the test a plate is pressed down on one side of the roof and then the other, and a vehicle rated at 6,000 pounds or less must take a force of three times its own unloaded weight without the plate moving more than 127 millimeters, about five inches, and without pressing more than 50 pounds of load onto a head form set where a seated adult’s head would be; for heavier vehicles up to 10,000 pounds the force is one and a half times (49 CFR 571.216a). The upgrade was issued in 2009 and doubled the old requirement, “from 1.5 times the vehicle’s unloaded weight to 3.0 times the vehicle’s unloaded weight”; its phase-in began on September 1, 2012, the lighter vehicles had to meet it by September 1, 2015 and the heavier ones by September 1, 2016, with certain exceptions (NHTSA, 2009 final rule). So the year a vehicle was built decides which roof rule it answers to: a lighter vehicle built before the phase-in was held to half the strength, and a vehicle over 6,000 pounds built before September 2016 was held to no roof standard at all. One honest caution comes from the agency’s own count: of the more than 10,000 people killed in rollovers each year, it found that “roof strength is relevant to only about seven percent (about 667) of those fatalities,” and estimated the upgrade would prevent 135 deaths a year. The roof is one question in a roll, not the only one; The Roll and Thrown Clear carry the belt, the window and the door.

What the tank is for. The fuel-system standard states its purpose in its first lines: “to reduce deaths and injuries occurring from fires that result from fuel spillage during and after motor vehicle crashes.” It covers passenger cars and the trucks, vans and SUVs rated at 10,000 pounds or less that burn a liquid fuel, and after the crash tests it turns the vehicle over. The car “is rotated about its longitudinal axis, with the axis kept horizontal, to each successive increment of 90°, 180°, and 270°,” each quarter turn taking one to three minutes, and “after reaching each 90° increment the vehicle is held in that position for 5 minutes.” Through all of it the fuel is supposed to stay in the tank: no more than 142 grams, about five ounces, in the first five minutes at each position, and no more than 28 grams, about an ounce, in any minute after that (49 CFR 571.301). A car that burns after a survivable roll raises the same question the crushed roof does, and Fire puts it the same way: the crash was one event and the fire was another. Where the fuel came from, the tank, the filler neck or a line, and whether the system was built and kept to hold it, is the question. What the fire does to a person still inside is what Smoke explains.

What the standard does in court. Meeting a federal standard does not end the case, but in Texas it changes who starts ahead. A maker that proves its design complied with mandatory federal standards “that were applicable to the product at the time of manufacture” “and that governed the product risk that allegedly caused harm” gets a rebuttable presumption that it is not liable, and the family rebuts it by proving the standard was “inadequate to protect the public from unreasonable risks of injury or damage,” or that the maker “withheld or misrepresented information or material” relevant to the government’s judgment of the standard’s adequacy (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 82.008). A roof that met the 1.5-times rule in 2010 met the rule of its year, and the argument has to be made about the rule. Then comes the burden every design case carries: “a safer alternative design,” one that “would have prevented or significantly reduced the risk” of the injury “without substantially impairing the product’s utility” and “was economically and technologically feasible” when the vehicle left the maker’s control, and proof that the defect was “a producing cause” of the death (Section 82.005). In a rollover the alternative design is often sitting in the same showroom: the stronger roof the maker already builds into another model, the tank placed where another model places it.

Whose case it is. A driver’s own speed is in the case too. Texas asks the jury to divide responsibility among everyone who contributed, and a claimant found more than half responsible recovers nothing, while one found less has the recovery reduced by that share (Sections 33.001 and 33.012), which is the arithmetic Comparative Fault walks through. That arithmetic belongs to the driver’s own claim. A passenger did not drive, and a juvenile in the back seat did not, so a passenger’s claim against the maker carries no part of the driver’s share; the maker answers for the percentage the jury puts on the vehicle. The same is true of the road owner and the other defendants Single-Vehicle names when there is no other driver, and the family’s own claims are the ones Wrongful Death describes.

The count. The roll is the crash that kills out of proportion to its numbers. In 2024, 6,250 people riding in passenger vehicles died in crashes in which the vehicle rolled over, 28 percent of all passenger-vehicle occupants killed that year, and in single-vehicle crashes 47 percent of the occupants who died were in a vehicle that rolled, against 13 percent in crashes with more than one vehicle (IIHS); Rolled carries the rest of the count and why the tall ones go over.

Why the vehicle is the evidence. Because there is no other driver’s account to weigh, the case is in the wreck itself. The roof crush measured against the standard. The airbag module’s data, which under the federal standard holds the speed and the brake in the seconds before impact, the record Delta-V is read from. The belt webbing and its marks, which say whether the belt held. The fuel tank, the filler neck and the lines, and the burn pattern that says where the fire started. A burned car gets hauled off and crushed for scrap within weeks, and the proof goes with it. The first job is to keep it, which is the letter Spoliation sends, and the second is to get the record the medical examiner makes, which Inquest explains, because whether the people inside died of the crash or of the fire is a question the autopsy answers.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks after a car rolls and burns, next to the pages on why a car rolls at all and on the single-vehicle crash that still has a defendant.
