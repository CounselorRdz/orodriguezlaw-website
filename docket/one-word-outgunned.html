Outgunned

There is a law firm with more than a thousand lawyers and offices on four continents. Their website says they are never outgunned.

Their Houston office is Suite 3900, on the thirty-ninth floor of 700 Louisiana Street. Mine is Suite 3950. We share a hallway. I think about that word every time I walk it.

Outgunned assumes the fight is a firefight: open field, volume wins, bring more barrels. Some fights work that way. Trials do not. A trial narrows as it approaches, the way a canyon narrows, until the whole war must pass through a doorway: one issue. One witness. One question. One rule the jury can hold in a closed hand. And at a doorway, an army stands in line.

The old strategists had an image for it. One tiger in the pass, and ten thousand deer wait on the mountain. The pass does not care about the headcount. It cares about who is standing in it, and how long he prepared to hold it.

Understand, this is not contempt for the giants. They represent the largest corporations on earth, and someone should. Their cases move billions from one balance sheet to another, and the billions presumably care. But nobody else does. No jury ever wiped an eye over a licensing dispute. My cases decide whether a family keeps the house, whether a man works again, whether a badge answers for a night it would rather forget. Smaller numbers. Larger stakes. Ask any twelve human beings which story holds them.

So the word does not survive the walk down my hallway. A gun is a tool for distance, and trials happen up close: one lawyer, one witness, twelve people near enough to read a face. Nobody has ever been outgunned across a counsel table. Out-prepared, yes. Every day. That is the only arms race running in that building, and it is won at night.

The move, for anyone who has ever felt outnumbered in any fight: stop counting them. Start narrowing the ground. The side that chooses the doorway never needs a headcount.

One word. It only frightens people who plan to fight in the open field.

Filed after midnight.
