The Ramp

Leer este parte en español →

A loaded truck does not take a ramp the way a car does. A car driven too fast into a curve slides before it tips. A loaded tractor tips before it slides, at about four tenths of the force of gravity, and once the inside wheels leave the pavement the only question left is what the trailer lands on.

Reported about 5 a.m. Saturday, an 18-wheeler overturned on the southbound entrance ramp of the West Loop near I-10, near North Post Oak Boulevard, and closed the whole ramp. The trailer was set upright just after 7:30, and by 9 a.m. Houston TranStar reported the ramp open. Officials had not said whether anyone was hurt or what caused the rig to flip (ABC13). That ramp feeds the stretch of road the state ranks as the most congested in Texas, the West Loop from the Katy Freeway to the Southwest Freeway, “at the top of the list again this year” (TxDOT, 2025 Texas Top 100 Congested Road Segments). At 5 a.m. on a Saturday the lanes beside that ramp are nearly empty. At 5 p.m. on a Tuesday they are not. No one is named here, the cause is unresolved, and nothing on this page decides it. It is here for the pattern: the heavy truck that goes over on a curve, in a city built out of curves stacked on top of each other.

The fraction. Every vehicle in a curve is pushed toward the outside, and the push grows with speed and with the tightness of the curve. A vehicle rolls when that push beats its rollover threshold, which is set by how wide it stands and how high it carries its weight, the arithmetic High and Heavy works through for a tanker. For a loaded tractor the number is small. In the rule that put stability control on new tractors, the federal safety agency wrote that “0.4g represents the margin of lateral stability on a typical fully loaded truck tractor with the loads having a high center of gravity,” and explained the difference between a truck and a car in one sentence each: “on light vehicles, the yaw stability threshold is typically lower than the roll stability threshold,” while for heavy vehicles “the roll stability threshold is lower than the yaw stability threshold in most operating conditions” (80 Fed. Reg. 36050). In plain words: a car driven too fast into a ramp skids, and a loaded truck driven too fast into the same ramp rolls. The model manual the states test commercial drivers from says the same thing without the physics: “If you take a curve too fast, two things can happen. The tires can lose their traction and continue straight ahead, so you skid off the road. Or, the tires may keep their traction and the vehicle rolls over.” Then the sentence that belongs in every one of these cases: “Tests have shown that trucks with a high center of gravity can roll over at the posted speed limit for a curve” (CDL Manual, Section 2.6.3). The manual tells the driver to “Slow to a safe speed before you enter a curve,” that “Braking in a curve is dangerous because it is easier to lock the wheels and cause a skid,” and, flatly, “Don’t ever exceed the posted speed limit for the curve.” The agency added the ramp-specific failure: “Some drivers may misjudge the curvature of ramps and not brake sufficiently to negotiate the curve safely” (80 Fed. Reg. 36050).

The sign. The road can say so out loud. The national sign manual provides a Truck Rollover Warning sign that “may be used to warn drivers of vehicles with a high center of gravity, such as trucks, tankers, and recreational vehicles, of a curve or turn where geometric conditions might contribute to a loss of control and a rollover as determined by an engineering study,” and when it is used “it shall be accompanied by an Advisory Speed (W13-1P) plaque indicating the recommended speed for vehicles with a higher center of gravity.” That advisory number is not a guess: “The advisory speed shall be determined by an engineering study that follows established engineering practices.” The sign can even be a changeable message “activated by the detection of an approaching vehicle with a high center of gravity that is traveling in excess of the recommended speed for the condition” (MUTCD, Sections 2C.13 and 2C.08). In a courtroom the yellow plaque under a truck rollover sign is a number an engineer chose for exactly the vehicle that rolled, and the Texas statute turns it into a duty. An operator “may not drive at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing,” and “shall, consistent with Subsections (a) and (b), drive at an appropriate reduced speed if” “the operator is approaching and going around a curve” (Transportation Code Section 545.351). Reasonable and Prudent explains why the number on the sign is where that duty begins and not where it ends.

The computer. Since 2017 a new tractor has been required to help. The federal standard for electronic stability control on heavy vehicles exists “to reduce crashes caused by rollover or by directional loss-of-control.” It applies to truck tractors and buses rated over 26,000 pounds; three-axle tractors built on or after August 1, 2017 must comply, and “All truck tractors manufactured on or after August 1, 2019 must comply with this standard.” The system is defined by what it does: it “enhances rollover stability by having the means to apply and adjust the vehicle brake torques individually at each wheel position” “to reduce lateral acceleration of a vehicle,” and it is “computer-controlled with the computer using a closed-loop algorithm” (49 CFR 571.136). The agency’s rule described the intervention plainly: “When the vehicle’s lateral acceleration approaches the roll stability threshold, the RSC system intervenes,” by one or more of “Decreasing engine power, using engine braking, applying the tractor’s drive-axle brakes, or applying the trailer’s brakes.” It estimated the standard “will prevent 40 to 56 percent of untripped rollover crashes and 14 percent of loss-of-control crashes,” which it put at “1,424 to 1,759 crashes per year resulting in 505 to 649 injuries and 40 to 49 fatalities,” against a base it stated in one line: “In 2011, there were approximately 8,000 crashes involving combination truck rollover” (80 Fed. Reg. 36050). Two things in that rule matter in the file. The computer is not magic: the agency listed “Entry speeds that are much too high for a curved roadway or entrance/exit ramp” among the situations where stability control may be less effective, which is the lawyer’s way of saying a ramp taken fast enough beats the brakes. And the standard is dated. A tractor on the road today may have been built before it, which makes the build date on the door the same first-week fact The Air Brake describes, and whether the system was present, working and not switched off is the maintenance question Inspection and The Jackknife follow through the paperwork.

What it lands on. A trailer that goes over on a ramp has three places to fall. Onto the ramp itself, where the cab roof and the driver’s belt decide the rest, the mechanics The Roll and Rolled set out for the people inside. Onto the lane beside it, where a car has no part built to take a loaded trailer from above, which is the crush Crush describes. Or, on an elevated ramp, over the wall, the fall Over the Barrier measured on the ramps of San Antonio. The load has its own path: a trailer on its side opens, and what was inside becomes the fixed object in the next lane, the pattern Shed traced. And the traffic behind the rollover arrives at highway speed into a stopped lane, which is how one crash becomes the chain The Second Hit sequences.

What the numbers do in court. The fraction sets what the driver owed: a driver who knows a loaded rig tips at four tenths of gravity is required to enter the ramp at a speed that keeps him under it, and the advisory plaque, if there was one, is the engineer’s statement of that speed. The standard sets what the company owed: a tractor that should have had stability control and did not, or had it and let it fail, is a company question, the kind Negligent Entrustment and Respondeat Superior put on the carrier and the trucking company page describes. The proof lives in the truck: the engine computer records the speed into the ramp and whether the brakes were applied, and the electronic log places the truck there, the records Logbook and the black box page explain, and a rolled tractor is righted, towed and repaired within hours, which is why Spoliation sends the letter the first week. And when a car cut across the ramp and the driver swerved, the question is the one The Swerve asks and the chair Responsible Third Party describes.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a loaded truck goes over on a ramp: how fast it entered, what the sign said, what year the tractor was built, whether its computer was working, and who was in the lane beside it.
