Who Served Him

Leer este parte en español →

When a drunk driver hurts or kills a stranger, the law does not have to stop at the driver. It can ask who served him after he was obviously intoxicated, and that question matters most when the driver who caused the harm cannot answer for it.

The pattern. A late crash. A driver who should not have been on the road. A person in another car who did nothing but drive home. By the time anyone stops him, the harm is done. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The driver first. The driver who caused the crash answers for it, and a conviction can open more of the civil case, as Intoxication Manslaughter and The Second Case explain (Intoxication Manslaughter; The Second Case). But a driver who has little or nothing, and too little insurance, can leave a family with a judgment and no way to collect it.

The second place to look. Texas makes the seller answer in a narrow case. “Providing, selling, or serving an alcoholic beverage may be made the basis of a statutory cause of action” on proof of two things: that “at the time the provision occurred it was apparent to the provider that the individual being sold, served, or provided with an alcoholic beverage was obviously intoxicated to the extent that he presented a clear danger to himself and others,” and that “the intoxication of the recipient of the alcoholic beverage was a proximate cause of the damages suffered” (Alcoholic Beverage Code Section 2.02; Proximate Cause). That he drank there is not enough. The question is whether it was apparent to the provider that the person was already obviously intoxicated, a clear danger to himself and others, and he was served anyway. The same section has a separate rule for minors: an adult 21 or older, other than the minor’s parent, guardian or spouse, or an adult in whose custody a court has committed the minor, “is liable for damages proximately caused by the intoxication of a minor under the age of 18” if the adult knowingly served or provided the alcohol that contributed to it, or knowingly allowed it to be served or provided on premises the adult owns or leases.

The seller’s defense, and who proves what. The Alcoholic Beverage Code gives a business a defense for its servers’ over-service. For provisions of the code “relating to the sales, service, dispensing, or delivery of alcoholic beverages to” an intoxicated person, among others, “the actions of an employee shall not be attributable to the employer if” the employer “requires its employees to attend a commission-approved seller training program,” the employee “has actually attended such a training program,” and the employer “has not directly or indirectly encouraged the employee to violate such law” (Section 106.14). In 2008, in a dram shop case about a bar’s opening night, the Supreme Court of Texas divided the proof: “the provider bears the burden of establishing the first two elements,” and “the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing direct or indirect encouragement.” Encouragement can be shown “by circumstantial evidence,” and “at the minimum, by evidence of the provider’s negligence” (20801, Inc. v. Parker). So the training records are the seller’s defense, and how the business actually ran its bar is the family’s answer to it.

Divided by the jury. A seller found liable is not automatically responsible for all of the damages. In a case in which a store sold beer to a driver who had spent the day cutting firewood and drinking beer, and he later crossed the center line and struck another car head-on, the Supreme Court of Texas held that “the language of the proportionate responsibility statute includes claims under the Dram Shop Act,” so “a dram shop is responsible for its proportionate share of the damages as determined by a jury” (F.F.P. Operating Partners, L.P. v. Duenez; Comparative Fault). The jury divides the responsibility, and the proportionate responsibility statute, including its separate rule for a defendant found more than fifty percent responsible, decides what the seller pays.

The record. A dram shop case lives in the business’s own records: the tab and the register’s times, the video over the bar and the door, who was working that night, and the training records the defense will rely on. They belong to the business, and they do not keep forever. Ask for them in writing, early (Spoliation).

Intoxication Manslaughter put the bar receipts and video on the list of what to preserve. The Second Case said a conviction opens doors in the civil case. Negligent Entrustment asks who handed over the keys (Negligent Entrustment). This page asks who poured.
