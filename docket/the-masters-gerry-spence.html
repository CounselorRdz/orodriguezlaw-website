Gerry Spence

He wore a fringed buckskin jacket into the biggest courtrooms in America, spoke like a rancher, and for more than fifty years he did not lose. Gerry Spence.

The record sounds impossible. He never lost a criminal trial in his life, and he did not lose a civil case after 1969. He won for the family of a nuclear-plant worker against a corporation the size of a government. He won an acquittal for a woman the whole world had already convicted in the press. He stood beside a man the federal government was determined to put away after a deadly standoff, and the jury said no.

His secret was the opposite of a trick. He refused to sound like a lawyer. He believed a jury can always tell when a person is performing, and that the moment you try to win, you begin to lose. So he spoke the truth plainly, in the words a real person uses, and he told the client’s story as a story, with a human being at the center of it.

He spent the last part of his life giving that away. He built a college for trial lawyers on his Wyoming ranch and taught a generation to stop imitating and start being themselves, to find the story, and to talk to a jury the way you would talk to a neighbor across a fence. Thousands of lawyers carry his method now.

Gerry Spence died in 2025, at ninety-six. The lesson he leaves is the hardest one for a lawyer to accept: that persuasion is not performance, and that the most powerful thing you can be in front of a jury is honestly, completely yourself.

In his own words: his TEDxJacksonHole talk, Fighting for the People, and Gerry Spence on more than thirty years of teaching trial lawyers.

Gerry Spence (1929 to 2025), the Wyoming trial lawyer known for the Karen Silkwood, Imelda Marcos, and Randy Weaver cases, and for founding the Trial Lawyers College. He wrote more than a dozen books on advocacy, among them How to Argue and Win Every Time.
