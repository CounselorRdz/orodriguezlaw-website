Flashover

Leer esta página en español →

Fire does not spread through a room. For most of its life it stays in the thing that is burning, a chair, a curtain, a mattress, and the room only gets hot. Then, in a second or two, everything in the room is burning at once. That second has a name, and the time it takes to arrive is the time a family has.

The layer. A fire in a room sends its smoke and heated gas to the ceiling, where they pool into a layer that deepens and heats as the fire grows. The layer radiates downward, the way a stove element does, onto every surface below it, and each of those surfaces, the sofa, the rug, the paper on the walls, the clothes on a chair, climbs toward the temperature at which it gives off flammable gas. The National Institute of Standards and Technology defines flashover as “the transition phase in the development of a contained fire” in which the surfaces exposed to the radiation from fire gases hotter than 600 degrees Celsius “reach ignition temperature more or less simultaneously and fire spreads rapidly through the space.” Its tables give the numbers: gases of at least 400 degrees Celsius, 752 Fahrenheit, at the beginning of room flashover; a heat flux to the floor of 20 kilowatts per square meter at the threshold of flashover; and a temperature inside a room undergoing flashover of about 1,000 degrees Celsius, 1,832 Fahrenheit. “This is the most dangerous stage of fire development” (NIST, Fire Dynamics). Before it, a person in the room has a fire to get away from. After it, there is no room.

The furniture. The second comes much sooner than it did. Underwriters Laboratories built living rooms two ways, one furnished as rooms were in the middle of the last century, with the natural materials of the time and the building materials of 1950 to 1970, and one furnished as rooms are now, with synthetic materials, and burned them. “All of the modern rooms transitioned to flashover” “in less than five minutes, while the fastest legacy room to achieve flashover did so in just over 29 minutes”; the legacy rooms “took at least 700 percent longer to reach flashover,” and the laboratory’s summary is that today’s homes see “fires that reach flashover more than eight times faster than homes built 50 years ago,” and that the faster burn “leaves significantly less time for occupants to escape the fire.” The reason is the chemistry of the couch. Synthetic foam and fabric release their heat faster than natural fiber, and “synthetic materials produce more smoke than do natural materials,” smoke in which “the combustion of the synthetics generated asphyxiants, irritants and airborne carcinogenic by-products.” The house makes it worse: the laboratory lists “larger homes + open house geometries + increased fuel loads + new construction materials” as the sum that changed the clock (UL, New Science Fire Safety Journal). An open floor plan is a bigger room with more in it, and a bigger room with more in it has a deeper gas layer feeding more surfaces.

The door. A fire needs air as much as fuel, and a modern house is tight. The federal laboratory draws two curves. In the traditional one the fire grows “until all of the fuel available is burning (fully developed),” and “Then as the fuel is burned away, the energy level begins to decay.” In the other, the one that describes a closed room in a modern house, “As the oxygen level within the structure is depleted, the fire decays,” and a change in ventilation “can result in a rapid increase in fire growth potentially leading to a flashover” (NIST, Fire Dynamics). The laboratory that burned the rooms found that by the time firefighters arrive, “in most cases, the fire has either transitioned to flashover prior to their arrival” or “has become ventilation-limited and is waiting for a ventilation opening to increase its burning rate,” and that “If a fire in a closed room is able to access air to burn from a failed window” it grows again (UL, New Science Fire Safety Journal). The opening does not have to be a window. It can be the bedroom door, opened by someone who smelled smoke and went to look. The fire that seemed to be dying behind it gets the air it was waiting for, and the person in the doorway is standing where the gas layer goes next. This is why firefighters say to close the door and get out, and why a closed door is a wall and an open one is a chimney.

The three minutes. The federal laboratory also measured the time a family actually has. In its tests of flaming furniture fires in a house, the time the study counted before conditions became untenable averaged 177 seconds, under three minutes; in the 1975 tests it compares itself to, the same measure had averaged 1,036 seconds, about seventeen minutes. The alarms got faster too, from an average of 146 seconds to the first alarm in the 1975 flaming tests to 47 seconds now, and it is not enough, because the fire got faster by more (NIST, 2008). Which alarm matters. The two kinds on the shelf, ionization and photoelectric, answer different fires, and the laboratory found that in smoldering fires “the photo detectors sensed the smoldering fires on average 30 minutes earlier than the ionization detectors,” and cautioned that “ionization alarms may not always provide enough time for the most sensitive populations” to escape one, while “photoelectric alarms may not always provide enough time to escape a flaming furniture fire” for the same people (NIST, 2008). What the smoke does to the people who hear the alarm late is Smoke, and what the heat does to the ones who do not get out is Fire.

What the numbers do in court. Each of them assigns a duty or answers a question. The five minutes make the alarm the case, because nothing else in the house can give the three minutes back, and Texas puts the alarm on the landlord: at least one “in each separate bedroom,” one in the corridor that serves them, one on every level (Property Code Section 92.255), tested at the start of the tenancy and repaired on notice during it (Section 92.258), the rule Smoke Alarm sets out, with a cause of action for the tenant’s guest who was hurt because it was not done (Section 92.2611). The door makes the warning the case when the house was not rented, because a host who knows the furnace is bad or the outlet sparks owes the guest the knowledge, which is Invitee and Notice. The chemistry makes the thing that started the fire the case, because a heater, a charger, a cord or a battery that lit a foam couch is a product someone sold, the question of a product case, and the fire marshal’s origin-and-cause file is where the answer starts. And the gas layer makes the record the case, because flashover destroys most of what would explain it. The alarm, the heater, the cord and the outlet are evidence until the drywall comes down, and the first letter in the case asks that they stay where they fell (Spoliation).

The science is settled. A fire heats the ceiling, the ceiling heats the room, the room lights at once, and in a house furnished the way houses are now the whole sequence fits inside five minutes. The question a Texas jury asks is who was supposed to give the people inside the three minutes, and whether they did.

The furniture: UL, New Science Fire Safety Journal, Issue 1 (2012) (the modern and legacy rooms; the five minutes, the 29 and the 700 percent; the eight times; the escape time; the synthetic materials, their smoke and by-products; the four factors; the ventilation-limited fire and the failed window).
