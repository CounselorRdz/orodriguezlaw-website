The Test He Refused

Leer este parte en español →

After a deadly truck crash the driver can say no to the officer’s test, and Texas law has an answer for that. What fewer people know is that the trucking company has a test of its own, on a clock measured in hours, and federal rules do not let it simply look away.

The pattern. A loaded truck runs up on slower traffic in the dark. A car is pushed off the road, the rig folds across the lanes, and a person in another vehicle does not survive. The truck driver climbs down unhurt. An officer asks him for a breath or blood sample, and he says no. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The officer’s test. Texas has already answered the refusal. A person arrested for an offense arising out of acts alleged to have been committed while operating a motor vehicle in a public place while intoxicated “is deemed to have consented,” subject to the rest of the chapter, to give one or more specimens of breath or blood (Section 724.011). When the arrest follows a collision the officer reasonably believes the offense caused, the driver refuses, and the officer reasonably believes that as a direct result of the collision “any individual has died, will die, or has suffered serious bodily injury,” the statute says the officer “shall require the taking of a specimen of the person’s blood” (Section 724.012). Even then the officer needs a warrant, or probable cause to believe that exigent circumstances exist, which is the point No Refusal explains. And at the driver’s own trial, the refusal “may be introduced into evidence” (Section 724.061).

The company’s test. The truck carries a second test the car does not. After a crash involving a commercial motor vehicle on a public road, federal rules require the motor carrier to test each surviving driver who was performing safety-sensitive functions with respect to the vehicle, for alcohol and for controlled substances, “as soon as practicable” when the crash took a human life. When someone was hurt and immediately treated away from the scene, or a vehicle was disabled and had to be towed away, the tests are required only if the driver is cited for a moving violation arising from the crash, within eight hours for the alcohol test and within thirty-two hours for the drug test (49 CFR 382.303). The clocks are short. If the alcohol test is not given within two hours, the company must write down the reasons; at eight hours it must stop trying and keep the same record. The drug test runs to thirty-two hours, with the same duty to write down why it was not done. Federal guidance adds that when someone dies after the crash but within those windows, the company is to keep trying until the windows close (FMCSA). For the alcohol test, a breath or blood test done by law enforcement or public safety officials with independent authority for the test can count; for the drug test, a urine test done by them can. Either way, the test must meet the applicable requirements, and the company must obtain the results.

The driver’s part. A driver subject to the test “shall remain readily available for such testing or may be deemed by the employer to have refused to submit to testing” (382.303), and he may not drink alcohol for eight hours after the crash, or until he takes the post-accident alcohol test, whichever comes first (382.209). The rule makes room for the people who were hurt: nothing in it requires delaying necessary medical attention, or keeps a driver from leaving the scene for the time needed to get help or emergency care. Refusing a required test is itself forbidden, and a company may not let a driver who refuses keep performing safety-sensitive work (382.211), the same bar the rules put on a driver who tests positive for controlled substances (382.215).

The turn. The driver can refuse the officer’s test. The company cannot skip its own. Either there is a result, the company’s or one it obtained from law enforcement, or there is a written record of the reasons there is not. Logbook taught that the proof in a truck case is paper the company keeps. This is more of that paper, made in the first hours after the crash. A broken safety rule is evidence of the standard of care, not a verdict by itself, and what a missing test means is a question for the whole record.

A sample not taken in the first hours cannot be taken later. Ask who was supposed to take it, and ask for the paper before it is filed away (Spoliation).
