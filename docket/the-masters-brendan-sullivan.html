Brendan Sullivan

Leer esta página en español →

Brendan Sullivan is the lawyer people call when the case already looks bad. By his own admission, as the Washingtonian reported, “by the time someone comes to me, they are pretty far up the creek.” He is here for what he did with an early case, in an Army courtroom: he remembered a rule, and he made the court follow it.

He graduated from Georgetown in 1964 and from its law school in 1967, and he had not made law review. He went into the Army through ROTC and served as a captain in the Transportation Corps, a supply officer at Fort Mason in San Francisco, until 1970. That year, at 28, he joined Williams & Connolly, the firm Edward Bennett Williams built, after a Georgetown law professor arranged the interview. Two years later, Williams told the professor, “He is the finest young lawyer we have ever hired.” More than fifty years later, the firm calls him a senior partner, and its page quotes Chambers calling him “one of the greatest lawyers ever.”

The rule came first. While he was a supply officer, his roommate, a young lawyer assigned to defend a soldier who had escaped from the nearby Presidio stockade, did not want the case and asked Sullivan to step in. At the first hearing Sullivan was still introducing himself to his client when the judge took the bench. What he had was a lesson from school: “They told us at law school that when somebody has been languishing in jail, you file a motion for unreasonable delay.” He moved to dismiss for delay, and the motion was granted, the magazine reports, “to everyone’s surprise.” Other prisoners in the stockade then asked for him. In the cases that followed he filed motion after motion, for a change of venue, to suppress evidence, to admit expert testimony, and the judge denied them. But, in the magazine’s account, he “had documentation in law for every request,” and he kept demanding that the military follow its own rules.

Then the school he joined. Williams, in the magazine’s telling, had adopted the creed of Frank Hogan, the senior lawyer at the firm where Williams began: never walk into court without knowing everything about the facts, the client, the opponent, the judge, and especially the lawyers on the other side. Williams let Sullivan take an office near his own to learn from him, and he called the young man “Solomon.” The firm’s lore, in Williams’s words, was blunter: “If the case was winnable, they wouldn’t hire us.” Today the firm says its defense lawyers are “homegrown,” each generation trained by the one before, and that it approaches “virtually every case with the view that it may end with a trial.”

And the composure. He was not one to celebrate. “I don’t do high fives,” he would say, the magazine reports. A lawyer hired late cannot afford to celebrate early. The next ruling is already coming.

Why he is here. Because an injury case has rules on the injured person’s side too, and the other side would rather nobody remembered them: the duty to preserve the evidence (Spoliation), the federal rules that decide who answers for a truck (Crane v. Penske), the statute that tells a driver to move over (Move Over). The lawyer who knows them, files the motion and makes the court enforce them can change a case that looks lost. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Ted Wells and Herbert Wachtell, who wrote the book on New York’s rules. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Learn the basic rules so well that you can find them with the judge already on the bench. Put the law behind every request, including the ones you expect to lose. And save the celebration for when the case is over.

Most lawyers who inherit a bad case start by explaining why it is bad. Sullivan started by reading the rules. One of them still worked.
