A Step From Traffic

Leer este parte en español →

A tow operator is hooking a disabled car at the edge of a highway, a few feet from traffic moving at seventy. A driver who does not move over, and does not slow down, takes the worker off his feet in a second. The first question the law asks is not what the worker was doing there. It is why the car neither moved over nor slowed down.

The pattern. The shoulder is a workplace. A tow operator at the winch, a highway crew beside a truck with its lights going, a lineman at a utility truck, a trooper at a driver’s window. None of them has a door, an airbag or steel around them. Lights are the only warning they can send, and only a driver who heeds the lights turns that warning into protection. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The duty. Texas wrote it down in 2003 and has added to it since (Move Over). When a driver comes up on one of the vehicles the statute lists, he must, unless a police officer directs otherwise, vacate the lane closest to it if the highway has two or more lanes in his direction, or slow to twenty miles an hour under the posted limit, or to five miles an hour where the limit is under twenty-five. The list covers police and other emergency vehicles, tow trucks, highway department and contract road crews not set off from traffic by cones, barrels or barrier, utility trucks, garbage and recycling trucks, and toll authority vehicles, most of them only while their warning lights are on. Since September 1, 2025, it also covers a vehicle driven by an animal control officer removing an animal or a carcass from the road, or by a person authorized to dispose of carcasses removing a carcass, and a vehicle driven by a local authority’s employee issuing parking citations, each using its warning lights (Section 545.157; Senate Bill 305). The rule puts a lane of empty space, or a slower speed, between a moving car and a person with nowhere to go.

Who is not on the list. A private car stopped on the shoulder with its flashers on. The statute names the vehicles that trigger the duty, and a stranded driver’s own car is not one of them; the 2025 session added two vehicles, and a private car was not one of them. The stranded driver is not left without law. Every driver still owes a speed that is reasonable and prudent for the conditions and a proper lookout, which is the ordinary case, and often a strong one, that Stranded walked through in September. Once a patrol car or a tow truck pulls in behind the stranded car with its lights on, the move-over duty is triggered. And when the stopped vehicle is a commercial truck, the truck owes a duty of its own to be seen, which The Shoulder set out.

The statute in a lawsuit. A driver who ignores the rule has done more than drive carelessly. When the injured person is one of the people a safety statute was written to protect, and the harm is the kind it was written to prevent, an unexcused violation can be the negligence itself, and the jury is not asked whether a careful driver would have done the same (Negligence Per Se). The tow operator, the trooper, the road crew and the lineman are the people this statute was written for, and being struck at the side of the road is the harm it was written to prevent. The usual answer, that the driver could not move over, meets the statute’s own text: a driver who could not change lanes had to slow down.

The worker’s share. Texas has the jury assign a percentage of responsibility to the injured person, to each defendant, and to others the law lets it name (Section 33.003; Comparative Fault). The defense will look for a share on the shoulder: the worker stood too close to the lane, or stepped out without looking. A worker standing where the job required, beside a vehicle with its lights going, is a hard place to put that share. The lane beside the worker was supposed to be empty.

Who answers. It starts with the driver who came through the lane. If the worker’s employer carries workers’ compensation, those benefits are generally the worker’s only remedy against the employer, but the bar protects the employer and its agents and employees, not a stranger coming down the highway (Labor Code Section 408.001). The worker may pursue the comp benefits and the claim against that driver at the same time, and the comp carrier has a right to be repaid from what is recovered (Section 417.001; Third-Party Claims). If the driver fled, or carried too little insurance or none, the worker’s own auto policy may answer. Texas auto insurers must include uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage unless a named insured rejects it in writing, and it protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from an uninsured or underinsured driver (Insurance Code Section 1952.101; Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims). For a driver who is never identified, the code requires that “actual physical contact must have occurred” (Section 1952.104).

What lasts. Where the stopped vehicle sat and whether its lights were on. The marks on the shoulder. The striking car’s event data recorder, with its speed and the moment the brake went down, if it went down at all (Delta-V). Any camera on the patrol car, the tow truck or the work truck, and the 911 time stamps. All of it is easiest to collect before the vehicles are towed and the shoulder is swept (Spoliation).

A person working at the edge of a road is not an obstacle to steer around. That person is the reason the lane is supposed to be empty. When a car takes a worker off his feet, the first question is not what he was doing there. It is why the car did not move over or slow down.
