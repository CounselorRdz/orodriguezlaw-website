The Tear

Leer esta página en español →

In a hard enough stop, the chest stops and the heart keeps going for an instant. The aorta is fixed at one point and relatively mobile just above it, and in a violent stop it can tear where the two meet. Most people with this injury never reach a hospital. Some arrive with no telltale sign of it at all.

The mechanism. A medical reference written for clinicians puts it plainly: “Traumatic thoracic aortic rupture is usually associated with a sudden and rapid deceleration,” and of blunt aortic injuries, “Approximately 81% follow a motor vehicle crash.” Falls from height and crush injuries are on the list too (StatPearls). How sudden the stop is depends on the change in speed and on how much distance the car and the restraints give the body to slow down in, which is the subject of Delta-V and Ride-Down.

Where it tears. At a spot with a name. “The aortic isthmus lies just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery,” and “The aortic isthmus is where this moveable section of the aorta meets the fixed descending thoracic aorta.” “This juncture is anchored by the ligamentum arteriosum,” a small band of tissue left over from before birth, and the reference says the attachment amplifies the shearing forces of a sudden stop (StatPearls). The part above keeps moving. The part below is held. The wall between them takes the difference.

How deadly. “Approximately 80% of patients with a blunt traumatic aortic injury die before reaching the hospital,” and among those who live to reach medical care, a free rupture, the wall torn all the way through, “is fatal in 95% to 100% of cases.” The reference adds a caution worth keeping: “many deaths may not be directly attributable to the aortic injury, as most of these patients have polytrauma,” meaning many injuries at once (StatPearls). For the family of someone who died at the scene, what the medical examiner found and wrote down is the first official record of the cause (Inquest).

The quiet version. Not every tear goes all the way through. The reference grades the injury in four steps: “Grade I: Intimal tear,” “Grade II: Intramural hematoma or dissection,” “Grade III: Pseudoaneurysm,” “Grade IV: Complete rupture of the aortic wall.” The ones short of a full rupture are the ones people are most likely to survive long enough to be found, and finding them is not easy: “there are no distinct signs or symptoms specific to an aortic injury,” and “patients may be completely asymptomatic” (StatPearls). That is why the crash itself is the first clue. A high-speed stop is a reason to look, whether or not the person in front of the doctor complains of anything.

Finding it. A plain chest film can show a widened space in the middle of the chest, but “The sensitivity of chest radiography for aortic injury approximates 40%,” so it misses more than it finds. CT angiography, a scan with contrast dye, is described “as the chosen diagnostic imaging modality when evaluating blunt thoracic aortic injury,” with “86% to 100% sensitivity” (StatPearls).

Fixing it. The first step is to take pressure off the torn wall: “Aggressive blood pressure control is a cornerstone of management,” preferably with an intravenous beta blocker. Today the repair is often done from inside the vessel, a stent graft placed through an artery, “known as thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR).” The reference reports “the mortality rate of endovascular repair is 7.2% compared to 23.5% with open surgery” (StatPearls).

In a case. The injury is written in two places. One is the medical record: the scan, the grade, the time from the crash to the diagnosis, the repair and what came after. The other is the crash: how fast the change in speed came, what the restraints did, what the body struck, which is the subject of The Third Collision. A missed diagnosis is a separate question from the crash, with its own rules. The crash data and the vehicle hold the second half of the story, and they do not keep (Spoliation).

The body is built to move with the car. The aorta is held in one place and free in the next, and in the hardest stops, the place where the two meet is where it commonly tears.

StatPearls, Traumatic Aortic Injuries (updated March 19, 2024). The figures are the reference’s own and come from the studies it cites. This is general information, not medical or legal advice. Text and figures as published at the time of this publication.
