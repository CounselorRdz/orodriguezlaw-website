The Fit

Leer este parte en español →

A seat belt is built for a grown body. On a child who has not grown into it, the same belt can ride up off the hips and across the soft middle, and the crash that would have bruised an adult can tear what is underneath.

The pattern. A hard crash. A child is belted in and comes through the impact awake and talking, and at the scene she looks like the lucky one. But an adult lap belt sits where an adult’s hips are. On a smaller child it can ride up over the belly instead of catching the bones of the pelvis, and in a sudden stop the body folds forward over the belt. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

How a belt is supposed to fit. The federal safety agency says it plainly: “For a seat belt to fit properly the lap belt must lie snugly across the upper thighs, not the stomach,” and “The shoulder belt should lie snugly across the shoulder and chest and not cross the neck or face.” That is what a booster seat is for. It “Raises and positions a child so the vehicle’s lap-and-shoulder belt fits properly over the stronger points of a child’s body, the hips and across the chest.” The agency’s advice is to “Keep your child in a booster seat until he or she is big enough to fit in a seat belt properly,” and to “Keep your child in the back seat at least through age 12” (NHTSA).

The injury. Surgeons have a name for what a lap belt in the wrong place can leave behind. “Seat-belt syndrome” is characterized by injury to the bowel and the tissue that anchors it, “along with an associated lumbar spine injury,” the spinal injury “usually a Chance type fracture,” an injury to the lower spine from the body being bent forward and pulled apart over a lap-type belt. The same report points to “improper placement of lap belts above the anterior iliac spine,” and notes that children have “underdeveloped anterior superior iliac spines and rib cage”: the bony points a belt is meant to catch are not fully formed yet. And it warns that “The diagnosis of abdominal and spinal injuries is often delayed or missed completely” (Canadian Journal of Surgery, 2001). The Third Collision explains what follows: the organs keep moving after the skeleton has stopped (The Third Collision). So a child who looks fine at the scene is a child to keep watching.

What the law asks. Texas writes a line into the law. A person commits an offense by driving a passenger vehicle with a child “younger than eight years of age, unless the child is taller than four feet, nine inches,” who is not secured “in a child passenger safety seat system according to the instructions of the manufacturer of the safety seat system” (Transportation Code Section 545.412). A driver also commits an offense by letting a child younger than seventeen who is past the car-seat rule ride unbelted in a seat that has a belt (Section 545.413). The fault for the crash itself is divided by percentages among the people whose conduct caused it (Comparative Fault), and the other side may ask how a child was restrained, as it asks about the seat belt (The Passenger).

The record. The proof is physical, and it is in the car: where the belt actually sat, whether a booster was there, the belt and the seat themselves, the data the vehicle kept. A wrecked car is towed, stored and sold, and the answer goes with it. Ask for it to be preserved before it is hauled off (Spoliation).

The Belt said the belt decides where the body goes in the instant after impact. The Egg Carton said a restraint works in the crash it was built for. A belt built for an adult, on a child, is a restraint in a crash it was not built for. Smoke and Hours Later said the worst of an injury can show itself after everyone has gone home, and the belly belongs on that list.
