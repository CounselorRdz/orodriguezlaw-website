Niccolo Machiavelli

Niccolo Machiavelli has been a slur for five hundred years, shorthand for the ruthless and the scheming. It is a libel on a careful man. What Machiavelli actually taught was something a trial lawyer cannot practice without: see the thing as it is, not as you wish it were.

Machiavelli was a Florentine, a civil servant and diplomat who spent his career watching how power actually moved through the city-states of Renaissance Italy. When his side fell and the Medici returned, he was arrested, tortured, and banished to a farm, and there, out of work and out of favor, he wrote a short book about how rulers gain and keep power. The Prince made his name a curse. But the cartoon Machiavelli, the sneering manipulator, is a misreading. His real offense was simpler and more useful. He described politics as it was, without the comforting varnish, and people have never forgiven him for taking the varnish off.

His own phrase for what he was after was the effectual truth of the thing, as opposed to the imagined version people prefer. He wrote that he would rather go to the real truth of the matter than to how we might wish it to be, and that a man who studies what ought to be done instead of what is done learns his own ruin. That sentence belongs on the wall of every trial lawyer’s office. A case is not what the client wishes had happened, and it is not what the law professor says should happen. It is what a jury of twelve tired strangers will actually do with the facts they are actually given. The lawyer who works from the imagined case loses to the one who works from the effectual one.

Machiavelli divided the forces that decide a life into two, fortune and skill, which he called fortuna and virtu. Fortune is the half you do not control, the weather of events, the luck of the draw. Skill is what you bring to meet it. His counsel was neither to trust luck nor to curse it, but to prepare so thoroughly that when fortune turns, and it always turns, you are ready to seize the moment it hands you. Every trial has fortuna in it, the ruling that goes the wrong way, the witness who surprises everyone, the juror you cannot read. You cannot abolish the luck. You can build the skill and the preparation that let you use whatever the luck throws.

His most notorious line is the one about fear and love, whether a prince should prefer to be loved or feared, and his answer, that it is safer to be feared if you cannot be both. Ripped from its context it sounds monstrous. In context it is a sober observation about reliability. Love, he noted, is held by a bond of obligation that people break the moment it costs them, while fear is more dependable. The point for a lawyer is not to make anyone afraid. It is the realism underneath: do not build your case, or your practice, on other people’s goodwill holding up under pressure. Opposing counsel’s courtesy, a witness’s promise, an adjuster’s friendliness, these thin out when the stakes rise. Plan for how people behave when it is hard, not how they behave when it is easy.

It has to be said plainly, because his name invites the opposite. Machiavelli is not a manual for cruelty, and nothing here is a license to deceive a court or cut a corner. A lawyer has duties, to the client, to the tribunal, to the truth, that Machiavelli’s princes did not. What survives translation into an honorable profession is only the clear sight: the refusal to be sentimental about facts, opponents, or odds. You can be a completely honest lawyer and still owe the man his due for teaching you to look at the board the way it really is.

So the most slandered name in the history of strategy earns a place among the masters. Machiavelli’s gift was not wickedness. It was the discipline of seeing clearly when everyone around him preferred the flattering picture. Strip away the tyrants and what is left is a warning every advocate needs: the case you wish you had will lose to the case you actually have, so learn to tell them apart, and try the one that is real.

Niccolo Machiavelli (1469 to 1527), Florentine diplomat and political theorist; he wrote The Prince (Il Principe) around 1513, after the Medici returned to power and removed him from office. The phrases considered here, the “effectual truth” of the matter and the discussion of fortune, skill, and whether it is better to be loved or feared, are from that work. Taken here for the discipline of realism, not as an endorsement of its politics. This is general information, not legal advice.
