What the Fire Took

Leer este parte en español →

A day after a tanker overturned and burned where the Eastex Freeway meets the North Loop, the investigators had a question that sounds simple: whose truck was it? The fire had taken the license plates and the placards. The law keeps a truck’s identity in more places than that, and on purpose.

What was reported. KHOU reported Wednesday that, according to Houston fire officials, “the intense fire destroyed the truck’s license plates and burned off the placards that would have helped emergency crews identify its cargo,” and that investigators were still working to determine which company owned the tanker and what it was carrying. A fire department official put the stakes in one sentence: “Once we can identify the company then it will be a lot easier to know where they came from and what they were hauling” (KHOU). Earlier reports said the tanker was hauling fuel; ABC13 reported that fire officials said so (ABC13). KHOU reported that a deputy fire chief said Tuesday that officials had not determined what the truck was carrying. It also reported that the truck’s own fuel tanks ruptured and spilled fuel onto the road, that none of it reached the storm sewers, that TxDOT crews inspected the overpass after some concrete broke apart, and that all lanes had reopened by 10 p.m. Tuesday. Fire officials said the tanker’s driver was killed. The driver had not been identified, and officials had not said what caused the tanker to overturn (KHOU).

The two reports describe the Jeep differently. KHOU reported that a Jeep traveling behind the tanker also caught fire and that its driver escaped safely. ABC13 reported, citing Houston police, that the driver of a Jeep was nearby and got out unharmed, and that officials said no other vehicles were involved. Under Investigation set out how the first reports disagreed; the reports have not been reconciled. What happens when a truck’s own fuel tanks are breached is the subject of Saddle Tanks.

The placard. The placard is the diamond on the sides and ends of the tank, and it is written for the first minutes. Federal rules require a bulk tank or vehicle carrying a hazardous material, with limited exceptions, to be placarded “on each side and each end” (49 C.F.R. 172.504), so that the crews who arrive know what they face before anyone finds a document. A placard does not come off when the load does, either: a tank that must be placarded when it is loaded must stay placarded after it is emptied, unless it has been cleaned and purged or another narrow exception applies (Empty). But placards ride on the outside of the tank. On Tuesday, according to fire officials, the fire burned them off.

The paper. A hazardous load also travels with a shipping paper, and the rules say where it must be. While the driver is at the controls, it must be within the driver’s immediate reach while belted in and either readily visible to someone entering the cab or “in a holder which is mounted to the inside of the door on the driver’s side,” so that it is “readily available to, and recognizable by, authorities in the event of accident or inspection” (49 C.F.R. 177.817). That copy rides in the cab, and a cab can burn too. So the rule does not stop there. The carrier that received the shipping paper must keep a copy for a year after accepting the load, accessible at or through its principal place of business, and make it available to an authorized official on request (the same section).

The name on the tractor. A commercial motor vehicle covered by the federal motor carrier safety rules must also display the name of the carrier operating it and the carrier’s USDOT number on both sides, legible in daylight from 50 feet while it is stationary (49 C.F.R. 390.21T). Whether those markings survived on Tuesday has not been reported.

Why the name matters. For a family, the company is not a detail. Until it is known, no one can ask it to keep its records, and the duty to preserve evidence does not wait for a lawsuit to be filed (Spoliation). The carrier is also where the records are: the shipping paper, and the files a carrier is generally required to keep on its trucks and its drivers. And many trucks keep electronic data of their own: an engine computer that can record speed and braking, and an electronic logging device that records the driver’s hours (Truck Black Box and ELD Data). That is why, for a family, finding the company comes first, and is not a formality.

The fire took the plates and the placards. The law requires the carrier to keep a copy of the paper that says what was inside it, accessible at or through its principal place of business. This Dispatch names no one and decides nothing about this crash.
