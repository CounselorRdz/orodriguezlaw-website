Truck Country

Leer este parte en español →

Two people died in South Texas this week hitting the side of a commercial truck. One at a farm-road intersection south of Mercedes, in Hidalgo County. One on Interstate 35 on the South Side of San Antonio. The corridor that runs from Eagle Pass and Laredo down through Starr County to McAllen and Brownsville is truck country: the Eagle Ford oilfield traffic, the freight coming north from the border, the produce and the tankers on two-lane roads built for pickups. This is the pattern as the newsrooms reported it, then connected to the pages that explain why a car loses to a truck and what the law does with it.

South of Mercedes, Tuesday, September 8, shortly after 7 a.m. At Military Road and FM 491, the Department of Public Safety said a Mazda 3 failed to yield the right of way at the intersection and collided with a red Kenworth truck tractor. One person died at the scene. Troopers are investigating. KRGV has the report. Note the phrase, because it will matter below: failed to yield is the trooper’s first-morning read, not a finding.

San Antonio, Sunday evening, September 6. Around 5:19 p.m., in the 4700 block of Interstate 35 South near West Southcross, police said the driver of a southbound Kia Forte struck a crane truck, the impact landing on the driver’s side of the car. He was unresponsive at the scene and died at the hospital. Police had not said what caused it, and the investigation is open. KSAT has the report.

The backdrop. The Texas Department of Transportation counts the crashes in the state’s five energy regions separately, because the roads there carry what the oilfield needs. In 2024, 1,023 people were killed in those regions, one in four of all traffic deaths in Texas. The Eagle Ford Shale alone, the belt of counties that begins at Maverick County on the border and runs northeast across the brush country, saw 14,518 crashes and 173 deaths that year. TxDOT’s own sentence for why: oil and gas production activity puts large trucks and heavy machinery on rural roads and highways, sharing them with local traffic. The energy sector safety page carries the figures.

What the pattern says. A car that meets the side of a truck meets it with the wrong part of the car. A car’s front end has feet of steel built to fold; its side has inches, which is the whole point of Inches of Steel. And a truck’s frame, trailer bed, or outrigger sits at a height the car’s bumper and crush structure were never designed to reach, so the energy-absorbing front end can pass beneath it and the first contact is at the windshield and the roof, which is Underride. What that does inside the body, when the cabin stops in an instant instead of over a foot of crush, is The Third Collision. Nothing here decides either crash above; the geometry of each is a reconstruction question. It explains why the same collision, car into the side of a commercial vehicle, so reliably ends the way these two did.

What the law does with it. Start with the trooper’s phrase. Failure to Yield explains why a preliminary right-of-way conclusion hides a dozen questions: the truck’s speed on a farm road at seven in the morning, the sightlines at that intersection, the sun angle, the truck’s lights, and whether the truck driver had the time and the attention to react, which in Texas is a Comparative Fault question, not an all-or-nothing one. Then the truck itself. A commercial truck carries its own witness, the engine control module and the electronic log, and the black box and ELD data page explains what is on it and how fast it can be lost. Whether the crane truck or the tractor was where it was supposed to be, maintained the way it was supposed to be, and driven inside its hours is the trucking company’s problem, not only the driver’s. And the guard that was or was not on the trailer, and the side guard that federal law still does not require, is Guard.

These cases are worked the same way in Hidalgo County, in Maverick County, in Webb County, and in Bexar County: the truck company’s records, the road, and the reconstruction. The truck wrecks pages explain how, and the same pages in Spanish do the same for the families who read that way first. No one in these two crashes is named here, and nothing on this page assigns fault in an open investigation. A dispatch keeps the record of what happened on South Texas roads this week, from the outlets that were there, next to the pages that explain it.
