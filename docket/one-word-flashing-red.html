Flashing Red

Leer esta página en español →

A flashing red light is a stop sign hanging over the intersection. A flashing yellow is permission to go on, carefully. A signal that has gone dark is a stop sign in every direction. Texas writes all three into its statutes, and each one puts the duty on the driver.

Flashing red. “The operator of a vehicle facing a flashing red signal shall stop at a clearly marked stop line.” Without a stop line, the driver stops before entering the crosswalk on the near side of the intersection. Then: “The right to proceed is subject to the rules applicable after stopping at a stop sign.” A flashing yellow is different. The driver facing it “may proceed through an intersection or past the signal only with caution.” The section “does not apply at a railroad crossing” (Section 544.008).

After the stop. The rules after a stop sign are in another section. A driver approaching an intersection “shall stop, yield, and grant immediate use of the intersection” in obedience to an official traffic-control device, including a stop sign, and “after stopping, may proceed when the intersection can be safely entered without interference or collision with traffic using a different street or roadway” (Section 545.151). So a flashing red is not a pause. It is a full stop, and then a wait for a safe gap in the cross traffic.

Why a signal flashes. The state’s signal manual says that “All traffic signals are programmed to operate in the flash mode for emergencies.” When the signal’s own monitoring equipment detects a problem, it goes to what the manual calls malfunction flash: “Malfunction flash mode is used when the conflict monitor senses a malfunction,” and “Malfunction flash should use all-red flash exclusively.” That is red in every direction, which works like an all-way stop. Flash can also be planned. “Scheduled flash mode typically uses the yellow-red flash type operation,” and signals “can operate in scheduled flash mode as a time-of-day operation (nighttime flash)” (TxDOT). At a yellow-red flash, the two roads are under different rules: the driver on the flashing yellow may go on with caution, and the driver on the flashing red must stop and wait.

A signal gone dark. When the power fails and a signal shows nothing at all, the statute answers that too. A driver facing a signal, other than a freeway entrance ramp control signal or a pedestrian hybrid beacon, “that does not display an indication in any of the signal heads shall stop as provided by Section 544.010 as if the intersection had a stop sign” (Section 544.007). The intersection rule says the same thing in its own words: the driver shall stop, yield, and grant immediate use of the intersection “if a traffic-control signal is present but does not display an indication in any of the signal heads” (Section 545.151). Where to stop is set out for stop signs: “at a clearly marked stop line,” or without one, before the crosswalk, or without either, “at the place nearest the intersecting roadway where the operator has a view of approaching traffic on the intersecting roadway” (Section 544.010).

The presumption. The intersection statute ends with a sentence that matters in a lawsuit: “An operator who is required by this section to stop and yield the right-of-way at an intersection to another vehicle and who is involved in a collision or interferes with other traffic at the intersection to whom right-of-way is to be given is presumed not to have yielded the right-of-way” (Section 545.151). It reaches drivers that section itself requires to stop and yield, which includes a driver at a stop sign and a driver at a dark signal. A presumption is where the argument starts, not where it ends, and a broken rule is evidence of the standard of care, not a verdict by itself (Negligence Per Se). The driver on the flashing yellow is not excused either. The statute lets him go on “only with caution,” and a jury can divide the fault between the two (Comparative Fault).

The evidence. The first question after a crash at a flashing light is which color faced which driver. Yellow one way and red the other is a different case from red in every direction, and a dark signal is different again. Photographs and video of the signal that night, and the people who saw it, answer that question before memory blurs it (Spoliation). Scheduled flash can be a nighttime operation, and what a driver could see at night, and how fast anyone can react to it, are questions of their own (Past the Light; The Reaction).

A flashing red does not mean the signal is broken and the rules are off. It means the rules have gone back to the oldest one at an intersection: stop, look, and go only when it is safe.
