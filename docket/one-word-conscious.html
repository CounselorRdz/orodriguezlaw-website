One WordEntry 165

Conscious

Leer esta página en español →

The word in the jury charge that decides what a death is worth to the person who died is not pain. It is conscious. The family’s claim is for what they lost. The person’s own claim is for what they felt, and the law pays only for what they were awake to feel.

Texas keeps a dead person’s claim alive. A cause of action for personal injury “does not abate because of the death of the injured person,” and it “survives to and in favor of the heirs, legal representatives, and estate of the injured person” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 71.021). That is the survival claim, the second of the two claims Wrongful Death describes, and it stands beside the family’s own claim, which belongs by statute to “the surviving spouse, children, and parents of the deceased” (Section 71.004). The family does not have to prove what the person felt. The estate does.

What the estate recovers is written in a jury charge the Texas Supreme Court reviewed in 2023. A highway beam fell on a pickup on a Texas interstate and killed the driver; the jury awarded his estate damages for his pain and anguish before death, and the charge defined them as “the conscious physical pain and” the “emotional pain, torment, and suffering experienced by” him “before his death.” The court read that as two things: “the mental anguish he suffered in anticipation of his injury” as the beams were falling, and the pain he felt after they hit him, for as long as he was conscious (United Rentals North America, Inc. v. Evans). The first is the fear of the thing coming; the second is the hurt of the thing itself. Both are real damages. Both have to be proved.

Then the court said what proof means, and this is the sentence the entry exists for. “Evidence that there was time for” the man “to anticipate the injury is not evidence that he actually did so.” The plaintiffs’ expert had testified there was time, in a moment, to think something was happening; he “offered no opinion on whether” the man “in fact realized the beam was falling before he was killed.” No one driving nearby saw him swerve or react. The plaintiffs had the burden to prove he “actually suffered the damages claimed,” and “There must instead be some evidence that damages were actually suffered.” On the pain afterward the rule was the same: if he was knocked unconscious at once, “then he did not suffer physical or mental pain following the impact,” and the only witness on that, the medical examiner, said “He may or may not have been knocked unconscious, and there’s no way to know,” and “declined to testify that, in her opinion,” he “more likely than not retained consciousness.” The court held there was no evidence for any of the survival damages, reversed that part of the judgment and rendered judgment that the estate take nothing on the claim. And it left one door open in a footnote: it did not decide whether mental anguish awards “for the split-second anticipation of injury” “should not be permitted at all.” So the rule has two edges. A long enough fall, a fire that burned for minutes, a drowning, a crash the person saw coming from the far side of the median: those are claims, if the record shows the person was there for them. A fraction of a second with no proof the person perceived it is not.

The move: build the record of the last minutes before anyone tells you there is none. The vehicle’s recorder keeps the brake and the steering in the final seconds, which is how Delta-V shows a driver who saw it coming; a swerve is awareness written in data. A radio call, a 911 call, a text sent from the car, a cockpit transmission: a person who says what is happening has proved they knew it, which is what Mayday is about. Witnesses who saw a face, heard a scream, watched a hand go up. The time between the first sign of trouble and the impact, and the time between the impact and death, from the dispatch log, the hospital record and the autopsy; Inquest explains that the medical examiner’s file is open before the lawsuit is, and it is where the question of consciousness is answered or lost. And the witness who ties it together is an Expert willing to say the words the court was waiting for in Evans, more likely than not, and to say why. Every one of those is a record that can be overwritten, which is why Spoliation is the first letter.

One caution, and it runs both ways. A person who never knew is not owed anguish money for the fear they did not feel, and an honest case does not pretend otherwise; the family’s claim for its own loss, the subject of Anguish, stands on its own and does not depend on it. But a person who did know, for a minute or for an hour, is owed what the charge says, and the defense will say there is no evidence of it in every case. There is evidence in most of them. It has to be found while it still exists. This is the shape of the law, not advice on any case.
