The Siren

Leer este parte en español →

A siren is a promise made on behalf of the people in the other car: that they will hear it, know where it is coming from, and get out of the way. Fifty years of study say the promise is often broken before the emergency vehicle reaches the intersection, and the law in Texas is written as if it knows that.

Early Sunday morning in Memphis, at Hickory Hill and East Raines Roads, a Memphis police vehicle carrying two officers answering what police called a man-down call collided with a car carrying six people. Five of the six died, the sixth is in critical condition, the two officers were taken to a hospital in critical condition and released several hours later, and the Tennessee Highway Patrol has taken over the investigation; nothing has been said about lights, sirens, speed or a traffic signal (Action News 5). It is not a Texas crash, no one is named here, and nothing on this page is about it. It is about the pattern it belongs to, which Texas sees too: a government vehicle on an emergency run meeting ordinary traffic at a corner, and the law that decides who answers for it.

Why the siren loses at the corner. Sound thins with distance; every doubling of the distance from the siren cuts the pressure at the ear in half. A closed car with the radio on takes more of what is left, and a siren does not throw its sound evenly in every direction. The federal government measured this in 1977. The Department of Transportation’s study of audible warning devices calculated how far ahead a siren could actually warn in three ordinary situations, one of them a rural road with the windows closed and the radio on, and concluded that “reliance on present audible warning devices to warn drivers in traffic is not justified,” recommending sirens that throw their sound more evenly forward and at higher frequencies (U.S. DOT, 1977). The U.S. Fire Administration’s guidance for emergency drivers starts from the same place: intersections are the most likely place for an emergency vehicle to meet a civilian vehicle, the most common reason is a driver who fails to yield, and the emergency driver must slow to a speed that allows a stop, should never proceed until certain that every other driver sees the emergency vehicle and is allowing it to proceed, and has only one sure way through, which is to visually confirm that all other vehicles have come to a complete stop (U.S. Fire Administration). In the agency’s review of firefighter injury crashes from 1996 to 2012, collisions with other vehicles produced 327 injuries, and 122 of those collisions happened at intersections; intersections also accounted for 18 percent of the vehicle-related deaths of law enforcement officers from 1992 to 2012. The arithmetic of the other car is the same as on every page of this site: at 45 mph a driver who hears the siren needs about a second and a half to place it and get a foot to the brake, 99 feet, and about 97 more to stop on dry pavement, call it 200 feet, so a siren first heard inside that distance, from the side, is heard too late, which is the window The Reaction describes. And when the two vehicles meet, the one hit in the side has only a door between the bumper and the person: side impacts were 22 percent of passenger vehicle occupant deaths in 2024, and 26 percent of the deaths in cars (IIHS), which is what Broadside walked through.

What the law asks. That is the turn. Texas gives the emergency driver privileges and keeps the duty. The operator of an authorized emergency vehicle may proceed past a red light or a stop sign, but only “after slowing as necessary for safe operation,” and may exceed the speed limit “as long as the operator does not endanger life or property” (Transportation Code Section 546.001); an operator using those privileges must use audible or visual signals, the choice left to the operator’s discretion under department policy (Section 546.003); and nothing in the chapter relieves the operator of “the duty to operate the vehicle with appropriate regard for the safety of all persons” or of “the consequences of reckless disregard for the safety of others” (Section 546.005). The other driver owes the siren a duty too: on the immediate approach of an emergency vehicle using its signals, yield, pull parallel to the right curb clear of any intersection, and stop until it has passed (Section 545.156). When the crash happens anyway, a claim against the city runs through the Tort Claims Act, which waives immunity for an employee’s operation of a motor vehicle (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.021) and then takes part of it back for emergencies: no claim for an employee responding to an emergency call if the action complied with the laws and ordinances applicable to emergency action or, where there is no such law, was not taken “with conscious indifference or reckless disregard for the safety of others” (Section 101.055(2)). The Texas Supreme Court set the bar in a fire-truck case: the truck went through an intersection against a red light with its lights and siren on and hit two cars, and the court held that the person suing must show an act the operator “knew or should have known posed a high degree of risk of serious injury,” because a negligence standard “could deter emergency personnel from acting decisively and taking calculated risks” and the test “requires a showing of more than a momentary judgment lapse” (City of Amarillo v. Martin). In 2022 the court added the rule that matters most when the department’s own policy was broken, in a case where a fleeing driver hit a family’s car and killed two children during a chase: a police department’s internal policies “in and of themselves, are not ‘laws’ or ‘ordinances,’” so breaking one does not by itself take the city out of the exception (City of San Antonio v. Maspero), the point Terminated made about a chase and Barricade made about a flooded road. A city’s liability is capped at two hundred fifty thousand dollars a person and five hundred thousand an occurrence (Section 101.023), the notice of a claim is due within six months (Section 101.101), and the fault of a driver who did not yield is set by the jury like anyone else’s (Section 33.003), which is Comparative Fault.

What the proof looks like. Whether the lights and siren were on, which the unit’s own camera and the dispatch log record to the second. The department’s policy on signals and on intersections, which is not the law but is the measure of what the driver knew. The signal’s timing from the city’s traffic system, the event data recorders of both vehicles, and the sight lines at the corner, because the question the law asks is what the operator knew or should have known about the risk, and the corner is where the answer sits.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the science and the law say, next to the pages on the two seconds a driver has once the danger is finally in view, and on what a hit in the side does to the person behind the door.
