The Bite

Leer esta página en español →

A police dog trained to bite is built to take hold of a person. The injury it leaves is decided by the grip, by where it lands, and by how many seconds pass before it lets go.

The grip. In a 2024 expert medical opinion, five physicians affiliated with Physicians for Human Rights, a nonprofit human rights group that uses medicine and science to document abuses, reviewed 30 California police-dog cases and described the mechanism: a “grasp-and-tear mechanism as the dog’s teeth enter the tissues followed by varying degrees of sudden and forceful pulling and torquing,” which “leads to particularly damaging soft-tissue injury patterns, with variable penetrations, crushing, and ripping of tissues.” Dog bites, they wrote, “often penetrate below the skin to the underlying blood vessels, nerves, tendons, muscles, and bone.” They noted that large dogs like German Shepherds and Belgian Malinois, “breeds typically used for police work,” can “tear large flaps of skin from the bone and inflict bone injuries like fractures,” and that “Police canine bites carry a significant risk of nerve injury” (Physicians for Human Rights).

Where it lands. A national study of emergency-room records from 2005 through 2013 counted an estimated 32,951 visits for police-dog bites, 1.1 percent of all emergency visits for dog bites. Compared with other dog bites, police-dog bites landed less often on the head and neck and more often on the legs, and the patients were nearly all male. They were admitted to the hospital at a rate of 3.7 percent (Loder and Meixner, 2019).

The seconds. “While training experts say bites should last seconds, we found numerous cases that lasted minutes,” The Marshall Project reported after a year-long investigation with three partners. Some experts said the longer bites make injuries worse, tearing flesh as the dogs are pulled away, and the reporters found that even dogs trained to release on a verbal command sometimes do not let go (The Marshall Project). The Fifth Circuit’s cases measure it the same way: one to two minutes in Cooper v. Brown, about 64 seconds from the first bite command to the release in Sligh v. City of Conroe, 43 seconds in Thomas v. Bruss.

The training. Departments train dogs in different ways, and the physicians’ opinion notes that studies have found differences in medical harms by training method: “bite and hold” versus “find and bark” (Physicians for Human Rights). The national study noted that its years spanned a time when a change in police-dog practice was encouraged, “from ‘find and bite’ to ‘find and bark’,” and it found no change in the rate of police-dog bites seen in emergency rooms (Loder and Meixner). There is no single national rulebook: “There aren’t federal regulations,” and each department sets its own policy (The Marshall Project).

After. The physicians listed long-term harms that included “disfigurement, scarring, nerve injury, loss of function of arms and legs,” and wrote that “The deep punctures from a dog bite can embed bacteria deep in the skin and other tissues,” leading to infection in around 20 percent of dog bites. They added that research shows “both acute stress disorders and chronic post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and nightmares are common among dog bite victims” (Physicians for Human Rights). Court records read the same way. In Cooper v. Brown, the Fifth Circuit wrote that as a result of the bite the man suffered “years of severe pain from lower-leg injuries that required multiple surgeries, including reconstruction and skin grafts” (Cooper v. Brown).

The record. A bite case is built from records that start disappearing early: body-camera video timed to the second, the count of release commands, the dog’s training and certification files, its history of bites, the photographs of the wounds before they were closed, and the surgeon’s notes after. Video on a short retention schedule can be gone before anyone asks for it (Spoliation).

On the video, a bite is measured in seconds. In the medical chart, it is measured in years.
