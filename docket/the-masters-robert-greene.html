Robert Greene

Some people write about mastery. Robert Greene made a career of taking it apart to see how it works, and the machine he found is one every trial lawyer is standing inside, whether he knows it or not.

Greene came to his subject the long way. Born in 1959, he studied the classics, then spent his twenties and thirties working, by his own count, something like fifty jobs, construction, translation, magazine editing, writing in Hollywood, before he published his first book at nearly forty. That book, The 48 Laws of Power, made him famous. The ones that followed, The 33 Strategies of War, Mastery, The Laws of Human Nature, made him something rarer: a writer that people in hard, competitive fields actually read to get better at what they do. His method never changed. He goes to history, to the lives of people who were undeniably great at something, Napoleon and Darwin and Franklin and dozens more, and mines those lives for the principles underneath, the moves that transfer.

His book Mastery is the one to hand a young lawyer, because it dismantles the most discouraging idea in any craft: that the great ones were simply born that way. Greene’s argument is that mastery is not a gift handed down at birth but the end of a process, and the process is open to anyone willing to walk it. Talent matters less than people believe. What separates the master from the merely competent is years, and the right kind of years.

It starts with what Greene calls the Life’s Task, the sense that a particular kind of work is yours, that you are suited to it in a way you did not entirely choose. For a lot of trial lawyers that is no mystery. They knew early that they wanted to stand up in a room and fight for someone with words. Greene’s point is that the pull is information. It tells you where to spend the years, and the years are the whole game.

Then comes the part no one gets to skip, the apprenticeship. Greene is unromantic about it. Before you are any good, you serve a long stretch where the job is not to shine but to observe, absorb, and acquire skills, to watch how the work is actually done and let it sink below the level of conscious thought. A young lawyer sitting second chair, carrying the boxes, watching a seasoned trial lawyer read a jury, is not wasting time. He is doing the exact thing Greene says makes a master later: paying deep attention in the years when no one is watching him.

What the apprenticeship buys, in the end, is intuition. Greene’s masters do not run down a checklist in the decisive moment; they have done the reps so many times that the knowledge has become a feel, a thing in the hands. Every trial lawyer has seen it. The veteran who senses the cross has gone one question too far and sits down a beat before the danger, the one who feels the room turn and changes course without deciding to. That is not magic and it is not raw talent. It is ten thousand hours arriving on time.

There is a coda to Greene worth telling, because it is the doctrine lived. In 2018, as he was about to publish The Laws of Human Nature, he suffered a serious stroke. A man whose whole subject is patience, apprenticeship, and the slow recovery of a skill then had to apply all of it to relearning how to move and to write. He has kept working. It is one thing to write that mastery is a matter of showing up through difficulty. It is another to have to prove it on yourself.

So he earns his place in a series about trial lawyers, even though he never tried a case. What Greene understood, and wrote down more clearly than almost anyone, is that greatness in a craft is not a lightning strike. It is a road with known stages, and the people who arrive are the ones who respected the apprenticeship, found the one task that was theirs, and put in the years. A young lawyer who believes that will outlast a talented one who does not.

Robert Greene (born 1959), American author of The 48 Laws of Power (1998), The 33 Strategies of War (2006), Mastery (2012), and The Laws of Human Nature (2018), among others; he distills the lives of historical figures into transferable principles of strategy and craft. The framework here, the Life’s Task and the apprenticeship, is drawn from Mastery. In 2018 Greene suffered a serious stroke and has continued to write and teach.
