John Quinn

Leer esta página en español →

John Quinn co-founded a law firm that does only one thing: fight. Its lawyers do not put deals together. They litigate and arbitrate business disputes. He is here for his firm’s maxim, and for the word he uses for what a jury wants from a lawyer.

He graduated from Claremont McKenna College and, in 1976, from Harvard Law School, where he was an editor of the Harvard Law Review. He started in New York at Cravath, Swaine & Moore as a transactional associate, though litigation was what interested him. After a short-lived firm he co-founded and a stint opening a Los Angeles office for another firm, he co-founded a firm of his own in Los Angeles in 1986, and three of that office’s five lawyers left with him. He was, by his own account, “not at all a natural business developer,” yet the firm now has more than a thousand lawyers in offices around the world. In May 2026, after forty years at its head, he stepped back from the firm’s executive leadership and stayed on as chairman. The same year, The American Lawyer named him a Lifetime Achievement honoree.

The rule comes first. In a 2019 interview with Joel Cohen for TalksOnLaw, as the site summarizes it, Quinn says a lawyer should ask from the first day: What is my high ground? What is the other side trying to prove, and how do I block it? His firm’s maxim, as the site reports it, is that “the side that figures out first what is ultimately going to matter wins.” A brief on the same site adds that “most cases at trial turn on just two or three issues,” and that the principle helps in discovery, at trial and in settlement. So he will sometimes take a deposition early, before documents are exchanged, to get admissions before the witness understands the case. He retains experts early. And his firm has long run its own focus groups, because, in the site’s explanation, lawyers do not think the way jurors do.

Then the word. “What’s important is credibility,” he says, and he means something specific: “that the jury believes that you’re a truth speaker” (TalksOnLaw). The summary adds that, according to research, jurors assume there is a truth and that the lawyers know it. The rest of his trial advice, as the site summarizes it, serves that word. A witness facing cross-examination should listen to the question, agree only if every part of it is true, and feel free to give a short, qualified answer, the site’s example being “That’s true in part.” In the summary’s telling, his test of good preparation is a lawyer who can sit with folded hands while the witness handles cross. There is, in the site’s words, “no substitute for preparation,” and some witnesses need hours while others need days. With the other side’s witnesses, the rule is restraint: sometimes the right cross is no questions at all, because cross-examining to no effect hurts. The summary puts the rule in four words: “When in doubt, don’t.” And a complex case has to be boiled down to a few simple themes, and they have to favor the client.

Why he is here. Because an injury case turns on two or three issues too, and the lawyer who finds them first decides what the trial is about: the driver’s hours in the logbook, the inspection nobody did, the color of the light. Because the first paragraph of every motion should say what those issues are (see Brass Tacks). Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Stephen Susman and Morgan Chu. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Start at the end: ask on the first day what the jury will have to believe for you to win, and what the other side is trying to prove. Prepare your witness so well that you can sit with folded hands through the other side’s cross-examination. And never trade credibility for a point, because the jury is listening for one thing above all: whether you speak the truth.

Most lawyers spend the first months of a case collecting. Quinn’s rule is to spend them deciding. Then you know what to collect.
