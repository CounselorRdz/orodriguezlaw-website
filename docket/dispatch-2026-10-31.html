The Second Case

Leer este parte en español →

A drunk-driving crash produces two cases, not one. The first belongs to the State, and it ends in a verdict of guilty or not guilty. The second belongs to the family that was hurt, and it asks a different question: who pays, and how much. The two are separate, but they are not sealed off from each other. When the first ends in a conviction, the second does not start from nothing. The hardest fact it needs has already been established in the criminal court, by a jury’s verdict or the driver’s own guilty plea.

The occasion. This week a Tarrant County jury sentenced a driver convicted of five counts of intoxication manslaughter for a crash on Interstate 35W in Fort Worth in August 2024 in which five people died, three of them children, to a total of sixty-eight years in prison, as CBS News Texas reported from the district attorney’s office. The driver and the five who died are named in that coverage and not here, and this page reaches no conclusion about any civil claim. It is here because a conviction like that one quietly changes the ground under the civil case that so often follows, and most families never learn how until they ask.

What a conviction carries into the civil case. Texas Rule of Evidence 803(22) lets “evidence of a final judgment of conviction” into a later trial when “it is offered in a civil case” and “the judgment was entered after a trial or guilty plea, but not a nolo contendere plea,” “the conviction was for a felony,” “the evidence is admitted to prove any fact essential to the judgment,” and “an appeal of the conviction is not pending.” Intoxication manslaughter is a felony, and its elements are the civil case’s hardest facts: that the driver was “intoxicated and by reason of that intoxication” caused “the death of another by accident or mistake.” So the family suing over a death does not have to prove from scratch that the driver was drunk and that the drinking caused the crash. The criminal court has already settled it, under the highest standard the law has, with the blood that No Refusal explains the State goes and gets. Read the rule’s own limits too: a plea of no contest does not count, and the judgment must be final with no appeal pending, which is one reason the civil case often waits. The record of the first proceeding becomes the evidence of the second. It is the principle behind the old saying papelito habla, the paper speaks: memory fades and stories change, but a judgment on the record stays exactly what it was.

What else the conviction opens. Texas caps the exemplary damages a jury may add to punish a defendant, at the greater of twice the economic damages plus up to seven hundred fifty thousand dollars of noneconomic damages, or two hundred thousand dollars. But the cap “does not apply to a cause of action against a defendant from whom a plaintiff seeks recovery of exemplary damages based on conduct described as a felony” in a list the statute gives, and intoxication manslaughter is on that list, the civil jury still having to find that conduct, which the conviction is there to prove; for every other felony listed the conduct must have been “committed knowingly or intentionally,” and the statute exempts intoxication assault and intoxication manslaughter from even that. And the driver is not the only one who can answer. Under the dram shop law, providing, selling or serving an alcoholic beverage “may be made the basis of a statutory cause of action” on proof that “it was apparent to the provider” that the person served “was obviously intoxicated to the extent that he presented a clear danger to himself and others” and that the intoxication “was a proximate cause of the damages suffered.” When the driver carried too little insurance or none, the family’s own uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, which every Texas auto policy must include unless it was rejected in writing, is a claim of its own. Intoxication Manslaughter lays out all three doors. The criminal case punishes. The civil case is where the family is made whole, and the judgment next door is often its strongest piece of evidence.
