Judy Clarke

Leer esta página en español →

Judy Clarke has spent her career on the other side of the courthouse from this site, defending people charged with the worst crimes of their time. She is here because of one word she asks juries for, and because it is the right word for a family asking a jury to value a life.

She grew up in Asheville, North Carolina, took a degree in psychology from Furman University in 1974 and her law degree from the University of South Carolina in 1977, and went west to the Federal Defenders of San Diego as a trial lawyer. Within five years she was running the office, as executive director from 1983 to 1991; from 1992 to 2002 she led the Federal Defenders of Eastern Washington and Idaho. In 1996 she was sworn in as president of the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers, the first public defender elected to lead it and the second woman. From 2002 to 2009 she was the first full-time Capital Resource Counsel for the federal defender program. She has taught at Washington and Lee, she is a Fellow of the American College of Trial Lawyers, and she practices in San Diego.

Her clients have included defendants in some of the most notorious criminal cases in the country. She seldom speaks to the press. A 2015 New Yorker profile, as the ABA Journal summarized it, reported that she routinely tries to plea-bargain for a life sentence, and that when that fails, she focuses on humanizing her client at trial. As late as 2013, the reports said every one of her clients had been spared death. In 2015, for the first time, a client she had stayed with through the sentencing phase was sentenced to death.

The method is time. NPR, drawing on a colleague who taught with her, reported that she “has endless patience and will spend whatever time it takes,” and that it can take a month or two before lawyer and client even start discussing the alleged crime. Before trial, with investigators and mental health experts, she spends hundreds of hours compiling a social history of her client’s life. A lawyer who learned from her put the lesson this way: “you have to look at the individual who is branded as a defendant.” A lawyer who served as her co-counsel said, “Her strength is being open with clients and giving them a sense of safety.”

In a rare public talk in 2013 she described the people she represents as “looking into the lens of life in prison in a box,” and she said: “Our job is to provide them with a reason to live.”

Then the word. Addressing a jury, as the ABA Journal recounted it, she did not ask for sympathy. She said: “We’re trying to gain your understanding.”

Why she is here. Because the instructions the Supreme Court of Texas approved for civil juries in 2011 tell jurors, in the court’s charge: “Do not let bias, prejudice, or sympathy play any part in your decision.” A family that lost someone, or a person who will live the rest of a life with an injury, cannot win on sympathy and should not try. What a jury is asked to measure in a wrongful death case or an injury case is, above all, the loss itself, and a jury can only measure a loss it understands. That understanding is built the way Judy Clarke builds hers: from the whole life, before and after, gathered over many hours, with investigators and experts, before anyone says a word in court. Because Ross Guberman counts her in Point Made among the advocates he studied, and a lawyer learns as much from the best of the other side as from the best of his own; this site already keeps two of the defense bar’s best, Percy Foreman and Racehorse Haynes. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from her. Spend the time; a client tells you what matters only after he trusts you. Build the whole life, not the worst day. And ask the jury for understanding, which the law allows and the facts can earn, instead of sympathy, which the 2011 Texas charge tells them to set aside.

So when the other side says your client’s case is a bid for sympathy, agree that sympathy has no place in the room. Then show the jury the life, and let them understand it.
