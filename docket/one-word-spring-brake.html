Spring Brake

Leer esta página en español →

On most trucks with air brakes, the parking brake is not a lever that squeezes the brakes on. It is a spring held off by air, and it does nothing until the air is let out, by the driver’s control or by a loss of pressure.

How it works. A state commercial driver handbook puts it in two sentences: “Powerful springs are held back by air pressure when driving. If the air pressure is removed, the springs put on the brakes.” In newer vehicles with air brakes, the control is “a diamond-shaped, yellow, push-pull control knob,” and the driver does one thing with it: “You pull the knob out to put the parking brakes (spring brakes) on, and push it in to release them” (California DMV, Commercial Driver Handbook). A knob left pushed in leaves the springs held off for as long as the air holds. The truck is stopped. It is not parked.

What the law asks. Texas law says an operator may not leave a vehicle unattended without, among other things, “setting the parking brake effectively,” and, on a grade, turning the front wheels to the curb or the side of the highway (Transportation Code Section 545.404). The handbook says the same thing in a driver’s words: “Use the parking brakes whenever you park,” and “Never leave your vehicle unattended without applying the parking brakes or chocking the wheels.” It names the exceptions as well: not when the brakes are very hot from a steep grade, or very wet in freezing temperatures, and then “Use wheel chocks on a level surface to hold the vehicle.”

What the truck must be built to do. The federal safety standard for air brakes lets a manufacturer prove its parking brake one of two ways. One is a hill: the vehicle “shall remain stationary facing uphill and facing downhill on a smooth, dry portland cement concrete roadway with a 20-percent grade,” both loaded to its full rated weight and at its empty weight plus 1,500 pounds, counting the driver and the test equipment. The standard also requires a mechanical means that holds the parking brake on once it is applied, even with the air pressure gone and without electrical power (FMVSS No. 121, S5.6). On the road, the motor carrier rules require the parking brake on most air-braked trucks and trailers built since 1975 to hold the vehicle stationary “under any condition of loading in which it is found on a public road (free of ice and snow)” (49 CFR 393.41(b)).

Adjustment. A spring can only clamp what the brake lets it clamp. “The braking power of spring brakes depends on the brakes being in adjustment,” the handbook says, and “If the brakes are not adjusted properly, neither the regular brakes nor the emergency/parking brakes will work right.” Brake Fade and Out of Service cover what an out-of-adjustment brake does on the road. Parked on a slope, the same brake can let the truck go.

When it rolls. A truck that rolls away does not need speed to do harm. It needs weight and something in its path: a worker between the trailer and a dock, a driver who stepped down to fix something, a car parked behind it. On a slope, a truck that pins someone can keep pressing. What that pressure does to a body, and why a person can die of it even when no single blow would have killed, is in Crush.

In a case. A rollaway leaves a short list of questions. Was the yellow knob pulled? Were the wheels chocked? Were the brakes in adjustment? How steep was the ground? What did the company train its drivers and yard workers to do before stepping away from a truck? The answers come from the position of the parking brake control after the event, the brake measurements, a survey of the ground, any video or vehicle data, and the company’s procedures and training records, and they are easier to get before they are lost (Spoliation).

A truck with air brakes is built to put on its own spring brakes when its air runs out. With the air in and the knob pushed in, the springs hold nothing. That is the whole difference between stopped and parked.
