The Fixed Object

A tree, a utility pole, a bridge pillar. They do not move and they do not give, and that is exactly why a car that leaves the road and finds one is so often the worst wreck of all.

When two cars strike each other, each one is, in a strange way, working for the other. Both have crush zones built to fold. Both have some give. Both move after impact and carry energy away with them. A fixed object does none of that. A concrete pillar does not fold, does not slide, does not take any of the energy with it. Whatever the vehicle brought to the collision, the vehicle and the body inside it have to absorb almost all of it.

Energy is the reason. The energy a moving vehicle carries grows with the square of its speed, so a small rise in speed is a large rise in energy that has to go somewhere. In a crash into something that yields, another car, or a guardrail designed to bend and deflect, that energy is spread over time and distance and shared with the thing that was struck. In a crash into something that does not yield, there is nothing to share it with. The vehicle stops in the few inches its own front end can crush, and the shorter that stop, the more violent the deceleration the body feels.

There is a second problem, and it is about width. A car’s front structure is built to spread a frontal load across the whole width of the vehicle, the rails and beams and crush zones working together. A narrow object defeats that design. A pole, a slender tree, or the corner of a barrier can drive past the spread-out structure and into a small part of the car, folding the vehicle around the object instead of crushing it flat. The peer-reviewed crash data has long placed narrow fixed objects, poles and trees especially, among the most injurious things a vehicle can strike.

This is also why the rigid barrier is the yardstick for crash testing. When the federal safety program measures how a new car protects the people inside it in a frontal crash, it drives the car into a fixed rigid barrier, because the immovable wall is the honest worst case for the structure. The roadside version of that wall is the pole the driver never meant to reach.

Here is why a trial lawyer cares. A run-off-road crash into a fixed object is not only a story about the driver. It is a story about the roadside. Ask what the vehicle struck and whether it had to be there. Ask whether a breakaway pole, a proper barrier, a clear recovery area, or a shielded hazard was called for and missing. Ask how many inches of crush there were, because that number is the stopping distance that decided everything. The object, the vehicle, and the scene all hold the answers, and all three can be cleared, repaired, or scrapped within weeks. Photograph and preserve them first.

The immovable object has no give and no mercy. When a car finds one, read the road as carefully as you read the wreck.

On the crash severity of narrow fixed roadside objects such as poles and trees, see The crash severity impacts of fixed roadside objects (Journal of Safety Research, 2005). On the fixed rigid barrier as the frontal crash-test standard, see the federal New Car Assessment Program (NHTSA). This is general information, not medical or legal advice.
