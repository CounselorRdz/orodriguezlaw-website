No Witnesses

Leer esta página en español →

A crash on an empty road at two in the morning has no eyewitness. It still has witnesses. They are made of rubber, glass, steel and data, and most of them do not last.

The marks. A tire that skids or slides sideways leaves a mark on the road. A guide on crash reconstruction written for prosecutors says these marks “should be photographed and their specific appearance documented to avoid misinterpretation later,” and warns that “Yaw marks are short-lived evidence and may disappear within a few days with heavy traffic on the roadway” (APRI, 2003). Glass and broken parts help show where the vehicles met. The same guide says “Debris location can be crucial in a specific instance, yet debris is often less than completely documented.”

Where they met, where they stopped. Reconstruction starts with the “point of impact” or “area of impact” and ends at final rest, “the point where a vehicle comes to rest”; the path between the two is the vehicle’s trajectory after the impact (APRI). The guide asks whether the road’s friction, its drag factor, was measured at the scene. With those points, those measurements and the weights of the vehicles, a reconstructionist works backward from where the vehicles ended up to how fast they were moving.

The crush. Bent metal is a record of energy. “The speed (energy) required to cause permanent deformation of a vehicle can be analyzed,” the guide says (APRI). How much a vehicle’s speed changed in the crash is what Delta-V measures; how fast that change came matters too, and both are what the body inside absorbs.

The black box. Federal rules do not require a car to carry an event data recorder. They set what one must record if the vehicle has one: passenger cars, multipurpose passenger vehicles, trucks and buses with a gross vehicle weight rating of 3,855 kilograms or less and an unloaded weight of 2,495 kilograms or less, made on or after September 1, 2012. The recorder captures an event when the vehicle’s speed changes by 8 kilometers an hour or more, about 5 miles an hour, within 150 milliseconds, front to back. For the five seconds before the crash, twice a second, it must record the vehicle’s speed, the accelerator or throttle, and whether the service brake was on, and it records the change in velocity during the crash itself (49 CFR Part 563). In December 2024 the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration extended the pre-crash record to 20 seconds at ten samples a second (89 Fed. Reg. 102810); in May 2026 it delayed the schedule, so the longer record phases in for vehicles produced from September 1, 2028, and reaches all covered vehicles of most manufacturers from September 1, 2031; small-volume and limited-line manufacturers have until September 1, 2032 (91 Fed. Reg. 28432). A big truck may keep its own records in its engine and its logging device (Logbook).

The paper. The officer’s report gives a diagram and a narrative, and it often comes from someone who did not see the crash either. It is where to start looking, not the answer (Read the Report).

Why early. Everything above can disappear. Marks fade under traffic, debris gets swept, the road gets patched, and the vehicles get repaired, sold or crushed, taking their recorders with them. The prosecutors’ guide says a vehicle should never be released from police control unless the prosecutor knows the defense has no further use for it (APRI). A family needs the same care. A letter asking that the vehicles and their data be kept should go out early, and a party who loses evidence it had a duty to keep can face consequences in court (Spoliation).

No eyewitness is not the same as no evidence. The road, the cars and the box keep the account, but only for a while.
