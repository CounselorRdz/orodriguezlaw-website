Up to Speed

Leer esta página en español →

A loaded truck pulls out of a driveway or comes down a ramp, and for a long stretch of road it is the slowest thing on it by a wide margin. That is physics, and no one is at fault for physics. What is in the driver’s hands is when to go, and the law, the design of the road and the manual commercial drivers are trained from all put that choice on the one entering.

The weight. The federal study of truck size and weight says the plain thing first: “Trucks are larger and, more importantly, accelerate more slowly than passenger cars, and thus have a greater effect on traffic flow than passenger cars.” The measure it uses is the truck’s “weight-to-horsepower ratio which is a measure of how the vehicle can accelerate,” and its tables run trucks at 150, 200 and 250 pounds for each horsepower (U.S. DOT, Comprehensive Truck Size and Weight Study, Volume III, Chapter IX). The arithmetic is short. On the Interstate system, “The maximum gross vehicle weight shall be 80,000 pounds except where lower gross vehicle weight is dictated by the bridge formula” (23 CFR 658.17). Put that weight behind a 400-horsepower engine and each horsepower is moving 200 pounds. A two-ton car with 200 horsepower asks each one to move 20. The study says where that leads: “As a vehicle’s weight increases, its ability to accelerate quickly for merging with freeway traffic and to maintain speed (especially when climbing hills) is degraded, unless larger engines or different gearing arrangements are used.” A working paper written for the federal study marks the outer limit: “Trucks with weight to power ratios of 300 pounds per horsepower (hp) and more can be traffic impediments and safety hazards because roads are often designed with the level of acceleration capability implied by 300 pound/hp or less” (FHWA, Vehicle Characteristics Affecting Safety). Weight is not a fixed fact about a truck. It is a fact about a load, and the shipping papers record it.

The difference. What slow acceleration costs is a difference in speed. “Poor acceleration is a concern as it can result in large speed differentials between vehicles in traffic, and crash risks increase significantly with increasing speed differential.” The study prints a table and then reads it aloud: “Table IX-3 indicates that crash involvement may be from 15 times to 16 times more likely at a speed differential of 20 miles-per-hour (mph).” In that table the involvement at a difference of 5 mph is about double what it is at none, at 10 mph nearly four times, at 15 mph nearly nine. The table stops at 20. A truck doing 30 in a lane where traffic is doing 70 is at 40. At that difference the space between them closes at about 59 feet every second, so six hundred feet of clear road behind the truck is gone in about ten seconds, and the driver coming up has to notice first that the thing ahead is not doing what everything else on the highway is doing. The eye is poor at that. It reads distance well and speed badly, which is Distance Lies, and at night it reads only the lamps and the reflective tape, which is Conspicuity.

What the road gives. Highway engineers build the answer into a ramp. The Texas design manual says of the acceleration lane, “The lane allows for acceleration and checking for gaps in the freeway traffic,” and that “The length of the acceleration lane is determined on the basis that merging vehicles should enter the through lane at a speed approximately equal to the running speed of the freeway.” For a taper-type entrance the target is narrow: “The geometrics should be designed in a way that drivers attain a speed that is within 5 mph of the operating speed of the freeway by the time they reach the point where the left edge of the acceleration lane joins the traveled way of the freeway” (TxDOT, Roadway Design Manual, Section 15.7.7). The manual’s table of minimum acceleration lane lengths, for flat grades, gives 1,620 feet to reach a 70 mph highway from a stop and 1,790 feet for 75, and for an upgrade of 3 to 4 percent its adjustment table multiplies acceleration lengths on 70 and 75 mph highways by 1.5 to 2 (Section 4.10.2, Tables 4-18 and 4-19). That is about a third of a mile of pavement set aside for an entering vehicle to get up to speed before it joins traffic. A driveway onto a highway or a frontage road often comes with none of it. The truck that pulls out there does its accelerating in the lane that traffic is using.

What the law asks. Texas writes the entering driver’s duty in one sentence: “An operator about to enter or cross a highway from an alley, building, or private road or driveway shall yield the right-of-way to a vehicle approaching on the highway to be entered” (Transportation Code Section 545.155). The frontage road has its own rule, and it favors the vehicle moving between the frontage road and the main lanes: a driver on “an access or feeder road of a limited-access or controlled-access highway shall yield the right-of-way to a vehicle entering or about to enter the access or feeder road from the highway or leaving or about to leave the access or feeder road to enter the highway” (Section 545.154). And on a roadway with marked lanes, the merge included, a driver “may not move from the lane unless that movement can be made safely” (Section 545.060). The driver already on the highway has a duty too, and the statute words it with care: to “control the speed of the vehicle as necessary to avoid colliding with another person or vehicle that is on or entering the highway in compliance with law and the duty of each person to use due care” (Section 545.351). Both duties go to a jury, which is why the box on a crash report marked failure to yield is where the argument begins and not where it ends (Failure to Yield; Negligence Per Se). Texas then divides the fault: a claimant recovers nothing “if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001), and below that line the award is reduced by the claimant’s share (Section 33.012; Comparative Fault).

What the driver was taught. What a professional is taught is in the model manual behind the handbooks commercial drivers study for the license. “Be aware of the size and weight of your vehicle when you cross or enter traffic.” “Because of slow acceleration and the space large vehicles require, you may need a much larger gap to enter traffic than you would in a car.” “Acceleration varies with the load. Allow more room if your vehicle is heavily loaded.” “Before you start across a road, make sure you can get all the way across before traffic reaches you” (Commercial Driver License Manual, Section 2.7.7). Those sentences are this page. The driver who pulled out had been taught that the truck was slow, that the load made it slower, and that the size of the gap was the driver’s to judge. Behind the driver is the carrier that hired, trained and dispatched him, on the terms Respondeat Superior and the page on trucking company liability set out.

What the body meets. The car that arrives at the back or the side of a slow trailer meets it at the difference in their speeds, and a trailer is the wrong shape to hit: its frame rides above the part of a car that was built to crush, which is Underride. The truck barely slows, which is Outweighed. The driver who swerves instead has traded one crash for another, a vehicle sideways at highway speed, which is The Roll.

The evidence. The gap can be rebuilt after the fact, and most of what rebuilds it belongs to someone else. The truck’s engine computer, which may hold its speed in the seconds before a hard stop, and any camera it carried. The cameras at the truck stop or the business whose driveway it was, which often record over themselves within days or weeks. The shipping papers and the scale ticket, because the weight of the load is the physics of this page. The sightline from the driveway, measured and photographed from the height of a truck driver’s eye. The other vehicle’s recorder, for its speed and its braking. The electronic log, for where the truck had been and how long the driver had been at it (Logbook). The first letter asks for all of it before anything is recorded over (Spoliation; truck black box and ELD data).

Why it matters. The law does not ask a loaded truck to be quick. It asks the driver to wait for a gap the truck can use. The difference between the gap that was there and the gap the truck needed is a number of feet and a number of seconds, and both can be measured afterward. The manual says the gap is the driver’s to judge. The case asks how it was judged.
