Special Defect

Leer esta página en español →

When the road itself is the hazard, the first fight is over a label. Texas sorts a dangerous road condition into one of two boxes, often before a jury hears a word, and the box decides what a family must prove about what the government knew. Special defect is the box that is harder to get into and far better to be in.

Special defect. The term comes from the Texas Tort Claims Act, and the Act never defines it. The ordinary rule comes first: “if a claim arises from a premise defect, the governmental unit owes to the claimant only the duty that a private person owes to a licensee on private property, unless the claimant pays for the use of the premises.” Then the exception: “The limitation of duty in this section does not apply to the duty to warn of special defects such as excavations or obstructions on highways, roads, or streets” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.022). Two examples and no definition. In 1978 the Texas Supreme Court read the two examples as a class: “Under the ejusdem generis rule, we are to construe ‘special defect’ to include those defects of the same kind or class as the ones expressly mentioned,” and “The two examples that are included in the statute are not exclusive and do not exhaust the class” (County of Harris v. Eaton). Every case since has been an argument about what is of the same kind as a hole dug in a road or a thing lying across it.

Two duties. The label is worth having because of what it does to the proof. The Court has set the two standards side by side: “a licensee must prove that the premises owner actually knew of the dangerous condition, while an invitee need only prove that the owner knew or reasonably should have known,” and “a licensee must prove that he did not know of the dangerous condition, while an invitee need not do so” (State Department of Highways v. Payne). For a special defect the government owes the invitee’s duty: “That duty requires the State to use ordinary care to reduce or eliminate an unreasonable risk of harm, which the State knew or reasonably should have known” (City of Dallas v. Reed). Should have known can be shown by how long a condition sat there. Actually knew is a different thing, and the Court reads it strictly, as “knowledge that the dangerous condition existed at the time of the accident” (Reed). In a case about loose gravel on a farm-to-market road, the claim ended the moment the Court held the gravel was not a special defect, because the jury had never been asked the two questions an ordinary defect requires: whether the highway department actually knew of the gravel, and whether the driver did not (Texas Department of Transportation v. York). The label also lifts the limits the Act puts on claims about signs and signals: that section “does not apply to the duty to warn of special defects such as excavations or roadway obstructions” (Section 101.060), which is the ground Barricade covers.

Who decides. Not the jury. “Whether a condition is a premise defect or a special defect is a question of duty involving statutory interpretation and thus an issue of law for the court to decide” (Payne). In practice a judge decides it early, often before the evidence is fully gathered, and an appellate court can decide it again. Of the six decisions linked below that answer no, four ended with the claim dismissed and two with a judgment that the claimant take nothing.

The test. The Court has never reduced the class to a rule. It has listed what it looks at: “(1) the size of the condition; (2) whether the condition unexpectedly and physically impairs an ordinary user’s ability to travel on the road; (3) whether the condition presents some unusual quality apart from the ordinary course of events; and (4) whether the condition presents an unexpected and unusual danger” (City of Denton v. Paper). And it has said which way doubt runs. “The class of special defects contemplated by the statute is narrow.” “It does not include common potholes or similar depressions in the roadway.” “Such irregularities in the roadway unfortunately are to be expected.”

The line, in inches. What is one: a hole “oval shaped, varying at places from six to ten inches in depth and extending over ninety percent of the width of the highway,” “four feet wide at some points and nine feet wide at others,” placed so “that one could not stay on the pavement and miss it.” “The hole in the highway in this instance had reached the proportions of a ditch across the highway,” the Court wrote, and “the abnormally large hole was a special defect” (Eaton). And a drop at the road’s edge: describing special defects as conditions that “unexpectedly and physically impair a car’s ability to travel on the road,” the Court cited a Beaumont decision “holding that ten-inch drop-off along shoulder that prevented car’s left wheels from reentering the roadway once they had slipped off was a special defect,” and in the same opinion used “a ditch in the road or a ten-inch drop along the shoulder” as its own shorthand for the class (State v. Rodriguez, citing Morse v. State; quoted again in Paper).

What is not: “a two-inch variance in elevation between traffic lanes is not a special defect,” because “there is nothing unusually dangerous about a slight drop-off between traffic lanes in the roadway,” and “Ordinary drivers, in the normal course of driving, should expect these slight variations on the road caused by normal deterioration” (Reed, a motorcycle changing lanes). A sunken area in a city street, its depth “from two inches to a few inches more at its deepest point,” that pitched a bicyclist over the handlebars (Paper). Loose gravel: “loose gravel is not a special defect as a matter of law” (York). An icy bridge, and a ninety-degree turn in a construction detour (York, collecting the earlier cases; Rodriguez).

Ordinary users. The class protects the people a road is for, doing what a road is for. “Our special-defect jurisprudence turns on the objective expectations of an ‘ordinary user’ who follows the ‘normal course of travel’” (University of Texas at Austin v. Hayes). A culvert that ended “about 22 feet from the roadbed” was not a special defect to a man walking into a field before sunrise; it was “located far enough from the paved surface that vehicular passengers and other normal users of the roadway were unlikely to encounter it” (Payne). A floodgate arm three feet off the roadway was not one to a driver who had already lost control of his car, and a bicyclist who rode around a barricade and into a chain “did not take the normal course of travel” (Hayes). But the condition does not have to be in the lane: “conditions threatening normal users of a road may be special defects even though they do not occur on the surface of a road” (Payne). That is what makes the road’s edge the hard case. The government will say that a driver whose tire left the pavement had left the normal course of travel, as the driver in the floodgate case had. The family will answer with the ten-inch shoulder the Court itself has held up as an example, a drop that would not let the wheels back on. The cases do not settle it in the abstract. They measure, which is where The Drop-Off and Single-Vehicle pick it up.

What the label does not reach. A special defect is a condition, not a decision. The Act “does not apply to a claim based on” “a governmental unit’s decision not to perform an act or on its failure to make a decision on the performance or nonperformance of an act if the law leaves the performance or nonperformance of the act to the discretion of the governmental unit” (Section 101.056), and “Design of any public work, such as a roadway, is a discretionary function involving many policy decisions, and the governmental entity responsible may not be sued for such decisions” (Rodriguez). How wide the shoulder was drawn and where the guardrail starts are the kind of choices courts treat as design. What the edge had become by the night of the crash is a condition, and a condition is what the Act can reach. Every claim under the Act also runs on the Act’s clock. The government “is entitled to receive notice of a claim against it under this chapter not later than six months after the day that the incident giving rise to the claim occurred”; the notice must reasonably describe “the damage or injury claimed,” “the time and place of the incident” and “the incident”; a city’s charter can require notice sooner, and the statute ratifies a charter period “permitted by law”; and the requirement falls away only “if the governmental unit has actual notice that death has occurred, that the claimant has received some injury, or that the claimant’s property has been damaged” (Section 101.101), which is a fact to be proved, not assumed.

The evidence. A label decided by a judge is decided on a record, and this record erodes. The depth of the drop or the hole, measured in more than one place, with a scale in the photograph. Its length, and where it sits against the edge line and the path a tire takes. How long it had been there: the maintenance logs, the inspection reports, the work orders, the complaints and the earlier crash reports on that stretch, which is how should have known is proved and, with the testimony of the people who kept that road, how actually knew is (Notice). Whose road it is, state, county or city, because that decides who must get the six-month notice and whether a charter has shortened it. And all of it before the road is repaired (Spoliation).

Why the word matters. Special defect is the difference between proving what the government should have seen and proving what it did see, and it is decided by a judge reading a tape measure. Two inches between lanes is a road. Ten inches at the shoulder is the Court’s own example of a special defect. Most cases live somewhere in between, and they are decided by what was measured before the road was fixed.
