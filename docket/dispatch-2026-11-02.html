A Hand in It

Leer este parte en español →

The Passenger was about the person with no hand in the crash, and the law that leaves them whole. Most people who get hurt are not passengers. Most of the time the injured person did something too. They stepped off the curb against the signal, or were going a little fast, or looked down for a second. The other side will say so, loudly, and treat it as the end of the case. In Texas it is not the end. Being partly at fault is a subtraction, not a wall, and it closes a claim only when the hurt person’s own share is greater than half.

The pattern. A person on foot is killed at a lit intersection before dawn, crossing where the signal said wait, and the driver who struck them is looked at for drinking. Two people, two wrong choices, one death. It is a pattern that repeats on Texas roads every month, and this page is about the pattern: it names no one, it is pegged to no single crash, and it decides nothing about any of them. It is here because when a family is told that their father crossed against the light, they are often told in the same breath that there is nothing to be done, and that is not what the law says.

What each side owes. The Transportation Code gives the person on foot a rule. “A pedestrian or sidewalk user may not start to cross a roadway in the direction of a ‘Don’t Walk’ signal or a ‘Wait’ signal” (Transportation Code Section 552.002), and outside a crosswalk, a pedestrian “shall yield the right-of-way to a vehicle on the highway if crossing a roadway at a place” “other than in a marked crosswalk or in an unmarked crosswalk at an intersection” (Section 552.005). So the person on foot can carry a share, and an honest page says so. But the same chapter gives the driver a rule that does not switch off when the pedestrian breaks theirs. “Notwithstanding another provision of this chapter, the operator of a vehicle shall” “exercise due care to avoid colliding with a pedestrian on a roadway,” “give warning by sounding the horn when necessary,” and “exercise proper precaution on observing a child or an obviously confused or incapacitated person on a roadway” (Section 552.008). Notwithstanding is the word that matters. Whatever the person out there did, the driver still had to be driving so that he could see them and stop, which is the duty I Never Saw Them is built around, and never at “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” (Section 545.351), and the circumstances before dawn include the two hundred feet of road a headlight shows, which Past the Light measures. And a driver who had been drinking has a due-care question of his own, which the jury answers in the percentages; what alcohol does to the last two seconds is the subject of The Reaction.

How Texas divides it. When both sides carry some of the blame, a Texas jury does not pick a winner. The charge asks it to “determine the percentage of responsibility, stated in whole numbers,” for “each claimant,” “each defendant,” “each settling person” and each responsible third party designated under the chapter (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003). Then two sentences do the rest. “In an action to which this chapter applies, a claimant may not recover damages if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Section 33.001). And for everyone under that line, “the court shall reduce the amount of damages to be recovered by the claimant with respect to a cause of action by a percentage equal to the claimant’s percentage of responsibility” (Section 33.012). A person found forty percent to blame does not walk away with nothing. They recover sixty percent of the loss. A person found fifty percent to blame still recovers half, because fifty is not greater than fifty. The wall stands at fifty-one, and the fight over where the number lands is the real fight, which is what Comparative Fault has said since the day it was written.

What the case asks. What the signal was showing, which the controller’s timing log and the intersection’s cameras keep. How fast the car was going and when the brakes went on, which the car’s own recorder keeps and Delta-V reads. What the driver could see from where he was, which is the headlight arithmetic and the lighting at the corner. Whether the driver was impaired, which is a blood result and a timeline, and not an arrest; a driver arrested on suspicion of driving while intoxicated has been charged with nothing yet, and Intoxication explains what the civil case does with the number when it comes. And whether all of it was preserved, because the cameras overwrite and the recorder can be lost with the car, which is why Spoliation is the first letter in a case like this.

This is the companion to The Passenger, read from the other seat. The passenger had no hand in it, and the law left them whole. The person who had a hand in it is not shut out. Their share is subtracted, and what is left is still theirs. No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law says about the imperfect plaintiff: responsibility in Texas is a percentage, not a verdict on who was the bad one.
