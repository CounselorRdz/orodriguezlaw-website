Miyamoto Musashi

Miyamoto Musashi fought more than sixty duels and lost none of them, and then he sat down in a cave and wrote a book about it. Four hundred years later that book is still read by people who will never hold a sword, because what he was really writing about was how to win.

Musashi was a swordsman in feudal Japan, born around 1584, who by his own account fought more than sixty duels and never lost one, the most famous of them settled with a single stroke on a strip of beach. He founded a school built on an oddity: fighting with two swords at once, the long and the short, where the convention was one. Near the end of his life, living as a hermit in a cave, he wrote The Book of Five Rings, a short and plain treatise on strategy divided into five parts he named for the elements. It is a fighting manual, and it is not, because Musashi insisted the way of strategy was one thing whether you were swinging a sword, running a household, or building a house.

The line that lands hardest for a trial lawyer is one of his shortest. “Perceive that which cannot be seen with the eye,” he wrote. He split sight in two: the ordinary looking that registers what is in front of you, and a deeper perception that reads intention, weight, the thing about to happen. A great cross-examiner has exactly this. He watches not the answer but the small hitch before it, the glance the witness gives his own lawyer, the pause that means the next sentence is being built rather than remembered. The facts on the page are what anyone can see. The case is often in what cannot be.

Musashi was obsessed with timing, which he called rhythm. Everything has a rhythm, he taught, a rhythm of rising and a rhythm of falling, and the master is the one who feels the opponent’s rhythm and breaks it while keeping his own. A trial has a rhythm too. There is a moment when a cross is landing and the witness is off balance, and there is a moment, one question later, when the balance comes back. Knowing the difference, pressing on the first and stopping before the second, is not a different skill from the one Musashi drilled. It is the same skill in a suit.

He was suspicious of specialists and of favorites. Do not develop an attachment to any one weapon, he warned, because the man who loves the long sword is lost when the fight calls for the short one. And he told his students to learn the ways of all professions, how the carpenter and the merchant and the farmer thought, because strategy was general and the narrow man could not see it. The lesson survives the translation intact. The lawyer who can only try one kind of case, or who reaches for the same move in every trial, has a favorite weapon. The complete one has learned enough medicine and engineering and human nature to fight on whatever ground the case is on.

His last and strangest chapter is the one he named the Void, or Emptiness. It is the shortest, and it is about what is left when the techniques have become so practiced that you no longer think about them, when knowledge has turned into a kind of nothing that simply acts. That is not mysticism to anyone who has watched a great trial lawyer work. The reps have gone out of view. What remains is a person who is simply present, responding to the room without a checklist, because the checklist has dissolved into instinct. Musashi’s whole book is a ladder to that emptiness, and every craft that can be mastered has the same top rung.

So a swordsman four centuries gone earns a place among the masters of a craft he never practiced. Musashi was not writing about killing. He was writing about attention, timing, breadth, and the long grind that turns technique into instinct. Strip the swords out of The Book of Five Rings and what is left is a manual for anyone whose work is to face another person across a contested space and prevail. A courtroom is one of those spaces.

Miyamoto Musashi (c. 1584 to 1645), the Japanese swordsman said to have won more than sixty duels undefeated and the founder of the two-sword Niten Ichi-ryu school; he wrote The Book of Five Rings (Go Rin no Sho) as a hermit near the end of his life. The quoted line is from that work (Victor Harris translation widely circulated). Considered here for the craft of strategy, not for combat. This is general information, not legal advice.
