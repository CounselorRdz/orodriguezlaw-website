Seth Waxman

Leer esta página en español →

“For me, the oral argument is all about the questions,” Seth Waxman told the National Law Journal in 2013. “The more questions, the merrier.” He is here for that attitude, and for the preparation that makes it possible.

He was Solicitor General of the United States from 1997 through January 2001. He is a partner at WilmerHale, where he co-chairs the firm’s appellate and Supreme Court practice, and the firm says he has delivered 90 oral arguments in the Supreme Court. His first came in 1992, in Withrow v. Williams. In 2001 he published an essay, “In the Shadow of Daniel Webster: Arguing Appeals in the Twenty-First Century,” that begins with the life of the great nineteenth-century advocate (Daniel Webster) before giving what the journal’s abstract calls his long reserved advice.

The preparation first. “I never show up in any court without knowing as much as I possibly can,” he said. For a case about seeds, that meant spending “a huge amount of time talking to agronomists around the country about germination rates.” He does not pretend it ever feels like enough: “There’s zero tolerance for error,” he told Super Lawyers in 2007, and “I don’t think anybody ever stands up and argues before the Supreme Court and feels totally prepared.”

Then the people on the bench. In that first argument, a joke from Justice Scalia taught him something he kept: “you can talk to them like human beings, and you don’t need to be afraid of them.” He wants the questions to come; what frustrates him, he said, is when they keep interrupting each other so that you never really get the chance to answer any of them.

And the shape of the answer. Watching him argue in 2022, a law professor told the Harvard Crimson that he would first lay out a response before filling it in, with phrasing like “I have a two-part or three-part answer.” The listener knows where the answer is going before it starts.

Why he is here. Because the questions are where an injury case is decided too: a judge’s questions at a hearing, a deposition, a juror’s face during the testimony. A lawyer who welcomes them has to have done the work first. In a crash case the agronomists are the reconstructionist, the treating doctor and the engineer, and the lawyer who has talked to them can answer what the jury is wondering (see Delta-V). Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Paul Clement and Carter Phillips. And because of three habits a trial lawyer can take from him. Learn the science until you can talk to the scientist. Treat the judge as a person you are helping, not an obstacle. And tell the listener how many parts the answer has, then give them in order.

Some lawyers hope for an easy bench. Waxman asks for questions, because a question is the court telling you what it needs.
