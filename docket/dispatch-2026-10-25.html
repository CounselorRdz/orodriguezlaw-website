The Clear Zone

Leer este parte en español →

A car that leaves the road needs somewhere to go. The highway engineers have a name for the somewhere, the clear zone, and a whole literature on how wide it should be and what should not be standing in it. When the somewhere is a ditch and a pole, the crash is over in the first roll, and what is left to ask is whether the road ever gave the driver a chance.

Early Sunday, about 2:39 a.m., Houston police were called to the 9000 block of Humble Westfield Road near McKnight Lane in northeast Houston. Officers said a car left the roadway, rolled several times into a ditch and struck a pole; the driver was thrown from the car and died at the scene, and he appeared not to have been wearing a seat belt. The cause remains under investigation (KPRC). The station gives his age and occupation; this page does not, and nothing on it decides why the car left the road. It is here for the roadside, which is the part of a single-vehicle crash that no one photographs until it is too late to measure.

The strip beside the road. The Federal Highway Administration defines a clear zone as “an unobstructed, traversable roadside area that allows a driver to stop safely, or regain control of a vehicle that has left the roadway,” and says what it buys: clear zones “can increase the likelihood that a roadway departure results in a safe recovery rather than a crash,” and “mitigate the severity of crashes that do occur.” The sentence that matters for a ditch comes a few lines later: “Clear roadsides consider both fixed objects and terrain that may cause vehicles to rollover.” How wide the strip should be is a judgment about risk: “The width of the clear zone should be based on risk (also called exposure)” (FHWA, Clear Zones). The agency’s older guidance page carries the design manual’s version, “the total roadside border area, starting at the edge of the traveled way, available for safe use by errant vehicles,” and, from the agency’s geometric design policy, the one number a city street gets: “For local roads and streets, a minimum clear zone of 7 to 10 feet is considered desirable on sections without curb,” with a 10-foot minimum recommended for collector roads. The design guide’s own clear-zone numbers, the page says, are “not considered as exact but as ranges of values within which judgment should be exercised in making design decisions” (FHWA, Clear Zone and Horizontal Clearance). A ditch that tips a car and a pole that stops it, inside those feet, are the two things the guidance was written against. The Fixed Object explains why the pole is the worst of the two, and The Roll why the ditch is not far behind.

The belt in a roll. The agency that writes the vehicle rules evaluated its ejection-mitigation standard in 2025, on six years of crash data, and put the belt in one sentence: “Occupants wearing a seat belt were 98 percent less likely to be ejected in rollover occurrences,” and “The combination of seat belt and curtain air bags reduces the probability of occupant ejections by 99 percent.” Among the dead in those years, “about 5.3 percent” of the belted “were at least partially ejected from the vehicles, whereas 43.3 percent of the fatally injured unbelted were ejected or partially ejected.” The curtain air bags the standard requires were themselves 44.7 percent effective at keeping people inside, and the study’s own conclusion is that “restraint use virtually eliminates the odds of occupant ejection in the event of rollover” (NHTSA, Evaluation of FMVSS No. 226). Thrown Clear is the Docket’s page on why a body outside the car meets the ground, the pole and the road with nothing left to absorb any of it, and why “he was not belted” is the beginning of a question about the hardware, whether the belt was there, whether it latched, whether it held, and not the end of one.

The road’s keeper. Here is the honest part. The clear zone is engineering guidance, not a statute, and a city that leaves a ditch and a pole eight feet from the pavement has not broken a law by doing it. A claim against the road’s keeper runs through the Tort Claims Act, and the Act is narrow about roads. For a premise defect, the governmental unit owes a driver only the duty a private landowner owes a licensee, which is the duty owed a social guest, a step above what is owed a trespasser and well below what is owed a customer, and the limit falls away for a special defect “such as excavations or obstructions on highways, roads, or streets,” or for a missing or broken traffic sign or signal (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.022). The Texas Supreme Court reads that class the way it reads most exceptions to immunity. In 2012 it took the case of a bicyclist thrown over her handlebars by a sunken patch in the center of a lane, “ranging from two inches to a few inches more at its deepest point,” and said: “The class of special defects contemplated by the statute is narrow.” The test is “whether the condition unexpectedly and physically impairs an ordinary user’s ability to travel on the road,” and a dip that could be avoided without leaving the roadway did not. That left the licensee standard, which requires actual knowledge, and “Because Paper has no evidence that the City knew about this hazard before her accident, it was error to deny the City’s motion for summary judgment.” The court reversed and dismissed the case (City of Denton v. Paper). The conditions Single-Vehicle lays out for a claim against the road are those: actual knowledge, a condition that threatens ordinary users, and notice to the unit within six months of the day it happened, or within the shorter period a city’s charter may set, which the Act excuses only where the unit already has actual notice “that death has occurred” or that someone was hurt (Section 101.101). A ditch beside a road the city has maintained for years is a condition the city knows about in the ordinary sense; what the licensee standard asks is whether it knew the condition was unreasonably dangerous, and that proof lives in the prior crashes at the same spot, the work orders, the complaints and the city’s own design standards for the street, none of which are on the scene Sunday morning.

The pole. The pole has an owner, and the owner is usually not the city. A private company that places a pole answers under ordinary negligence rather than the Act, on proof that putting it where it stood, or leaving it there after the road changed around it, was unreasonable, and the clear-zone guidance is the yardstick an expert measures that by. That claim has its own hard parts: a pole that has stood in the same place for thirty years, a road built around it, a driver who left the pavement at a speed no one yet knows. Texas forbids every driver “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” (Transportation Code Section 545.351), and the jury that hears a roadside case will be asked to divide it, in whole-number percentages, among the driver, the road’s keeper and the pole’s owner (Section 33.003), with the claim lost outright if the driver’s share passes fifty (Section 33.001; Comparative Fault). That arithmetic is why the roadside case is brought rarely and won less often, and why, when it is the right case, it is built from the ground out.

What the case asks. The roadside, measured: the distance from the edge of the pavement to the ditch and to the pole, the slope of the ditch, the width of the shoulder if there was one, the curb if there was not, photographed before the grader comes. The pole: who owns it, when it was set, whether it is a breakaway design, what the owner’s own placement standards say. The road: the design speed, the city’s street standards, every crash report at that block, which is where actual knowledge is found or is not. The car: the recorder’s speed and braking in the seconds before it left the road (Delta-V), the belt, the latch, the roof, preserved before the salvage yard crushes them (Spoliation). And the clock: six months to the notice, less if a city charter says so, two years to the suit, all counted from Sunday.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on Humble Westfield Road. A dispatch keeps the record of what the road is supposed to give a driver who leaves it, what the belt does in a roll, and what the law asks before anyone but the driver is made to answer for a ditch and a pole.
