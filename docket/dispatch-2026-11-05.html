Hours Later

A plant fire puts a column of smoke over a town, and the people who breathe it are sorted, without knowing it, by chemistry. Some gases announce themselves the instant they are breathed. Others say almost nothing, and those are the ones that put a person who walked away feeling fine into an emergency room that night.

The pattern. A fire or a leak at an industrial plant sends a plume over a neighborhood or across a work yard. An order to shelter in place goes out, then an all-clear, and most people go home. This page names no plant, no company and no town, is pegged to no single release, and decides nothing about any of them. It is here because the measure of what the smoke did is almost never how it felt at the scene.

The ones that burn. The federal toxic-substances agency writes a medical management guideline, substance by substance, for the doctors who treat the exposure, and the guidelines put the injury where the gas lands. Ammonia dissolves readily in water, and its guideline says it “causes rapid onset of a burning sensation in the eyes, nose, and throat”; “Inhalation of ammonia may cause nasopharyngeal and tracheal burns, bronchiolar and alveolar edema, and airway destruction,” and “Upper airway swelling and pulmonary edema may lead to airway obstruction” (ATSDR, Ammonia). Hydrogen chloride is more soluble still, and for it “The greatest impact is on the upper respiratory tract”; “Its odor and highly irritating properties generally provide adequate warning for acute, high-level exposures” (ATSDR, Hydrogen Chloride). The pain is its own alarm: people cough, their eyes stream, and they get out. Yet even these are not finished when the burning stops. For ammonia, “Pulmonary injury may continue to evolve over 18 to 24 hours.” For hydrogen chloride, the guideline says delayed effects “are unlikely in patients who have minor symptoms that resolve quickly,” and in the next breath that “symptoms can be delayed for 1 to 2 days.”

The ones that wait. Phosgene “is slightly soluble in water,” and its guideline is blunt about what that means: people breathing low concentrations “may experience no irritation or only mild irritation of the upper airway,” and “Phosgene’s irritating quality can be mild and delayed, which may result in a lack of avoidance.” Its smell is no help either; because its odor threshold sits well above the exposure limit, “odor provides insufficient warning of hazardous concentrations.” Then it reaches the deep lung, where it can “cause cell death in the alveoli and bronchioles,” and “a patient may be free of symptoms for 30 minutes to 48 hours before respiratory damage becomes evident” (ATSDR, Phosgene). The nitrogen oxides are the same story told in hours. “The primary site of toxicity is the lower respiratory tract,” where nitrogen dioxide is thought to be “converted to nitric and nitrous acids in the distal airways”; “A symptom-free period of 3 to 30 hours may then be followed by the onset of pulmonary edema,” which the guideline describes as coming “after what may appear to have been quite a trivial exposure,” and it warns that “Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema may develop even if initial pulmonary signs were minimal,” so “it is important that exposed subjects be maintained under medical surveillance for the first 48 hours post-exposure” (ATSDR, Nitrogen Oxides). Pulmonary edema is fluid where air should be. The gas that hurt at once is the one you survived on the spot. The gas that felt like almost nothing is the one to watch, which is the lesson The Quiet Gas teaches about carbon monoxide and Smoke teaches about the airway that swells over the first day.

What the law asks. For a plant that handles dangerous chemicals in quantity, a federal standard sets the duty. It applies to “A process which involves a chemical at or above the specified threshold quantities listed in appendix A to this section” and to a process that holds a Category 1 flammable gas or a low-flashpoint flammable liquid on site in one location “in a quantity of 10,000 pounds (4535.9 kg) or more,” and it contains “requirements for preventing or minimizing the consequences of catastrophic releases” (29 CFR 1910.119). The employer “shall perform an initial process hazard analysis (hazard evaluation) on processes covered by this standard,” must keep up the mechanical integrity of “Pressure vessels and storage tanks,” “Piping systems (including piping components such as valves)” and “Relief and vent systems and devices,” must “establish and implement written procedures to manage changes” to the process, and “shall establish and implement an emergency action plan for the entire plant,” which under the rule it points to must include “Procedures to account for all employees after evacuation” and “Procedures to be followed by employees performing rescue or medical duties” (29 CFR 1910.38). A broken rule is evidence of what care required, and the jury still decides the breach, the point Negligence Per Se makes about safety rules. For a worker, comp is a floor and not a ceiling, as On the Job put it: the comp benefit is “the exclusive remedy” against an employer that carries it, except that the statute “does not prohibit the recovery of exemplary damages by the surviving spouse or heirs of the body of a deceased employee whose death was caused by an intentional act or omission of the employer or by the employer’s gross negligence” (Labor Code Section 408.001); the worker “may seek damages from a third party who is or becomes liable to pay damages for an injury or death that is compensable under this subtitle and may also pursue a claim for workers’ compensation benefits” (Section 417.001), which is where the chemical’s maker, a maintenance contractor and the property owner come in (Third-Party Industrial Claims); and against an employer that opted out of comp, “it is not a defense that” “the employee was guilty of contributory negligence,” that he “assumed the risk of injury or death,” or that “the injury or death was caused by the negligence of a fellow employee” (Section 406.033; Nonsubscriber Work Injury). For the family next door, the question is the plant’s ordinary care and what the air monitors recorded, with the fault divided in whole-number percentages among the claimant, each defendant, each settling person and each designated responsible third party (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003). And because the worst of this injury arrives late, the record made early is the case: the emergency-room visit kept, the time and the symptoms written down, the shelter order and the all-clear saved, the air monitoring asked for before it is gone, which is what Notice and Spoliation are about, and the dose that DuFour v. BP shows an expert must be able to prove.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any release. A dispatch keeps the record of the fact families are rarely told at the all-clear: the all-clear is about the air outside, and the lungs keep their own clock.
