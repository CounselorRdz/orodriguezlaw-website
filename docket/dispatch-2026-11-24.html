Room to Clear

Leer este parte en español →

A railroad crossing can trap a vehicle two ways. A car can stop on the rails because the line of traffic ahead has nowhere to go. A long, low truck can climb the hump and catch its underside on the top. Either way, the next thing to arrive may be a train that cannot stop.

What was reported. The city of Schertz recently spent fourteen thousand dollars from its general fund street maintenance budget to restripe the lines near the railroad crossings along FM 78, KSAT reported on November 23. Police also asked for more signs near the crossings and assigned officers to ticket and warn drivers who do not follow railroad safety laws. Police said many of the crashes involved cars stopping on the tracks, or long trucks getting stuck because of low ground clearance, and that the collisions caused lengthy shutdowns on key roads (KSAT). In August the station reported eleven train crashes involving people or trucks in Schertz since 2022, most of them on Schertz Parkway and FM 78 (KSAT, August 4). Those stories do not say whether anyone was hurt. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

Two ways to be trapped. The first is the line. A driver rolls onto the crossing behind traffic waiting at a light or a sign on the far side, the line stops, and the car is left on the rails. Texas law says an operator may not stop, stand or park a vehicle “on a railroad track,” unless stopping is necessary to avoid conflict with other traffic or to obey the law, a police officer or a traffic-control device (Transportation Code Section 545.302), and a federal rule says no driver of a commercial motor vehicle “shall drive onto a highway-rail grade crossing” without enough space to drive completely through it without stopping (49 CFR 392.12; see Safe Clearance). The second is the hump. In March, describing the crossing at FM 78 and Schertz Parkway, a public information officer told the station: “That’s where people are going up and then over, and their trailer hitches are catching” (KSAT, March 11). That is the problem High-Centered explains.

The owners. A crossing is a place where several owners meet. In August, KSAT reported that the city was trying to coordinate with TxDOT and Union Pacific to repaint the lines that tell drivers where to stop before the tracks. TxDOT said that, in general, it is responsible for maintaining and funding pavement markings and traffic control devices on state-maintained roadways; Union Pacific said state and local highway authorities are responsible for maintaining safety stripes, and that it has an approval process for restriping near its tracks (KSAT, August 4). The road, the paint, the signs and the rails each belong to someone. After a crash, those are the questions: who knew vehicles were getting trapped, who could change it, and what was done (see Special Defect and Spoliation).

What the train cannot do. “It takes the average freight train traveling at 55 mph more than a mile to stop,” Union Pacific says, and it puts the difference in weight this way: a car is to a train about what a soda can is to a car (Union Pacific). A crew can see a car on the rails and still have no way to stop short of it (see The Grade Crossing).

Who answers. A driver who takes a truck onto a crossing it cannot clear has broken a rule written for that moment, and a driver who stops on the rails may have broken one too, depending on why he stopped. When a court adopts such a rule as the standard of care, an unexcused violation establishes the breach, though it is not an automatic verdict: the violation still has to have caused the harm (Negligence Per Se). The route is a question too: in March, KSAT reported that police had started ticketing truck drivers caught using non-truck routes (KSAT, March 11). And when a crossing is known to trap vehicles, the people responsible for the road, the markings and the rails can be part of the case, with fault divided among everyone the jury finds responsible (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003; see Comparative Fault). The Crossing explains which claims against a railroad federal law closes and which it leaves open.

A crossing is a few seconds of road. The rule for the driver is simple: do not start across unless you can finish. The case after a train asks who else made finishing harder than it had to be.
