Morgan Chu

Leer esta página en español →

Morgan Chu tries patent cases, the kind where a jury of people who are not engineers has to decide what an engineer invented. He is here because of how he teaches a jury, and because of what he calls the tougher part of the job.

He left high school without a diploma and got into UCLA through an admissions exception. In the words of a 2004 profile in Super Lawyers, “Chu earned a B.A., an M.A., a Ph.D., and a J.D. in eight years.” The first three degrees are from UCLA and the law degree from Harvard, and he also holds a master’s degree in law from Yale. He clerked for Judge Charles M. Merrill of the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit and joined Irell & Manella in 1977. He became a partner in 1982, has served on the firm’s executive committee since 1985, was co-managing partner for two terms, from 1997 to 2003, and chairs its litigation group. In 2006, in its first awards for excellence, Chambers named him the outstanding intellectual property lawyer in the United States, and it has called him, as the American Academy of Arts and Sciences quotes it, “beyond doubt the most gifted trial lawyer in the USA.” The Academy elected him in 2023.

The teaching is a method, and the method is testing. Describing his preparation for a 1986 trial, he told the UC Davis Business Law Journal in an interview published in January 2005: “We tested out ways of explaining the subject matter to people to see if it would hold, see if it would stick.” It was “a lengthy, in-depth iterative process.” “We wanted to empower the jurors,” he said, “so that they could manipulate the concepts for themselves.” He does not think an intellectual property litigator needs a scientific background. “What’s important is having an inquiring mind,” he said, and enthusiasm for “teaching those same subjects to a judge and a jury.” His own way of learning, he told Super Lawyers, is to “sit down with the smartest people in the world on these matters and ask a lot of dumb questions.” Good trial lawyers, he told the journal, enjoy tough problems and love solving them, and he described the method: “look at the problem, tilt it, look at its underside, turn it around, flip it.”

Then the discipline. “The key is credibility,” he told the journal, and “Jurors start out, rightfully, being skeptical of everyone’s position in a trial.” So a case “has to be built, brick by brick. And each brick adds to the credibility.” Lawyers learn to spot issues, he said, but are not taught as well how to throw them away. “The tougher part is throwing away issues.” In his view, “it’s never wise to try a case by attempting to erect all fifteen defenses,” because “the weak defenses clutter up and get in the way of the strong defenses.” Asked about the biggest mistake he has seen trial lawyers make, he said that “they lose credibility by stretching their arguments to the breaking point.” Once trial starts, the plan keeps moving. “We have a strategy, but we’re always reevaluating it,” he said, describing daily reviews and decisions such as “deciding not to call certain witnesses that we were certain to call at the beginning of trial.” And with the client, the same honesty: “My job is to advise them of all the risks, all the expenses, all the burdens, how it might not work out” (Super Lawyers).

Why he is here. Because an injury case is a science case more often than people think: the physics of a crash (Delta-V), the path from the wreck to the body (The Mechanism Paragraph), the chemistry of a leak (The Dose). A jury that can work the concepts for itself does not need to take anyone’s expert on faith. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside John Quinn, who uses the same word for what a jury wants. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Test your explanation before trial until it holds and it sticks. Throw away the weak arguments, because they clutter up the strong ones. And tell the client how it might not work out.

The fifteenth defense feels like insurance. Chu’s lesson is that it costs the other fourteen some of their credibility. Build with bricks that hold.
