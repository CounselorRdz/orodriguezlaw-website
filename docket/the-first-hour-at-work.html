The First Hour: At Work

Leer esta página en español →

Part 2 of 3. After a serious injury at work, the company starts writing its account of what happened before the worker leaves the hospital. The first hour decides whether there is any other account.

The employer’s clock. Federal rules require an employer to report a work-related death to OSHA “Within eight (8) hours,” and an in-patient hospitalization, an amputation or the loss of an eye within twenty-four hours. A death must be reported if it happens within thirty days of the incident; a hospitalization, amputation or loss of an eye, if it happens within twenty-four hours of it. The clock runs from when the employer learns of the event, and a crash on a public road outside a construction work zone does not have to be reported this way (29 CFR 1904.39).

The worker’s clock. If the employer carries comp, an injured worker must notify the employer “not later than the 30th day after” the injury, and the notice can go to the employer or to a supervisor or manager (Section 409.001). Missing that deadline can cost the comp claim unless the employer, a supervisor or the carrier knew of the injury, the state finds good cause, or the claim is not contested (Section 409.002). Telling a supervisor the same day, and keeping a copy or a text that shows it, is the best proof that notice was given.

The scene. The machine, the guard, the ladder, the harness: the equipment is the evidence, and it is the first thing a company wants to fix and put back to work (The Harness). Photographs, the model and serial numbers, and the names of the coworkers who saw it are what a worker can keep. A coworker’s memory is like any witness’s: freshest now (Part 1). A company that loses evidence it had a duty to keep can face consequences in court (Spoliation).

The paper. If the employer has no comp coverage, a waiver of the right to sue is not valid if it was signed earlier than the tenth business day after the initial report of injury, or before a medical evaluation by a doctor who is not an emergency care doctor (Section 406.033(f); Nonsubscriber). If the employer has coverage, who the treating doctor will be matters, and the first choice belongs to the worker (Section 408.022; El doctor de la empresa, in Spanish).

Who else. The employer is rarely the only company at a work site. A contractor, an equipment maker or the property owner can share the blame, and comp does not shield them unless that company provided the worker’s comp coverage (Third-Party Claims; Statutory Employer).

At work, the company keeps the records. The first hour is when a worker can start keeping his own.
