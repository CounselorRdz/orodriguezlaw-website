Reasonable and Prudent

Leer esta página en español →

Reasonable and prudent. Three words that make the number on the sign the beginning of the speed law and not the end of it. A driver can be under the limit and over the law.

The rule. Texas writes its speed law as a standard first and a number second. An operator “may not drive at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing,” may not drive faster than is reasonable and prudent under the conditions “having regard for actual and potential hazards then existing,” and “shall control the speed of the vehicle as necessary to avoid colliding with another person or vehicle that is on or entering the highway in compliance with law and the duty of each person to use due care.” Then the statute names five places where an appropriate reduced speed is required: approaching and crossing an intersection or a railroad grade crossing, approaching and going around a curve, approaching a hill crest, on a narrow or winding roadway, and wherever “a special hazard exists with regard to traffic, including pedestrians, or weather or highway conditions” (Transportation Code Section 545.351). Rain is a condition. A dark road is a condition. A child’s bicycle at the curb is a potential hazard. None of them changes the sign, and every one of them changes the lawful speed.

The sign. The number comes in the next section, and it comes in as evidence rather than as the rule. Driving faster than the listed limits, 30 miles an hour on an urban street, 70 on a numbered highway outside an urban district, is “prima facie evidence that the speed is not reasonable and prudent and that the speed is unlawful” (Section 545.352), which is the point Squared and Negligence Per Se both make: over the limit is presumed unreasonable, and the driver can try to explain it. The section says the listed speeds are lawful unless a special hazard requires a slower one. So the limit is a ceiling with a trapdoor under it. A driver at 65 in a 70 on a wet road at night with a stopped car ahead is not protected by the sign, and the question the jury answers is the same one the statute asked first.

The count. The state’s own crash tables say how often the trapdoor opens. In 2023, Texas officers tagged 23,874 units, the vehicles and people in a crash, as speeding at an unsafe speed under the limit, 509 of them in fatal crashes, against 2,678 tagged as speeding over the limit, 316 of them in fatal crashes, and 138,995 tagged as failing to control speed, 501 of them in fatal crashes (TxDOT). More units in fatal Texas crashes were tagged for an unsafe speed under the limit than for speeding over it. The federal count draws the same line: the national agency calls a crash speeding-related when a driver was charged with a speeding offense or an officer indicated that “racing, driving too fast for conditions,” or exceeding the posted limit was a factor, and on that definition 11,288 people died in speeding-related crashes in 2024, 29 percent of everyone killed on the road, 87 percent of them off the interstates (NHTSA). What speed does to the body is the arithmetic Seven Times the Energy and Closing Speed walk through: the energy rises with the square of the speed, and so does the braking distance.

After dark. The clearest statement of the standard for night is in the federal model manual commercial driver tests are built on, and nothing in it is special to trucks. With low beams “you can see ahead about 250 feet and with high beams about 350-500 feet.” “You must adjust your speed to keep your stopping distance within your sight distance. This means going slowly enough to be able to stop within the range of your headlights. Otherwise, by the time you see a hazard, you will not have time to stop” (Model Commercial Driver’s License Manual). At sixty miles an hour, a second and a half of reaction and a hard stop take about 300 feet, so a driver on low beams on an unlit road posted sixty is overdriving his headlights by fifty feet before anything is in them, which is the measure Past the Light sets out and the duty I Never Saw Them turns it into.

The crash report. An officer who writes failed to control speed, or speeding at an unsafe speed under the limit, on the report has recorded a contributing factor, his judgment at the scene from what he saw and was told; it is the officer’s opinion, not a finding, and Failed to Control Speed is about how that phrase is written and read. It is not nothing either. The code tells the insurer and the lawyer where the officer thought the case was, and the report’s diagram, measurements and witness list are where the proof of it begins.

In a civil case. The statute is the standard of care, and the jury applies it to the conditions that existed: the light, the weather, the traffic, the road and what could be seen. The posted limit is evidence on both sides, prima facie unreasonable above it, no safe harbor below it once the conditions called for less. Because the standard is what a reasonable and prudent driver would have done, the case is usually about the conditions themselves, and the fault is divided under proportionate responsibility among everyone whose speed or lookout or lane played a part, which is Comparative Fault.

The move. The event data recorder, which kept the speed in the seconds before impact and the moment the brake went down, which is Delta-V. The weather and the light at the hour, from the records that keep them. The road: its lighting, its curve, its surface, the sight line to where the hazard was. The yaw and skid marks, which give the speed when there is no recorder. The traffic cameras and the dash cameras of everyone who passed. And the preservation letter the first week, because a vehicle is repaired or sold and a recorder is overwritten, which is why Spoliation is where the speed case starts.

Reasonable and prudent. The sign tells you what the state will presume. The road tells you what the law requires.

The rule: Tex. Transp. Code § 545.351 and § 545.352. The count: TxDOT, Crashes by Unit Contributing Factor, 2023 Statewide Crashes (the table counts units reported with the factor, not crashes, and prints the categories as SPEEDING - UNSAFE (UNDER LIMIT), 23,874 units, 509 in fatal crashes; SPEEDING - (OVERLIMIT), 2,678 units, 316 in fatal crashes; FAILED TO CONTROL SPEED, 138,995 units, 501 in fatal crashes); NHTSA, Traffic Safety Facts: Speeding, 2024 Data, DOT HS 813 823 (July 2026) (the definition of a speeding-related crash; 11,288 deaths, 29 percent of traffic fatalities; 87 percent on non-interstate roadways). After dark: AAMVA, 2005 Model Commercial Driver’s License Manual (July 2010), published by FMCSA, Section 2.11.4. The stopping arithmetic assumes a second and a half to perceive and react and braking at seven-tenths of gravity on dry pavement. General information, not legal advice.
