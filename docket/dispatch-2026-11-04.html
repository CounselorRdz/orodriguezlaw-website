Nineteen to One

Leer este parte en español →

A car rear-ending another car is a bad day. A loaded truck rear-ending a car is a different kind of event, and the difference is not the driver’s temper, and not the speed alone. It is weight. Federal law caps a tractor-trailer on the interstate at 80,000 pounds. The barrier the crash-test engineers roll into the side of a car to stand in for a midsize SUV weighs 4,200. That is about nineteen to one, and when two bodies that unequal meet, physics does something to the smaller one it does not do to the larger: it shares the speed out by weight.

The pattern. Traffic is stopped or crawling, the way it stops a hundred times a day on a Texas freeway, and a rig comes up behind the back of the line. This page names no one, is pegged to no single crash, and decides nothing about any of them. It is here because the family of a person hit from behind by a truck is often told what people are told about any rear-end collision, that it was a fender bender gone unlucky, and the weight says otherwise.

The two numbers. The federal weight statute puts the ceiling in one clause: on the interstate system, “such overall gross weight may not exceed eighty thousand pounds, including all enforcement tolerances,” outside the special permits it lists (23 U.S.C. 127). The car-side number comes from the people who crash cars for a living. When the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety toughened its side-impact test, it built a heavier striking barrier: “The new barrier weighs 4,200 pounds,” “close to the weight of today’s midsize SUVs,” replacing “a 3,300-pound barrier” from the original test (IIHS, Aug. 4, 2022). Eighty thousand divided by 4,200 is about nineteen. Against the lighter car the old barrier stood for, it is about twenty-four.

Why it does not stop in time. The commercial driver’s manual says why a loaded rig needs the room it needs. With air brakes “it takes a little time (one half second or more) for the air to flow through the lines to the brakes,” and the manual adds that lag to perception and reaction and the braking itself, so that at 55 miles an hour, for an average driver with good brakes and good traction, “the total stopping distance is over 450 feet” (CDL Manual, Section 5). The federal standard a new tractor must pass, measured from the moment the pedal starts to move, is 250 feet from 60 miles an hour for most tractors loaded to their rated weight and tested with an unbraked control trailer, and 310 for the heaviest (49 CFR 571.121), a distance the highway agency cut “from the current distance of 355 feet to 250 feet” in 2009 (NHTSA, 74 Fed. Reg. 37122); The Air Brake walks through the rule and the arithmetic. The extra room a truck needs is not a courtesy. It is the whole margin of safety, and when the rig is following too close, or moving too fast for the line ahead, the margin is already gone before the driver’s foot moves.

What the weight does. Here is the arithmetic, and it is only arithmetic. When two vehicles lock together in a collision, the momentum of the pair is shared by weight. Take a rig at the federal limit still doing 50 miles an hour when it strikes a stopped 4,200-pound vehicle: 80,000 times 50, divided by the combined 84,200, puts the pair at about 47 miles an hour the instant they move off together. The car has gone from zero to 47 in a fraction of a second. The truck has slowed from 50 to 47. The people in the car took almost the entire change in speed, which Delta-V explains is the number the body actually feels, and for them it is close to being struck at the truck’s full speed; Closing Speed gives the speed everyone pictures, and this is the other variable. Seven Times the Energy explains what speed squared does to the energy; mass is the multiplier on the other side of the equation. Then the car is shoved into whatever sits ahead of it, a hit from behind and a hit from the front inside about a second, the neck thrown into the shape The S-Curve describes before the head restraint catches it, and the body meets the inside of the car in the way The Third Collision and Ride-Down lay out. The figures here are arithmetic on the two sourced weights, not a measurement of any crash.

What the law asks. Texas does not treat the truck as a bigger car, and it does not treat the line of stopped traffic as the truck’s problem to solve at the last second. “An operator shall, if following another vehicle, maintain an assured clear distance between the two vehicles so that, considering the speed of the vehicles, traffic, and the conditions of the highway, the operator can safely stop without colliding with the preceding vehicle or veering into another vehicle, object, or person on or near the highway” (Transportation Code Section 545.062), and no driver may go at “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” (Section 545.351). The weight is why the distance a rig must keep is longer than a car’s, and the breach is still the jury’s to find, not automatic, which is the point Negligence Per Se makes about rules written in words like reasonable. Behind the driver stands the company that dispatched the load, on the terms Trucking Company Liability sets out, and the truck’s own computer wrote down its speed and its braking in the seconds that decide the case, which is why the first letter asks for it (the black box). A jury then divides the fault in whole-number percentages among everyone who had a hand in it (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003), and a person hit from behind while stopped in traffic has, in the ordinary case, no hand in it at all, the question Comparative Fault walks through.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any crash. A dispatch keeps the record of the number families are rarely told: closing speed is the number everyone pictures, and mass is the number that makes it fatal.
