Loaded

Leer este parte en español →

When a truck and a car collide, the crash is only half the danger. The other half is what the truck was carrying. Most freight spills onto the road and gets swept up. Some of it catches fire, or leaks into a ditch, or has to be pumped out before anyone can be pulled from the wreck. This week a tanker rollover in East Texas was the second kind. Here is the pattern as the newsroom reported it, next to the pages that explain what the law does with a load.

Sunday afternoon, Van Zandt County. A westbound diesel tanker carrying about seven thousand gallons struck a pickup on Highway 64, rolled onto its side, and was then hit by an oncoming vehicle, as reported by KBTX. Fuel leaked from several compartments into the road and a ditch, and crews laid down foam to hold off a fire. One person was flown to a hospital in serious condition and two more were treated at the scene. The highway was closed both directions for hours while a hazardous-materials team pumped the fuel off before the tanker could be righted. No one is named here.

The backdrop. The load is the reason a routine wreck stops being routine. Highways carry the overwhelming majority of the country’s hazardous materials, more than two million shipments on an average day, and a CBS News review of federal data found that hazmat incidents on the road climbed about two and a half times over a decade. The cargo that does the most harm is not the exotic one. When federal analysts ranked hazardous materials by the deaths and injuries they cause in transport, the everyday fuels sat at the top, gasoline and diesel, the flammable liquids that move by the millions of gallons every day.

What the pattern says. A passenger car carries maybe a tank of gasoline, and federal rules require its fuel system to hold that fuel through a survivable crash, precisely so an impact the body could live through does not become a fire; that standard, and what happens when a car burns anyway, is what Fire is about. A tanker is not a car with a fuel system. It is a rolling fuel supply, thousands of gallons in a thin steel shell, and when it ruptures the fire is not the rare exception the standard guards against. It is the load. The impact can be survivable and the fire not, and the burn and the smoke, not the collision, are what so often do the killing.

What the law does with it. So the driving is only the start of the case. A commercial driver owes the same duty to control the vehicle and yield the road as anyone else, measured the same way. But a placarded load adds a second body of duty, and it sits on the company as much as on the driver: federal rules require a hazardous-materials endorsement and training before a driver may carry it, placards so responders know what they face, and routing rules that keep such loads from crowds and tunnels where there is a practicable alternative (49 CFR Part 397). The company that chose the driver and put the load on the road answers for both, which is what Respondeat Superior and Negligent Entrustment are about. The cargo is a second source of harm and a second source of duty. The trucking company liability and truck wrecks pages, with the Before You Call companion, cover how a case like that gets built.

No one in this crash is named here, and nothing on this page assigns fault in an open investigation. A dispatch keeps the record of what happened on Texas roads this week, from the outlets that were there, next to the pages that explain it.
