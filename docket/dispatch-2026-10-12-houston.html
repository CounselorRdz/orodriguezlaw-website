The Perimeter

Leer este parte en español →

A crash does not end when the cars stop. For as long as it takes to reach the people inside, clear the wreckage and measure the road, the scene sits in a live lane, and the law asks every driver who comes up on it to treat it as the hazard it is.

Two Harris County nights, two scenes. Overnight into Saturday, near Hillcroft Avenue and Richmond Avenue in southwest Houston, police said their initial information was that a vehicle traveling northbound on Hillcroft at a high rate of speed ran a red light and crashed into another vehicle; Houston Fire Department crews extracted the driver of the second vehicle, who was later pronounced dead. Officers set up a perimeter around the scene to continue the investigation, and then, investigators said, multiple vehicles crashed into the perimeter, including drivers who authorities said were intoxicated. A sergeant on foot was hit by one of them and suffered injuries that were not life-threatening. Officials did not identify the drivers who crashed into the perimeter or say how many vehicles were involved, and the investigation is ongoing (ABC13). Early Sunday, at about 3:40 a.m., Harris County deputies were dispatched to the 16800 block of West Grand Parkway, where investigators said the driver of a 2017 Buick Encore failed to stay in a single lane and left the roadway into a grass median, continued northbound to the end of the median, where it opened to the underpass, went airborne, hit the wall on the opposite side and caught fire with the driver inside. The Spring Creek Fire Department found the driver with no signs of life; the driver has not been identified, and the Sheriff’s Office vehicular crimes division is investigating (ABC13). No one is named here. Both investigations are open, and nothing on this page decides fault in either. They are here for what they share: a road at night, a line that was crossed, and what waited on the other side of it.

The edge line. The Federal Highway Administration defines a roadway departure crash as one “which occurs after a vehicle crosses an edge line or a center line, or otherwise leaves the traveled way,” and from 2016 to 2018 an average of 19,158 deaths came from them, 51 percent of all traffic deaths in the United States; nearly 12,000 people a year die when their vehicle leaves its lane on a rural road (FHWA). What waits past the line decides the rest. A grass median is forgiving for exactly as long as it lasts, and where it ends at an underpass the wall on the far side is the thing The Fixed Object describes, a stop taken in inches instead of feet, which is the arithmetic Ride-Down set out this week; a fire afterward is the injury Fire explains. The duty is the plainest one in the code: on a roadway with marked lanes an operator “shall drive as nearly as practical entirely within a single lane” and may not leave it “unless that movement can be made safely” (Transportation Code Section 545.060). The sentence an investigator writes, failed to stay in a single lane, is where the inquiry starts, not where it ends, because a single-vehicle crash is not a crash without questions, and Single-Vehicle lists them: what the driver was doing, what the car was doing, what the road put in his way, and the car that never touched him.

The scene. At Hillcroft the hazard was the investigation itself. A perimeter is patrol cars with their lights going, officers on foot, a wrecked car at the scene and fire apparatus, in a lane that was open an hour before, and Texas writes three duties for the driver who comes up on it. The move-over statute applies to a stationary authorized emergency vehicle “using visual signals that meet the requirements of Sections 547.305 and 547.702”; unless a police officer directs otherwise, the driver shall vacate the lane closest to the vehicle on a highway with two or more lanes in his direction, or slow to twenty miles an hour under the posted limit where the limit is twenty-five or more, and an offense that results in bodily injury to another is a Class A misdemeanor (Section 545.157), the rule Move Over set out on Saturday. The speed statute asks for more than the number on the sign: an operator may not drive faster than is reasonable and prudent under the conditions, “having regard for actual and potential hazards then existing,” shall control speed “as necessary to avoid colliding with another person or vehicle that is on or entering the highway in compliance with law,” and shall drive at an appropriate reduced speed when approaching and crossing an intersection and where “a special hazard exists with regard to traffic, including pedestrians” (Section 545.351). A sergeant on foot at the scene is the pedestrian the subsection means. And the first crash began at a signal: an operator facing a steady red “shall stop at a clearly marked stop line,” or before entering the crosswalk, and a vehicle that is not turning “shall remain standing until an indication to proceed is shown” (Section 544.007), which is the rule Broadside walked through for the car that is hit in the side, and the one Stranded described from the other direction, a scene in a Houston freeway lane with people on foot in it.

Who answers. Three claims live in one night at Hillcroft, and they do not all point the same way. The family of the driver hit at the light has the ordinary claim against the driver police say ran it, with the statutes above as the measure of care through the doctrine Negligence Per Se explains, and the fault divided by the trier of fact, usually the jury, under proportionate responsibility (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003), which is Comparative Fault. An officer hit on foot at a scene he was working has the same claim against the driver who hit him that any pedestrian would, and when a driver who crashed into a scene had been drinking, the Alcoholic Beverage Code adds a defendant: a bar or other provider can be sued on proof that “at the time the provision occurred it was apparent to the provider that the individual being sold, served, or provided with an alcoholic beverage was obviously intoxicated to the extent that he presented a clear danger to himself and others” and that the intoxication “was a proximate cause of the damages suffered” (Section 2.02), the claim Intoxication describes. The driver who hit the perimeter will say what every driver who hits a stopped vehicle says, that he could not see it in time, and the answer is in the record of the scene: the lights, the time the perimeter went up, the cones or flares if there were any, and the cameras. A claim that the city set the perimeter badly is a different case; it has to get past the Tort Claims Act and its emergency exception (Section 101.055), the road The Siren walked this week, and the claim against the driver who hit the scene does not run through it.

What the proof looks like. The dispatch log, which fixes the first call, the moment the perimeter went up and the time of each crash into it, to the second. The dash and body cameras on every unit at the scene, and the traffic signal’s timing from the city’s system, for the crash that started it. Each striking vehicle’s event data recorder, which kept the speed and the moment the brake went down, if it went down at all, which is Delta-V. For the car that left the Grand Parkway, the same recorder for speed, steering and brake, the road’s own records of the median’s end, the barrier and the lighting, and the first photographs, because a fire consumes the evidence of what came before it, which is why Spoliation starts the morning after.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides either crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the science and the law say, next to the pages on the two lines a Harris County weekend put in a live lane: the one at the edge of the road, and the one around the first crash.
