Five Minutes

Leer este parte en español →

A house fire used to give a family about seventeen minutes. It now gives them about three. The difference is not the fire department. It is, more than anything, the furniture, and the small plastic disc on the ceiling that someone was supposed to test.

Shortly after midnight on Friday, fire crews were called to a home in the 200 block of Blakeley Drive on San Antonio’s Northeast Side, near Rittiman Road and Austin Highway. “While on scene, firefighters rescued one person and a pet from the home, but a second person did not survive, SAFD said.” The person rescued was taken to Brooke Army Medical Center for smoke inhalation. “Investigators said the inside of the home was severely damaged,” and the cause “remains under investigation by the San Antonio Fire Department’s arson team,” which is the unit that investigates fires and not an accusation about this one. The same report carried the department’s reminder to check smoke alarms every month, to install them on the ceiling in every room, “especially bedrooms,” and to call 311 for help getting one, because some stations install them free (KSAT). It followed another deadly house fire in Texas by two days. On Wednesday morning a fire in Van Zandt County, in East Texas, killed a mother and four children, as the sheriff’s office there confirmed on Friday (KLTV), and no cause has been reported for that one either. No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened in either house.

The clock. A room fire has a moment after which no one in the room survives, and the science has a name for it. The National Institute of Standards and Technology defines flashover as “the transition phase in the development of a contained fire” in which surfaces exposed to the radiation from fire gases hotter than 600 degrees Celsius “reach ignition temperature more or less simultaneously and fire spreads rapidly through the space,” and calls it “the most dangerous stage of fire development” (NIST, Fire Dynamics). A fire in a room becomes a room on fire. What changed is how fast it comes. Underwriters Laboratories furnished rooms two ways, one with the natural materials of fifty years ago and one with what is in a living room now, and burned them: “All of the modern rooms transitioned to flashover” “in less than five minutes, while the fastest legacy room to achieve flashover did so in just over 29 minutes,” which is to say today’s homes see “fires that reach flashover more than eight times faster than homes built 50 years ago.” The reason is the foam and the synthetic fabric, which release their heat faster than the natural materials they replaced and whose smoke carries “asphyxiants, irritants and airborne carcinogenic by-products,” made worse by “larger homes + open house geometries + increased fuel loads + new construction materials” (UL, New Science Fire Safety Journal). The physics, and what it means for the person who opens the bedroom door, is Flashover. What the smoke does on the way in is Smoke, and what the heat does is Fire.

The alarm. The federal fire laboratory measured the time a family actually has. In its tests of flaming furniture fires in a house, the time the study counted before conditions in the home became untenable averaged 177 seconds, under three minutes; in the 1975 tests it compares itself to, the same measure had averaged 1,036 seconds, about seventeen minutes (NIST, 2008). Three minutes is enough if the alarm sounds early and the family knows what the sound means. It also matters which alarm. The study found that in smoldering fires, the kind that start in a cushion or a mattress, “the photo detectors sensed the smoldering fires on average 30 minutes earlier than the ionization detectors,” and cautioned that “ionization alarms may not always provide enough time for the most sensitive populations” to get out of one, while “photoelectric alarms may not always provide enough time to escape a flaming furniture fire” for the same people (NIST, 2008). The alarm on the ceiling is a choice someone made, and the choice has a number attached.

The landlord. When the house is rented, Texas says who makes the choice. A landlord “shall install at least one smoke alarm in each separate bedroom in a dwelling unit,” one in a corridor that serves several bedrooms, and one on every level (Property Code Section 92.255). The landlord “shall determine that the smoke alarm is in good working order at the beginning of the tenant’s possession” by testing it, and during the lease “has a duty to inspect and repair a smoke alarm, but only if the tenant gives the landlord notice of a malfunction or requests to the landlord that the smoke alarm be inspected or repaired”; after the tenant moves in, the landlord “is not obligated to provide batteries” if the alarm worked on the first day (Section 92.258). A tenant who takes the battery out without replacing it, or disconnects the alarm, answers for that (Section 92.2611). And the statute reaches past the lease: a tenant’s “guest or invitee who suffers damage because of a landlord’s failure to install, inspect, or repair a smoke alarm as required by this subchapter may recover a judgment against the landlord for the damage,” and against a tenant who disabled it (Section 92.2611). The whole of that rule, and what it does and does not cover, is Smoke Alarm. The reports do not say who owned either house, or whether anyone rented it.

What the case asks. Whether there was an alarm, where it was, what kind it was and whether it sounded, which the fire investigator’s photographs usually answer before anyone thinks to ask. Who owned the house and who lived in it, because the duty to install and test belongs to a landlord, and the duty to warn a guest about a danger the host knew of is the one Invitee sets out. What started it, which is the fire marshal’s origin-and-cause work, and whether what started it was a thing someone sold: a space heater, a charger, a cord, a battery, an outlet, the questions of a product case. What the person who was carried out breathed, because the injury from smoke is measured late and not at the door, which Smoke explains. And what is left. The alarm that did not sound is the best witness in the house, and so are the heater and the cord, and all three go to the dumpster with the drywall unless a letter asks that they be kept (Spoliation).

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on Blakeley Drive or in East Texas. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a house burns in the time it takes to find the keys: what the room was made of, what was on the ceiling and whether it worked, who was supposed to make sure it did, and what the smoke did to the people who got out.
