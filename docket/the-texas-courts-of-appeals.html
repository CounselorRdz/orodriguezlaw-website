The Texas Courts of Appeals

Leer esta página en español →

Part 1 of 3. Lose in a Texas trial court and the case is not over. It goes upstairs, to judges who never see a witness. Before you go up, it helps to know the building.

The Fifth Circuit has its own field guide on this Docket. That court hears appeals from federal courtrooms. A case tried in a Texas state court climbs a different set of stairs, under different rules, to a different set of judges, and after the November 2026 election, several new ones. This is the state-side guide: what the courts are, who sits on them, and how a civil case moves from one to the next. Part 2 takes a closer look at one of them, the Fourth Court of Appeals in San Antonio. Part 3 walks a single appeal from start to finish: the appeal of a summary judgment.

What It Is

Texas has two high courts. The Supreme Court of Texas has “statewide, final appellate jurisdiction in all civil and juvenile cases.” The Court of Criminal Appeals has the final word in criminal cases (Office of Court Administration). An injury case is a civil case, so its last stop, if it gets that far, is the Supreme Court.

Below them are the courts of appeals. The state is organized into 15 districts, with a court of appeals in each (Government Code Section 22.201). Fourteen are regional: the First through the Fourteenth hear civil and criminal appeals from the district and county courts in their districts. Each court has between three and thirteen justices, and as of March 2025 the law authorized 85 justices across all of them (OCA).

The Fifteenth is new, and it is different. The Legislature created it in 2023, and its first term began on September 1, 2024 (Fifteenth Court of Appeals). Its district is every county in the state (Section 22.201(p)). It sits in Austin, and it has exclusive intermediate appellate jurisdiction over, among other matters, civil appeals brought by or against the state or a state agency, with exceptions the statute lists (Section 22.220(d)), and over appeals from the Business Court (Fifteenth Court). For its first three years the statute gives it a chief justice and two justices; a third justice is added in its fourth year and a fourth in its fifth (Section 22.216).

For an injury case between private parties, the road is simpler. Each court of appeals has appellate jurisdiction of the civil cases within its district (Section 22.220(a)), so the appeal goes to the court for the district where the case was tried.

Who Sits On Them

Texas elects its judges. Most are “elected in partisan elections by the qualified voters of the geographical areas they serve.” Appellate judges serve six-year terms, and when an appellate seat opens between elections, the Governor fills it by appointment. An appellate judge must be a citizen of the United States and of Texas, at least 35 years old, and licensed to practice law for at least ten years (OCA).

The Supreme Court is a chief justice and eight justices (OCA). Today it is Chief Justice Jimmy Blacklock and Justices Debra Lehrmann, the senior justice, in Place 3; James P. Sullivan, Place 2; John Phillip Devine, Place 4; Rebeca Aizpuru Huddle, Place 5; Jane Bland, Place 6; Kyle D. Hawkins, Place 7; Brett Busby, Place 8; and Evan A. Young, Place 9 (Supreme Court of Texas).

That bench changes next year. Four seats were on the November 3, 2026 ballot, the chief justice’s and Places 2, 7 and 8, and the four justices who held them lost (Texas Tribune; KSAT). Maggie Ellis, a justice of the Third Court of Appeals in Austin, was elected chief justice (Texas Tribune). Chari Kelly, who has served on the Third Court since 2019, won Place 2 for the rest of that seat’s term (KSAT; The Dallas Morning News; Third Court of Appeals). Kristen Hawkins won Place 7 from Justice Kyle D. Hawkins (no relation), and Gisela D. Triana won Place 8 (The Dallas Morning News). The other five justices were not on the ballot. The new members join the Court next year (The Dallas Morning News).

The courts of appeals are elected district by district, and the Fifteenth, whose district is the whole state, statewide. All three of its seats were on the November 2026 ballot, and all three changed hands (Texas Tribune). Part 2 introduces the seven justices of the Fourth Court and the one seat there that changed.

How A Case Climbs

The court of appeals. An ordinary civil appeal is taken from a final judgment of the district or county court (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 51.012). In the court of appeals, the case is assigned to a panel of three justices (Rule 41.1(a)). The panel reads the record and the briefs, may hear oral argument (Rule 39.1), and must hand down a written opinion that addresses “every issue raised and necessary to final disposition of the appeal” (Rule 47.1). When the issues are settled, the court should write a brief memorandum opinion; a fuller opinion is for a case that does more, such as establishing a new rule of law (Rule 47.4).

Rehearing. The losing side may file a motion for rehearing within 15 days after the court of appeals’ judgment, or, in the same window, ask the full court to reconsider the case en banc (Rule 49).

The Supreme Court. The next step is not automatic. A party asks for review with a petition, filed within 45 days after the court of appeals’ judgment or after its last ruling on a timely motion for rehearing or en banc reconsideration. The Court decides whether to take the case, and the petition has to explain why it should. Since January 1, 2026, a petition opens with an introduction that identifies “the most persuasive grounds for the Court to grant review,” each issue must cite the record to show it was preserved, and full briefs on the merits are filed only when the Court orders them (Supreme Court of Texas, Misc. Docket No. 25-9104). Because review is the Court’s choice, the court of appeals can be the last word.

Transfers. An appeal does not always stay home. One of the Supreme Court’s administrative jobs is moving cases among the courts of appeals “to obtain a more equal distribution of workload” (OCA), under a statute that lets it transfer cases for good cause (Government Code Section 73.001). Cases properly filed in the Fifteenth Court are shielded from those docket-equalizing transfers (Section 73.001(b)). And a court that receives a transferred case must decide it under the precedent of the court it came from (Rule 41.3), so the law of the home district travels with the case.

What It Means For An Injury Case

An appeal is not a second trial. There are no witnesses and no jury, only the record and the briefs. And the court of appeals will hear a complaint only if it was made in the trial court and the trial court ruled on it, or refused to rule and the party objected to the refusal (Rule 33.1; see Preserved). That is why the work of an appeal starts long before anyone files a notice of appeal. Part 3 shows what that means for one way an injury case can end without a trial: summary judgment.
