The Blackout

Leer este parte en español →

Sometimes the crash that looks like one driver’s plain mistake is something else. The driver is found slumped at the wheel, and the question is whether a stroke, a seizure or a faint took him before the car ever left its lane. When that is what happened, Texas law does not ask only what the driver did. It asks whether he could have seen it coming.

On Thursday night, in a crash reported at about 9:30, a vehicle going north on Aldine Westfield Road in Spring, in north Harris County, at Hirschfield Road, collided with a vehicle coming out of a parking lot. The northbound driver was given CPR at the scene and died at the hospital; the other driver came away with scratches and a fractured pelvis. Investigators said that the dead man’s injuries appeared inconsistent with the impact of the crash, which raises the possibility that a medical episode came first, and that the medical examiner’s findings will help settle it (KPRC). No one is named here, no agency was quoted by name, the cause is unresolved, and nothing on this page decides it. It is here for a pattern the Docket has not run: the driver who loses consciousness at the wheel, and whether anyone answers for the crash that follows.

A medical event. When a driver loses consciousness and a crash follows, the instinct on both sides is that no one is to blame. It was a medical event, an act of the body, nothing a lawyer can touch. That is half right. A driver who is suddenly stricken, and for that reason cannot control the car, is not negligent. But only when the loss of consciousness was truly unforeseen, and that one word does all the work.

Only if it was unforeseen. A Houston court of appeals put the rule in a single sentence in a case that began four days into a cross-country trip. A man towing a camper through Houston moved left toward stopped traffic. His wife looked over and found that his hands were in his lap, “his head was down, and he was completely unresponsive,” and in the seconds before the collision she “tried unsuccessfully to move Calvin’s foot off the gas pedal.” The truck or its camper struck at least six vehicles. At the hospital, doctors concluded that he “lost consciousness while driving because he had suffered a stroke.” The driver of one of the cars sued for negligence and negligence per se, and the court held that “unforeseeable loss of consciousness is a complete defense to the claim that a driver negligently caused a motor-vehicle accident” (Gomez v. Cooke), resting on a Houston decision from 1965. The court called the result an unavoidable accident, “an event not proximately caused by the negligence of any party to it” (Reinhart v. Young), one “that ordinary care and diligence could not have prevented, or one which could not have been foreseen or prevented by the exercise of reasonable precautions” (Gomez, quoting Otis Elevator Co. v. Shows). The court affirmed the summary judgment on the negligence claim and the negligence per se claim alike, on that one ground, which is why Negligence Per Se does not change the analysis here: a statute violated by an unconscious driver was not violated by a choice.

The word that carries the weight. Everything turns on unforeseeable. The driver in that case had had high blood pressure for at least ten years, and high blood pressure is a risk factor for stroke. It did not matter, because his physician had examined him eleven days before the crash, found the condition unchanged, rated his stroke risk as “minimal,” and “neither told Calvin that there was a risk of stroke nor foresaw a risk that Calvin would suffer a stroke and become incapacitated while driving”; far from warning him off the road, the doctor “told Calvin to bring back pictures.” The court also held that the driver had no duty to study his own condition: “Calvin is a layman, and determining the effect and foreseeable course of a medical condition is a matter for experts,” and he “is not required to know more than an expert, or to foresee what his treating physician could not” (Gomez). Turn each of those facts around and the defense fails. A prior episode. A diagnosed condition known to cause blackouts. A medication that carries that warning. A doctor who said not to drive. Feeling ill before turning the key. Any of them can make the collapse foreseeable, and a foreseeable collapse is not an unavoidable accident. It is negligence with a medical cause, and the case stands. Sudden Incapacity lays the rule out at length.

The state already draws that line for licensing. The Department of Public Safety “shall revoke the person’s license if the department determines that the person” “is incapable of safely operating a motor vehicle,” or has failed to provide medical records or undergo the examinations a panel of the medical advisory board requires (Transportation Code Section 521.294). For a commercial driver the line is federal and categorical: a person must not operate a commercial motor vehicle unless medically certified, and a driver with an “established medical history or clinical diagnosis of epilepsy,” or of any other condition likely to cause a loss of consciousness or of the ability to control the vehicle, is not physically qualified (49 CFR 391.41). Those files, the license record, the medical certificate, the doctor’s chart, are where foreseeability is proved or disproved.

A step past the emergency. This rule sits next to the one Sudden Emergency explains, where a conscious driver meets a danger not of his own making and the jury still measures whether his reaction was reasonable. A driver who blacks out does not react at all. The two rest on the same idea, that the law does not charge a person for a harm no ordinary care could have prevented, and both reach the jury as instructions rather than as separate questions, the shape The Swerve explained. But they turn on different facts. One asks whether a choice was reasonable. The other asks whether the collapse could have been seen coming.

The record decides. Because the driver often cannot say what happened, and sometimes does not survive to say it, the answer is in the record and not in the telling. The medical history and the prescriptions. The autopsy or the emergency-room workup that says whether a stroke or a cardiac event came before the impact or was caused by it, the question the medical examiner is being asked in the crash above, and the one Inquest follows through the system. And the car’s own recorder: the federal standard requires an event data recorder, where a vehicle carries one, to log the vehicle’s indicated speed and whether the service brake was on or off in the seconds before the event (49 CFR 563.7). A driver who was braking and steering was awake; the second and a half The Reaction describes is a conscious driver’s. A car that ran on at speed with no brake and no correction is consistent with a driver already gone. That record is the one Spoliation sends the letter for in the first week, because a module can be overwritten and a car can be scrapped. The count is not small: Texas officers tagged 3,762 units, each vehicle or pedestrian in a crash, with the factor the state prints as ILL (EXPLAIN IN NARRATIVE) in 2023, 65 of them in fatal crashes (TxDOT). The narrative that code points to is the first version of the story. The records are the rest of it.

Who is left to pay. If the collapse was unforeseeable, no one was negligent, and the claim against the driver and his liability insurer ends there. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage follows the same rule, because it protects insureds “who are legally entitled to recover from owners or operators of uninsured or underinsured motor vehicles” (Insurance Code Section 1952.101), and no one is entitled to recover for an unavoidable accident. What does not follow fault is personal injury protection, when the policy carries it: those benefits “are payable without regard to” “the fault or nonfault of the named insured or recipient in causing or contributing to the collision” (Section 1952.155), and they reach the necessary medical, hospital and funeral expenses and the lost income incurred within three years of the accident (Section 1952.151). If the collapse was foreseeable, the case stands, the driver’s share is set the way Comparative Fault describes, and the passenger in the stricken driver’s own car holds the same claim the other driver does.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a driver is said to have lost consciousness at the wheel, next to the page on the driver who reacts to an emergency and the page on the record that outlasts memory.
