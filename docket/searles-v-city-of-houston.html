The Fifth CircuitDecided · No. 25-20383

Searles v. City of Houston

COURT: U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, on appeal from the Southern District of Texas (Houston).
DECIDED: July 9, 2026, No. 25-20383 (Higginson, J., for a panel with Richman and Douglas, JJ.). Published. Affirmed summary judgment for the officer.
WHY IT MATTERS: The panel resolved the appeal only on whether the law was clearly established. It did not decide whether the shooting violated the Fourth Amendment, so the opinion adds no new answer to that question for the next case.

A man wanted on three felony warrants gets out of an SUV, turns back for a bag, and is shot once, before the officer finishes saying the word “hands.” The district court held the shooting reasonable and the law unsettled. The Fifth Circuit affirmed on the second ground and said nothing about the first.

The scene

On April 27, 2022, Houston police set out to arrest a man on three felony warrants: aggravated assault of a family member, felon in possession of a firearm, and evading arrest in a motor vehicle. Officers followed an SUV he had gotten into. After he got out, he “turned back and retrieved a closed bag from the car.” The officer drew his gun as he got out of his patrol car; one to two seconds later he began a command and fired “a single shot,” striking the man in the neck and killing him. The shot came, the court wrote, before the officer “finished uttering the word ‘hands.’” Most of the critical images on the officer’s body-worn camera, the court wrote, are “obstructed by” his arms. Officers later confirmed that a gray bag near the man held a firearm. The officer testified that the man “turned towards” him and another officer and that he saw “a ‘dark-colored object’” at the man’s waist that he believed was a firearm. The family did not dispute that the man held an object at his waist; it disputed the turn, arguing that he was turning to flee rather than to face the officers (Searles v. City of Houston).

The family and the man’s estate sued the officer, the former police chief and the City of Houston. The district court converted the motions to dismiss into motions for summary judgment, allowed limited discovery on immunity, and on the officer’s motion granted him qualified immunity on both grounds: the force was objectively reasonable, and no clearly established law said otherwise.

The holding

Qualified immunity asks two questions: did the officer violate a constitutional right, and was that right clearly established at the time? A court may answer either one first, and may stop after one. Here the panel wrote: “bound by our qualified immunity precedent, we affirm the district court on the basis that there is no clearly established law.” It explained the choice: “We resolve this case under the clearly established prong because of the intensely factual circumstances, which do not meaningfully assist the growth of the law.” It added that it did not discount how hard the first question was, or how much the answer weighed.

The standard it applied is a high one. Immunity is denied only when existing precedent puts the constitutional question “beyond debate,” and officers are entitled to immunity “unless existing precedent ‘squarely governs’ the specific facts at issue.” The family had to point to cases with “analogous or near-analogous facts.”

The cases that were not close enough

The family offered three Fifth Circuit decisions. In the first, from 1996, the parties disputed whether the man shot was unarmed, and three witnesses testified; here, the panel said, “the parties here do not dispute that [he] held an object by his waist.” In the second, the court’s 2019 en banc decision about a suicidal teenager with a gun to his own head, the young man was shot while facing away and without a warning; here the man knew the police were there, was not shot from behind, and was wanted on firearm-related warrants. In the third, from 2021, the dispute was whether the man was “visibly unarmed,” and he had no known criminal history. None of them was close enough: the family had “not raised authority that clearly establishes that [the officer’s] use of lethal force violated the law.”

The opinion decided only the officer’s appeal. In a footnote, noting that only the officer had moved for and won summary judgment, the panel wrote that “we make no determination with respect to the other defendants,” the former chief and the city.

Why it matters

A decision on the second question alone leaves the first question where it was. The next family whose case turns on similar facts will search for a precedent that squarely governs them and will not find this one, because this opinion did not say whether the force was lawful. That is how qualified immunity works when a court takes the second question first, and it is why the evidence in these cases is gathered second by second: the timing of the command and the shot, what each camera angle shows and hides, what the officer knew beforehand, and what was found afterward. For the Supreme Court’s 2025 rule that the force question looks at the whole encounter, see Barnes v. Felix; for how the Fifth Circuit has handled the two questions in other force cases, see Sligh v. City of Conroe and Immunity.
