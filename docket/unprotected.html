Unprotected

Leer esta página en español →

On Foot is about the person outside the vehicle who is standing on the ground. This page is about the person outside the vehicle who is sitting on a bicycle: a few feet higher, moving at ten or fifteen miles an hour, with a frame of aluminum under the body and nothing at all around it. The crash is decided by the same three things, the speed, the shape of the front end and the light, and by a fourth the person on foot does not have: the rider’s own motion, which carries the head higher and farther than a pedestrian’s before it comes down.

The numbers. “A total of 1,075 bicyclists were killed in crashes with motor vehicles in 2024, the third-highest number ever recorded,” and bicyclist deaths have “risen 73% since reaching their lowest point in 2010” (IIHS, Fatality Facts 2024: Bicyclists). The federal count is broader: its word is pedalcyclists, which takes in bicyclists and other cyclists, and since 2022 riders of motorized bicycles as well, and it says: “In 2024 there were 1,103 pedalcyclists killed in traffic crashes in the United States,” “a decrease of 6 percent from 1,173 in 2023,” while “an estimated 52,887 pedalcyclists were injured” (NHTSA, Bicyclists and Other Cyclists, 2024 data). Where and when is in both reports and they agree. “In 2024, 81% of bicyclists were killed in urban areas” (IIHS); “most pedalcyclist fatalities (81%) were in urban areas” (NHTSA). “Thirty-nine percent of bicyclist deaths in 2024 occurred at intersections” (IIHS), which is to say that six in ten did not; “Sixty percent of pedalcyclist fatalities occurred at locations that were not intersections” (NHTSA). The hour is the evening: deaths “peaked during the hours from 6 p.m. to 9 p.m. (23%)” (IIHS), and “More pedalcyclist fatalities occurred in the dark (52%) than in daylight (44%), dusk (3%), or dawn (1%)” (NHTSA). The rider killed is not a child: “the average age of pedalcyclists killed in traffic crashes was 48” (NHTSA).

The vehicle. “Light trucks were the most frequently involved vehicle in single-vehicle traffic crashes in which a pedalcyclist was killed,” 552 of 1,035 such crashes in 2024, and in 89 percent of those “the pedalcyclist was struck by the front of the light truck”; across every kind of vehicle, the front did the striking in 869 of the 1,035, 84 percent (NHTSA). Light trucks, in the federal vocabulary, are pickups, SUVs and vans, and the front of one is the tall, blunt shape On Foot describes with the Institute’s hood-height research: the car that takes a person’s legs and carries him on the hood, against the tall front that takes the torso and knocks the person down. A rider changes the geometry. Seated, his pelvis is already near the height of a car’s hood and his head is above it; a car’s front end takes the bicycle and the lower body and the rider’s upper body comes down on the hood, the windshield frame or the roof edge, and the tall front end that would take a pedestrian in the chest takes a seated rider there as well and throws him. The Institute states the result without the mechanism: “In a majority of bicyclist deaths, the most serious injuries are to the head” (IIHS). The Wrap and the Ground explains why the second impact, the pavement, is so often the one that kills, and a rider arrives at it from higher up and with his own speed added to the vehicle’s. Speed does to a rider what it does to a pedestrian, and the curve is the one on On Foot: the risk of death climbs steeply through the thirties and forties, which is why a city street is where most of these crashes happen and a highway shoulder is where the worst of them do.

The helmet. “Sixty-eight percent of bicyclists killed in 2024 were not wearing helmets” (IIHS). A helmet is built for the head and for a fall, and the number says what it says. It does not say what put the rider on the ground. The Left Turn puts the helmet question for motorcyclists where it belongs, and the same place holds here: whether a rider wore one goes to how badly he was hurt, and the question of whether the driver should have seen him and left him room is a different question, answered by the driver’s speed, his lights and his attention, none of which the helmet changes.

The dark. More than half of these deaths happen in the dark, and the evening peak is the hour the sun goes down for most of the year. Past the Light does the arithmetic of low beams: about two hundred feet of road, which at highway speed is less than the distance a car needs to stop, so that a dark shape appears at the edge of the light with no time left. A bicycle is a narrower shape than a person on foot and a faster one, and a rider in dark clothing with no rear lamp is, to the driver behind him, a dark object until he is not. Looking, Not Seeing explains the other half of the problem, the driver who looked and did not see because a small shape far away does not register as something to brake for, and Conspicuity explains why the law answers that problem with lamps and reflectors it can count. Texas counts them for bicycles too, below.

What the law asks. The first rule gives the rider the road: “A person operating a bicycle has the rights and duties applicable to a driver operating a vehicle under this subtitle,” unless the chapter changes a rule or a rule by its nature cannot apply to a bicycle (Transportation Code Section 551.101). A bicycle is traffic, and the driver overtaking it owes it what he owes any slower vehicle. The second rule is where the fight usually is. A rider who “is moving slower than the other traffic on the roadway shall ride as near as practicable to the right curb or edge of the roadway, unless” one of the listed conditions holds: passing, preparing a left turn, something on the road that makes the edge unsafe, or an outside lane that is “less than 14 feet in width and does not have a designated bicycle lane adjacent to that lane” or is “too narrow for a bicycle and a motor vehicle to safely travel side by side,” in which case the rider may take the lane; and “Persons operating bicycles on a roadway may ride two abreast,” in a single lane on a laned road and without impeding “the normal and reasonable flow of traffic on the roadway” (Section 551.103). Most city lanes are narrower than fourteen feet. The third rule is the one for the dark: a bicycle ridden at night must carry “a lamp on the front of the bicycle that emits a white light visible from a distance of at least 500 feet” and, at the rear, a red reflector of an approved type or “a lamp that emits a red light visible from a distance of 500 feet to the rear of the bicycle” (Section 551.104). And the driver’s own rule does not change because the person ahead is on two wheels: an operator “may not drive at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” and shall control speed “as necessary to avoid colliding with another person or vehicle that is on or entering the highway in compliance with law and the duty of each person to use due care” (Section 545.351). When the rider made a mistake, no lamp, the wrong side of the road, a stop sign run, Texas weighs it and divides rather than ends: a claimant may not recover “if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001), and below that line the court reduces the damages “by a percentage equal to the claimant’s percentage of responsibility” (Section 33.012) and the case goes on, which is Comparative Fault. I Never Saw Them is the sentence the driver says afterward, and the law’s answer to it, and that answer is the same for a rider as for a walker: the question is not whether he saw the person but whether he was driving so that he could.

The record. The speed is in the vehicle’s event data recorder and in the marks. The point of impact is on the bicycle, in the bent fork or the crushed rear wheel, and on the vehicle, in the dent at the height where the body met it; the height says car or truck, and the place on the front says whether the rider was ahead or alongside. The lamps are a matter of what was on the bicycle and whether they were lit, which the first photographs at the scene decide and later ones cannot. The light is a matter of the time, the weather and the streetlights. The injuries say the sequence: a head wound at windshield height says the rider came over the hood; a crushed chest or pelvis and injuries from the wheels say a tall front end that knocked him down. The driver’s phone says whether he was looking. The first letter asks for the recorder, the camera, the phone records and the vehicle before any of it is repaired (Spoliation), and for the bicycle itself, which the family should not let anyone take away. Nothing here is about any one crash; what happened on a particular street is for its record to say.
