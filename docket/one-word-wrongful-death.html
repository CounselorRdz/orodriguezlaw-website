Wrongful Death

The law splits a death into two claims, and knowing which is which is the difference between a family made whole and a family half-paid.

When someone is killed by another's fault, Texas gives the family not one lawsuit but two, running side by side. The first is the wrongful-death claim, and it belongs to the living: the surviving spouse, the children, the parents. It is for their loss, the support that stopped coming, the companionship that will not come again, the grief they carry forward.

The second is the survival claim, and it belongs to the person who died, brought by the estate on his behalf. It is for what he endured before he passed: the conscious pain, the fear of the moments he understood, the medical bills, the burial. In plain terms, the wrongful-death claim asks what the family lost; the survival claim asks what he suffered.

Why it matters that they are separate: the beneficiaries are set by statute, spouse, children, parents, and no one else, so a devoted sibling, or the grandmother who actually raised the child, may have no standing to bring the wrongful-death half at all, however real their grief. And the two claims recover different things, so pleading only one leaves money, and justice, on the table.

The clock is the same and it is short. Both generally run on the two-year statute of limitations, and the proof a death case needs, the vehicle, the scene, the records, ages out faster than that. The first duty is preservation, the same day.

The move: treat a fatal case as two cases from day one. Identify every statutory beneficiary, plead both the family's loss and the decedent's suffering, and lock down the evidence before it is gone. A death is not one number. It is two questions, and the family deserves an answer to both.

One caution. This is the shape of the law, not advice on any case; who may recover, and what a claim is worth, turn entirely on the facts.

The Texas Wrongful Death Act and survival statute are Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 71 (wrongful death, § 71.002; beneficiaries, § 71.004; survival, § 71.021). This is general information, not legal advice.
