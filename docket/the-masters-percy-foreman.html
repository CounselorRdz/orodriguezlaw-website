Percy Foreman

Percy Foreman defended something like fifteen hundred people facing the electric chair, and only one of them was ever executed. Sit with that number. It is not luck, and it is not a single technicality repeated a thousand times. It is what happens when a lawyer decides that the most hated person in the courthouse is exactly the person the Constitution was written for, and then spends fifty years proving it.

He was born in small-town East Texas in 1902, took his law degree from the University of Texas in the 1920s, and built his practice in Houston into the most feared criminal defense operation in the state. He was a big man with a big voice and a bigger presence, and he took the cases nobody else wanted, the ones the public had already decided, the ones where the client walked in with the whole world against him. Over a career that ran more than half a century, he handled around fifteen hundred capital cases. He lost fifty-three. Only one client ever went to the chair.

That record rests on a conviction he never apologized for. A defense lawyer does not have to believe his client is innocent, or even likable. His job is to make the state prove its case, every element of it, to twelve people, beyond a reasonable doubt, and to stand between one human being and the full power of a government trying to cage or kill him. Foreman understood that this is not a loophole in the system. It is the system. The harder a client is to defend, the more the principle matters, because a right that protects only the sympathetic is not a right at all.

His method followed from that. He famously tried to put everyone but his client on trial, the victim, the police, the investigation, the state's own witnesses, the sloppy work, because reasonable doubt does not require proving your man did not do it. It requires showing the state has not proved that he did. He attacked a prosecution from every angle at once and was relentless about it, on the theory that a case is a chain and you only have to break one link. It is the same instinct Racehorse Haynes ran on in the same era: find every independent reason the state loses, and never bet the case on just one.

The cases that made him famous are still studied. He won an acquittal for Candace Mossler in a murder trial the whole country had written off. He represented Jack Ruby for a few days before they parted ways. And in his most famous and most second-guessed case, he represented James Earl Ray, charged with the assassination of Dr. Martin Luther King. On Foreman's advice Ray pleaded guilty to avoid the death penalty and took a ninety-nine-year sentence, and Ray later claimed he had been pressured into it, which Foreman denied. It is a hard chapter, and it belongs in any honest account of him, because taking the most reviled defendant in the country was exactly the work he believed in, and it is also where the weight of that work shows.

He was famous, too, for his fees, which were enormous, and for a courtroom manner that filled the room. But the show was never the substance. Behind it was a lawyer who understood evidence, who knew a jury, and who had thought harder than almost anyone alive about what the burden of proof actually means when a life is on the line. The theater got the attention. The preparation won the cases.

For a trial lawyer, Foreman is a lesson in nerve. He took the cases that looked lost, and he took them because someone had to, and he made the state earn every conviction it got. Whatever side of the docket you work, that is the discipline to steal from him: make the other side prove it, test every link in the chain, and never let the unpopularity of a position stand in for the strength of the proof against it. That is not a defense lawyer's trick. It is what a fair fight requires.

He died in Houston in 1988, past eighty, still the standard by which Texas measures a criminal defense lawyer. The number is his monument, fifteen hundred defended and one lost to the executioner, but the number is really just proof of the idea behind it. Everyone gets a defense. Foreman gave it to them for fifty years, and he gave it his whole strength, and that is why they still say his name in Texas courtrooms.

Percy Foreman (1902 to 1988), the Houston criminal defense lawyer regarded as one of the greatest trial advocates in Texas history, who over a fifty-year career defended roughly fifteen hundred capital cases and lost only one client to execution, among them the acquittal of Candace Mossler and the representation of James Earl Ray. Considered here for the craft, and for the principle that everyone is entitled to a defense. This is general information, not legal advice.
