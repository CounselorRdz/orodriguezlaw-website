Retrograde Extrapolation

Leer esta página en español →

A blood or breath test almost never happens at the moment of the crash. It happens an hour later, or three, after the scene, the arrest, the ride, the warrant. Retrograde extrapolation is the attempt to work back from that later number to the one that mattered. Texas’s highest criminal court has said it can be done, and then listed what a court should weigh before trusting it.

Retrograde extrapolation. In the court’s words, “Retrograde extrapolation is the computation back in time of the blood-alcohol level”: an estimate, from a test taken later, of what the level was when the person was driving (Mata v. State). It matters because one of Texas’s two definitions of intoxicated is a number. A person is intoxicated if he does not have “the normal use of mental or physical faculties” because of alcohol, a drug or another substance, or if he is “having an alcohol concentration of 0.08 or more” (Penal Code Section 49.01). The number is about the body at the wheel. The test is about the body at the hospital.

The curve. The court laid out the science in a few sentences. “As alcohol is consumed, it passes from the stomach and intestines into the blood, a process referred to as absorption.” “At some point after drinking has ceased, the person’s BAC will reach a peak.” “After the peak, the BAC will begin to fall as alcohol is eliminated from the person’s body,” and “The body eliminates alcohol through the liver at a slow but consistent rate” (Mata). The federal alcohol institute adds the part people forget: “BAC can continue to rise even when a person stops drinking or is unconscious,” because “Alcohol in the stomach and intestine continues to enter the bloodstream” (NIAAA). So a later test can read higher than the level at the wheel, or lower, depending on where on the curve each moment fell. “A reading from a single breath test will not reflect where the person is on his BAC curve” (Mata).

The rate. Working backward means assuming a rate, and the rate is not the same for everyone. In Mata itself the State’s expert testified that “the elimination rate could range from .018 to .025,” and the court, describing a scientific article it relied on, wrote that “the actual individual range is generally between .01 and .02” (Mata). A small difference in the rate, multiplied by several hours, is a large difference in the answer.

What the court looks for. “We believe that the science of retrograde extrapolation can be reliable in a given case,” the court wrote, and then put the weight on the person doing it: “The expert’s ability to apply the science and explain it with clarity to the court is a paramount consideration.” He “must demonstrate an awareness of the subtleties of the science and the risks inherent in any extrapolation.” The court named what bears on whether an estimate can be trusted: the length of time between the offense and the tests, how many tests there were and how far apart, and whether, and how far, the expert knew the person’s own characteristics, which the court said might include “the person’s weight and gender,” “the person’s typical drinking pattern and tolerance for alcohol,” “how much the person had to drink on the day or night in question,” “what the person drank, the duration of the drinking spree,” and “the time of the last drink, and how much and what the person had to eat” “either before, during, or after the drinking.” More than one test helps: “multiple tests will increase the ability to plot a subject’s BAC curve.” In Mata the court held that the trial court had abused its discretion by admitting the extrapolation, because the State had not shown by clear and convincing evidence that it was reliable, and it sent the case back for a decision on harm (Mata).

In a civil case. Mata is a criminal case, and the number it governs is the State’s. The same science comes up when a family sues the driver for gross negligence, or the bar that served him (Who Served Him). The same questions follow the number into that courtroom: when the drinking started and stopped, what was eaten, when the blood was drawn, and whether there was one test or two. Those facts are evidence too, and they go stale: the receipts, the video, the hospital’s draw time (Spoliation). The test itself, and the company’s duty to run its own, is in The Test He Refused.

A number from the hospital at two in the morning is a fact about two in the morning. Moving it back to the crash takes the facts the court listed, and the fewer the tests, the more of those facts it takes. Without enough of them, it is a guess with a decimal point.
