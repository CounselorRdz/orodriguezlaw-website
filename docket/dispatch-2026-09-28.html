Rolled

Leer este parte en español →

Most crashes are decided between two drivers. A rollover is different. The moment a vehicle leaves its wheels and begins to turn over, the other car is often already out of the story, and the question becomes what the vehicle itself did next: whether it stayed upright, whether its roof held, and whether it kept the people inside. It is the one crash that puts the vehicle on trial.

The count. The federal safety agency calls a rollover “one of the most dangerous forms of crashes among passenger vehicles.” In 2023, 6,596 people riding in passenger vehicles were killed in vehicles that rolled over, 28 percent of all the passenger-vehicle occupants who died that year. The danger is not spread evenly. Among the occupants killed, the share who died in a vehicle that rolled was 38 percent in pickups, 33 percent in SUVs, 25 percent in vans, and 20 percent in passenger cars (NHTSA).

Why the tall ones go over. Whether a sliding vehicle keeps sliding or trips and rolls comes down to a fraction. When the federal agency built its rollover rating for new cars, it started from one number, the static stability factor: “one-half the track width of a vehicle divided by the height of its center of gravity” (Federal Register, 2001). Wide and low, and a skid stays a skid. Tall and narrow, and the same skid can catch a rut, a soft shoulder or a curb and put the vehicle on its roof. It is the same fraction that decides when a loaded tanker goes over, which is what High and Heavy is about. Speed makes all of it worse, because the energy that has to go somewhere climbs with the square of the speed, which is what Closing Speed measures.

Inside the roll. Once the vehicle is turning, two things decide whether the people inside survive. The first is whether they stay inside: an unbelted occupant is thrown against the roof and the glass and, too often, out, which is why Thrown Clear treats keeping a person in as the belt’s first job. The second is whether the space around them holds. If the roof comes down toward the seats, the survival space closes, and what reaches the body is the load The Crush describes. The Roll sets out the physics of the turns themselves: not one impact, but a series of them.

What the law does with it. Texas requires the belt, and going without it can be weighed against a person’s recovery, though it does not end the case, which is Comparative Fault (Transportation Code Section 545.413). But a rollover also asks the vehicle’s own questions, and federal law answers two of them. Every new car, SUV, pickup and van rated at 10,000 pounds or less and built since September 1, 2011 has had to carry electronic stability control, a system whose stated purpose is to reduce the deaths and injuries from crashes “in which the driver loses directional control of the vehicle, including those resulting in vehicle rollover” (Standard No. 126). And the roof standard was rewritten to double the force a roof must withstand in the federal test, from 1.5 to 3.0 times the vehicle’s unloaded weight for vehicles rated at 6,000 pounds or less, with a new requirement that the roof keep the occupant’s headroom, the survival space, during the test (Standard No. 216a). Once a vehicle is over, the case asks whether it made the injury worse than it had to be, which is exactly what Crashworthiness means, and the injuries that follow a roof coming down, to the spine and the brain, are what the serious-injury pages are about. One honest caution: a driver’s own speed and steering are in the mix too, and Texas divides the fault among everyone who owed a duty.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the numbers say about the vehicles on Texas roads, next to the pages that explain why the crash that turns a car onto its roof is the one that asks what the car was built to do.
