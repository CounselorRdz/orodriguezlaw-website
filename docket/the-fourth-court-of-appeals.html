The Fourth Court of Appeals

Leer esta página en español →

Part 2 of 3. Thirty-two counties, seven justices, one building in San Antonio. A civil case tried anywhere from the Hill Country to the Rio Grande in this part of Texas is appealed here, unless it belongs to the Fifteenth Court or the Supreme Court sends it somewhere else.

Part 1 of this series, The Texas Courts of Appeals, mapped the whole system. This part zooms in on one court of appeals, the way the Fifth Circuit guide does for that court: what it is, who sits on it, and how it works.

What It Is

The Fourth Court of Appeals is one of the fourteen regional courts of appeals. Its district is 32 counties: Atascosa, Bandera, Bexar, Brooks, Dimmit, Duval, Edwards, Frio, Gillespie, Guadalupe, Jim Hogg, Jim Wells, Karnes, Kendall, Kerr, Kimble, Kinney, LaSalle, McMullen, Mason, Maverick, Medina, Menard, Real, Starr, Sutton, Uvalde, Val Verde, Webb, Wilson, Zapata and Zavala (Government Code Section 22.201(e)). It hears civil and criminal appeals from the district and county courts of those counties (Section 22.220(a); OCA).

The Court sits in the Cadena-Reeves Justice Center, 300 Dolorosa, in San Antonio (Fourth Court of Appeals). By statute it is a chief justice and six justices (Section 22.216(d)). It had three justices until 1981, when the number increased to seven (Court history).

Who Sits On It

The seven members serve staggered six-year terms (San Antonio Report). As of November 2026, they are:

Chief Justice Rebeca Martinez, Place 1, elected to the Court in 2012 and elected chief justice in 2020 (Fourth Court).
Justice Velia J. Meza, Place 2, who took office on January 1, 2025.
Justice H. Todd McCray, Place 3.
Justice Lori Massey Brissette, Place 4, appointed by the Governor effective July 8, 2024.
Justice Adrian A. Spears II, Place 5.
Justice Irene Rios, Place 6, elected to the Court in 2016.
Justice Lori I. Valenzuela, Place 7, appointed in January 2021 (Fourth Court).

One seat was on the November 3, 2026 ballot: chief justice (San Antonio Report). Chief Justice Martinez did not seek reelection and has announced her retirement at the end of her current term (Fourth Court; San Antonio Report). With every precinct reporting, the returns show Antonia “Toni” Arteaga with the most votes for the seat (KSAT; Decision Desk HQ). The other six justices were not on the ballot.

How It Decides

Like every court of appeals, the Fourth Court decides cases in panels of three justices (Rule 41.1(a)). A party who wants oral argument asks for it on the front cover of its brief (Rule 39.7), and the court may decide that argument is unnecessary, for instance because the facts and legal arguments are adequately presented in the briefs and record (Rule 39.1). The opinion must address every issue raised and necessary to decide the appeal (Rule 47.1). The losing side then has 15 days to ask the panel for rehearing or the full court for reconsideration en banc (Rule 49).

One thing the county map does not show: a case tried in the Fourth District can be decided somewhere else. The Supreme Court transfers appeals among the courts of appeals to even out their workloads (OCA; Government Code Section 73.001). When an appeal from the Fourth District is transferred, the court that receives it must decide it under the Fourth Court’s precedent (Rule 41.3). And an appeal can arrive here from another district the same way, bringing that district’s precedent with it.

How To Read It

Three habits help before you file in this Court or read one of its opinions. Know the panel: unless the full court votes to hear it en banc, three justices decide the case, not seven. Know the form: a memorandum opinion is what the rule calls for when the issues are settled, and a fuller opinion is reserved for cases that do more, such as establishing a new rule of law (Rule 47.4). And know where the case came from: if it was transferred in, the precedent that governs is the home court’s (Rule 41.3).
