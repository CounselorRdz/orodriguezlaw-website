David Boies

Leer esta página en español →

David Boies did not learn to read until the third grade, and he is still a slow reader. He calls his dyslexia “a difference, not a disability,” and his career has been an argument for that sentence. He is here for his rule about facts.

He grew up, in his words, “in a small farming community in northern Illinois in the 1940s.” His firm’s biography lists the University of Redlands, a bachelor’s degree from Northwestern in 1964, a law degree magna cum laude from Yale in 1966 and a master’s degree in law from New York University in 1967. He joined Cravath, Swaine & Moore, and he was a partner there, in his thirties, when he learned he was dyslexic. In 1997 he left Cravath and, with Jonathan Schiller, started the firm now called Boies Schiller Flexner. He chaired it until 2024 and is now its founding partner and chairman emeritus. He is a Fellow of the American College of Trial Lawyers.

The rule first. “You cannot intuit the facts,” he told The American Lawyer in 2024. “You have to just spend the time investigating, learning, probing, refining.” Preparation, in his telling, means “understanding which of those facts are important and how they are important.” Before a deposition, he told Northwestern’s alumni magazine in 2000, the work is to “learn as much as I can about the case and industry and the people involved,” and then to “just go in and have a dialogue with them.”

Then the narrative. In an interview for the Academy of Achievement he put it in six words: “Control the facts, control the narrative.” The narratives compete, he says, and each side picks one that favors its case. “But you must be sure that the narrative is true.” A story the lawyer cannot prove will not survive cross-examination or the documents, and the jury may stop trusting the lawyer who told it. Because no lawyer controls all the evidence at a trial, the work is to “package, mold, present that information in a way that supports the narrative that you’re presenting.”

Then the difference. “It’s an input difference,” he told Law360 in 2018. “It’s not a processing difference.” Reading slowly, he says, “puts a premium on selecting what I read and what I focus on.” Law360 described a lawyer who “rarely relies on notes,” and in his own words he has “always been somebody who, for example, tends to talk without notes.” The Yale Center for Dyslexia and Creativity quotes him more simply: “Life is not a timed examination.”

Why he is here. Because an injury case is won on the records the other side assumed nobody would read closely: the driver’s hours in the logbook, the speed and the braking stored in the event data recorder (Delta-V), the maintenance file, the safety manual. Because a deposition goes best when the lawyer knows the industry and the people well enough to simply talk with the witness. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Ted Wells and Morgan Chu. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Spend the time, because you cannot intuit the facts. Tell a story that is true. And choose what you read with care, because no one reads everything.

A timed test measures speed. A trial measures something else: who knows the facts, and who can be trusted with them.
