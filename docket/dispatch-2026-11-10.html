Something in the Road

Leer este parte en español →

There is a sentence a driver says after striking a person on a bicycle from behind at night and driving on: I thought I hit something in the road. It may be an honest sentence. It is never the end of the question, because when a collision is reasonably likely to have hurt someone, Texas law does not let the driver be the judge of what he struck.

The pattern. On a dark road a person on a bicycle is a narrow shape moving the same direction as the traffic behind, and the crash this page is about has three parts: the driver does not see the rider in time, the front of the car takes the rider from behind, and the driver keeps going, for a block or for good. The federal count sketches the setting. “In 2024 there were 1,103 pedalcyclists killed in traffic crashes in the United States,” and “More pedalcyclist fatalities occurred in the dark (52%) than in daylight (44%), dusk (3%), or dawn (1%)”; “Sixty percent of pedalcyclist fatalities occurred at locations that were not intersections”; and of the 1,035 riders killed in single-vehicle crashes that began with the vehicle striking the rider, the front of the vehicle did the striking for 869 (NHTSA, Bicyclists and Other Cyclists, 2024 data). The report does not sort those deaths by who was overtaking whom. This page names no one, is pegged to no crash and decides nothing about any of them. It is about the geometry and the duty. Unprotected is what a vehicle does to a person on a bicycle; Gone and Render Aid are what a driver owes after.

Why the rider is seen late. When two things move the same way, what brings them together is only the difference between their speeds, the point Slow Moving made with a tractor. A car at 45 coming up on a rider at 12 is closing at 33. That is the mercy in the geometry and also the trap: the shape ahead grows slowly, and nothing about it says emergency until it is close. What the driver has to see it by is the headlamps. “The low-beam illumination of headlights evaluated by IIHS ranges from 125 feet to 460 feet,” and the Institute reported in October 2021 that since its ratings began in 2016 the average for all the headlights it tested “rose from less than 180 feet to more than 200 feet” (IIHS). Take 200 feet of light and the allowances Past the Light uses: a second and a half to see and react, the window The Reaction describes, and then hard braking on dry pavement. At 45 the driver must first see the rider from about 125 feet back to come down to the rider’s speed before reaching him, and has that. At 60 he needs about 216, and does not: the gap he needs is longer than the light. Texas answers the dark from the rider’s side. A bicycle ridden at night must carry “a lamp on the front of the bicycle that emits a white light visible from a distance of at least 500 feet in front of the bicycle,” and at the rear either an approved red reflector “visible when directly in front of lawful upper beams of motor vehicle headlamps from all distances from 50 to 300 feet” or “a lamp that emits a red light visible from a distance of 500 feet to the rear of the bicycle” (Transportation Code Section 551.104). Read the reflector rule closely. It is written for upper beams, and it ends at 300 feet. A reflector returns the light it is given; a lamp makes its own. That difference can be the first fact in the case.

What the body meets. The front of a car takes the bicycle and the lower body, and the rider’s upper body comes down on the hood and the frame of the windshield before it goes to the road, the sequence Unprotected and The Hood Line describe. How hard is a question of speed, and the American numbers this page relies on are for people on foot: “The average risk of death for a pedestrian reaches 10% at an impact speed of 23 mph, 25% at 32 mph, 50% at 42 mph, 75% at 50 mph, and 90% at 58 mph” (AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety). That curve was drawn for pedestrians, not riders, and a rider struck from behind is struck at the difference between the two speeds, not at the number on the speedometer. But it says what a few miles an hour are worth: between 23 and 42, the risk goes from one in ten to one in two.

What the law asks. Three things, in the order they happen.

The pass. “A person operating a bicycle has the rights and duties applicable to a driver operating a vehicle,” with exceptions the chapter spells out (Section 551.101), so the driver coming up behind a rider owes what he owes any slower vehicle ahead. The passing rule says he “shall pass to the left of the other vehicle at a safe distance” and “may not move back to the right side of the roadway until safely clear of the passed vehicle” (Section 545.053). The state statute does not count the feet. It says safe, and a jury measures it. The speed rule points the same way: a driver must “control the speed of the vehicle as necessary to avoid colliding with another person or vehicle that is on or entering the highway in compliance with law and the duty of each person to use due care” (Section 545.351).

The rider. The rider’s own rules are in the case too, and they can bear on how the fault is divided. The lamp and the reflector, above. The place in the lane: a rider moving slower than traffic “shall ride as near as practicable to the right curb or edge of the roadway,” unless, among other things, the outside lane is “less than 14 feet in width and does not have a designated bicycle lane adjacent to that lane” or is “too narrow for a bicycle and a motor vehicle to safely travel side by side” (Section 551.103), in which case the rule does not hold him to the edge. A broken equipment rule is evidence of the standard of care, not a verdict; what a statute violation does and does not prove is the subject of Negligence Per Se. And Texas divides responsibility by percentage: a claimant recovers nothing “if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001), and below that line the award is reduced by the claimant’s share (Section 33.012; Comparative Fault).

The stop. Then the sentence the driver said. Feeling an impact is a reason to stop. It is not leave to keep driving while deciding what it was. The operator of a vehicle in a collision “that results or is reasonably likely to result in injury to or death of a person” shall “immediately stop the vehicle at the scene of the collision or as close to the scene as possible,” “immediately return to the scene of the collision if the vehicle is not stopped at the scene of the collision,” “immediately determine whether a person is involved in the collision, and if a person is involved in the collision, whether that person requires aid,” and stay until the duty to give information and help is met (Section 550.021). The third duty is the answer to I hit something: the law makes the driver find out. Leaving is a crime by itself, “a felony of the second degree” when the collision resulted in a death and “a felony of the third degree” when it resulted in serious bodily injury. In the civil case it is three things at once: evidence a jury may weigh (Flight), a harm of its own when the minutes without help mattered, and, when the driver is never found, the reason the claim turns to the rider’s own uninsured motorist coverage, all of which Render Aid walks through.

What the proof looks like. The car keeps the record the driver did not. The damage low on the front and across the hood and the glass, the paint and fabric that crossed from one to the other, and the parts left on the road, a lens, a mirror, a piece of grille, which matter most when the car itself is gone (Render Aid). The bicycle: whether it carried a lamp, whether the lamp was switched on, where the reflector was and whether it faced the rear. The point of impact in the lane, from the gouges and the debris, measured before traffic and rain move them. The headlamps of the car, their aim and their condition. The cameras on the businesses along the road, and the time stamps on the 911 calls, which fix how long the rider lay there. The driver’s phone. None of it waits (Spoliation).

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any one crash. A dispatch keeps the record of a pattern. Slow Moving said the danger of a slow thing ahead is the difference between two speeds. Here the slow thing ahead is a person. The driver feels the proof. The law’s question is what he owes in the instant he feels it, and the answer is written down: stop, go back, and find out.
