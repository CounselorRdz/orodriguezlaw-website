The First Hour: The Crash

Leer esta página en español →

Part 1 of 3. The first hour after a crash holds evidence that will never be this good again. Much of it is gone by morning. Safety and medical care come first; this page is about what comes right after.

What the law requires. A driver in a crash that results, or is reasonably likely to result, in injury or death must stop, go back if he did not stop, find out whether anyone needs aid, and stay until he has done what Section 550.023 requires (Section 550.021). He must give his name and address, the registration number of the vehicle and the name of his liability insurer, show his driver’s license if asked and it is available, and give an injured person reasonable assistance, including taking the person, or arranging a ride, to a doctor or hospital “if it is apparent that treatment is necessary, or if the injured person requests the transportation” (Section 550.023). Render Aid explains what follows when a driver does not.

The people. A federal guide for police says “a witness’ memory of an event can be fragile,” tells officers to separate witnesses, and says to plan the interview “as soon as the witness is physically and emotionally capable” (National Institute of Justice, 1999). The bystander who saw the light, the driver in the next lane, what the other driver said first: all of it is most reliable now. A name and a phone number, written down, outlast the scene.

The road. Tire marks and debris show where the vehicles met and how they moved, and some of it does not last: “Yaw marks are short-lived evidence and may disappear within a few days with heavy traffic on the roadway” (APRI, 2003). Photographs taken before the vehicles are moved, and after, keep what the road will not.

The vehicles. A vehicle with an event data recorder keeps a short record of the seconds before the crash, and that record goes wherever the vehicle goes: the tow yard, the body shop, the salvage auction (No Witnesses). The first hour is when to learn where each vehicle is being taken.

The body. Not every injury announces itself at the scene. A head injury can leave a person talking and walking before it shows (The Lucid Interval). Getting checked does two things: it treats the injury, and it makes the first medical record of it.

The paper. The officer’s crash report comes later. It is where to start looking, not the answer (Read the Report).

The first hour belongs to safety and to the people who are hurt. Whatever can be written down, photographed or kept in that hour is worth more than anything found later.

Tex. Transp. Code § 550.021 and § 550.023, as posted on texas.public.law (verified May 26, 2025); National Institute of Justice, Eyewitness Evidence: A Guide for Law Enforcement (NCJ 178240, October 1999); John Kwasnoski, Crash Reconstruction Basics for Prosecutors (American Prosecutors Research Institute, March 2003).
