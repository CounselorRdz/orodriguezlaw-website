On Foot

Leer este parte en español →

Texas counted 772 people killed on foot in 2024. A person who is walking has no hood to crush and no cabin to ride inside; the body is the crumple zone. Whether a case belongs to the driver or to the person who stepped off the curb turns on one question that closes far more slowly than the ticket does: who owed the other the yield. Here is the pattern the newsroom reported this week, next to the pages that explain the injury and the law.

Friday, before dawn, near the Texas Medical Center. Houston police said a car heading west on the cross street had a green light when it struck a person walking in the marked crosswalk on Fannin Street. That person was taken to a hospital and pronounced dead. The driver stayed at the scene, showed no signs of intoxication, and the case was referred to a grand jury for review, as reported by KPRC and ABC13. No one here is named.

The backdrop. The state keeps the count. The Texas Department of Transportation recorded 772 pedestrians killed in 2024, out of 6,095 crashes that hurt someone on foot, and another 1,455 seriously injured. People on foot are in about one percent of Texas crashes and nearly nineteen percent of the deaths. TxDOT’s Be Safe. Drive Smart. campaign names four causes, and two are the same failure from opposite sides of the crosswalk: a pedestrian who does not yield, and a driver who does not. The other two are inattention and speed. These deaths climb in the fall and winter, when the evening commute moves into the dark, as reported by KSAT.

What the pattern says. A person struck by a car is not hit once. The bumper takes the legs, the hood and windshield take the torso and head, and the pavement takes what is left, and it is usually that last impact, the ground, that does the killing. That is the whole of The Wrap and the Ground. And the driver who says he never saw the person until it was too late is often telling the plain truth, because a brain can look straight at something in the road and not register it in time to stop, which is what Looking, Not Seeing is about. A body on foot carries none of the structure that saves a driver, so the margin a car spends folding, a person spends on the ground.

What the law does with it. Texas gives a pedestrian the right of way in a crosswalk, tells a pedestrian crossing elsewhere to yield, and tells every driver, green light or not, to use due care to avoid striking a person on foot (Transportation Code Chapter 552). So the principle runs both ways: right of way opens the case, it does not close it, which is why Failure to Yield is about who owed the yield and not who held the green. And where a person crossed outside a crosswalk, or against the signal, that does not end the claim; under Comparative Fault the share divides the recovery rather than erasing it, until it passes half. Sorting out who owed whom, and where the person was walking, is the work; the car accidents and serious personal injury pages, with their Before You Call companion, cover how.

No one in this crash is named here, and nothing on this page assigns fault in an open investigation. A dispatch keeps the record of what happened on Texas roads this week, from the outlets that were there, next to the pages that explain it.
