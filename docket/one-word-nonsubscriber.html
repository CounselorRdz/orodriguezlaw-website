Nonsubscriber

Leer esta página en español →

Texas lets a private employer decide not to carry workers’ compensation. The law has a word for that employer: nonsubscriber. The word decides which door an injured worker walks through.

The choice. The Texas Department of Insurance puts it in one line: “Texas employers, except for public entities, can choose whether to provide” workers’ compensation coverage (TDI). An employer that carries it gets a shield. For a covered employee, comp benefits are “the exclusive remedy” against the employer for a work injury, with one exception written into the statute: the surviving spouse or heirs of the body of a worker whose death was caused by the employer’s intentional act or omission or by its gross negligence may recover exemplary damages (Section 408.001). An employer that does not carry it gives up the shield, and more.

What the employer loses. In a suit by an injured worker, a nonsubscriber may not argue that the worker was guilty of contributory negligence, that the worker assumed the risk, or that a fellow employee’s negligence caused the injury (Section 406.033(a)). The Texas Supreme Court held in 2000 that a nonsubscribing employer “is not entitled to a jury question” on the injured employee’s comparative responsibility (Kroger Co. v. Keng, Tex. 2000). What the employer keeps is narrow: the defense that the worker meant to bring about the injury, or was intoxicated when it happened (Section 406.033(c)).

What the worker must prove. The trade is not a free pass. In that suit “the plaintiff must prove negligence of the employer” or of an agent or servant of the employer acting within the general scope of the job (Section 406.033(d)). Losing three defenses is not the same as losing the case. The employer can still argue it was not negligent at all, or that it had no duty to warn of a danger that was open and obvious or already known to the worker; the Texas Supreme Court held in 2015 that this no-duty rule is not one of the defenses the statute takes away (Austin v. Kroger Texas, Tex. 2015).

The paper in the first days. Some nonsubscribers ask an injured worker to sign a waiver of the right to sue. A waiver signed before the injury is “void and unenforceable” (Section 406.033(e)). A waiver signed after the injury holds only if all of these are true: the worker signed it voluntarily and knowing what it does; it was signed “not earlier than the 10th business day after the date of the initial report of injury”; the worker first had “a medical evaluation from a nonemergency care doctor”; and it is a writing that states the parties’ true intent, with the waiver terms conspicuous on the face of the agreement (Section 406.033(f), (g)). A paper signed at the hospital the day after the injury does not meet that test.

How to tell. A nonsubscriber has to say so. Unless its employees are exempt from coverage, it must file DWC Form-005 with the state between February 1 and April 30 each year, and within 30 days of hiring its first employee; post written notice at the workplace “in English, Spanish, and any other language that is appropriate”; and give written notice to new employees (TDI). Look for the posted notice, and ask the employer in writing.

Other companies. Whether or not the employer carries comp, another company whose negligence helped cause the injury can usually be sued, unless it provided the worker’s comp coverage and so stands in the employer’s place (Third-Party Claims; Statutory Employer).

Nonsubscriber is one word that changes three things: who can be sued, what the employer can argue, and what a worker should not sign before the tenth business day after reporting the injury.
