The Gas You Stop Smelling

Leer esta página en español →

Hydrogen sulfide gives a warning almost anyone recognizes, the smell of rotten eggs, and then takes it back. At the levels that matter most, the nose can stop reporting the gas while the gas is still there.

The gas. The federal medical guideline for doctors describes “a characteristic rotten-egg odor” and a gas “slightly heavier than air” that “may accumulate in enclosed, poorly ventilated, and low-lying areas” (ATSDR). Sometimes it is made on the spot by chemistry: phosphorus pentasulfide, a solid, “reacts with water to generate the highly toxic and flammable gas hydrogen sulfide” (NOAA CAMEO Chemicals), and the federal pocket guide lists water first among the substances it is incompatible with (NIOSH). “Workers are primarily exposed to hydrogen sulfide by breathing it” (OSHA).

The smell that quits. OSHA’s own table puts the odor threshold between 0.01 and 1.5 parts per million, the point “when rotten egg smell is first noticeable to some.” At 100 parts per million it lists “loss of smell after 2-15 minutes (olfactory fatigue),” and from 100 to 150, “Loss of smell (olfactory fatigue or paralysis)” (OSHA). At high concentrations, the agency says, the ability to smell the gas “can be lost instantly (olfactory paralysis),” so its instruction begins in capitals: “DO NOT rely on your sense of smell” (OSHA). The guideline for doctors says the same thing from the patient’s side: “If the rotten egg odor is no longer noticeable, it may not necessarily mean that exposure has stopped” (ATSDR). A smell that goes away is not good news by itself.

What it does inside. The guideline gives the mechanism in a line: the gas can cause “inhibition of the cytochrome oxidase enzyme system,” “resulting in lack of oxygen use in the cells” (ATSDR). In plainer words, the cells cannot use oxygen properly. At 2 to 5 parts per million, OSHA says, prolonged exposure may cause nausea, tearing of the eyes and headaches; at 50 to 100 parts per million, “Slight conjunctivitis (‘gas eye’) and respiratory tract irritation after 1 hour.” At high levels the guideline’s sentence is short: “only a few breaths can lead to immediate loss of consciousness, coma, respiratory paralysis, seizures, and death” (ATSDR). OSHA’s table calls it “knockdown,” an “immediate collapse within 1 to 2 breaths,” and the agency notes that knockdown “often results in falls that can seriously injure the worker” (OSHA).

The injury that waits. Not all of the harm comes at once. The guideline warns that at higher concentrations fluid in the lungs “may be immediate or delayed for up to 72 hours,” and that “patients seriously exposed by inhalation should be monitored for 24 hours” (ATSDR), the same delayed pattern Hours Later describes for other gases. “Survivors of severe exposure may develop psychological disturbances and permanent damage to the brain and heart” (ATSDR), and OSHA adds that some people who breathed enough of it to become unconscious “continue to have headaches and poor attention span, memory, and motor function after waking up” (OSHA). The guideline is also fair the other way: “A single small exposure from which a person recovers quickly is not likely to cause delayed or long-term effects.” Both sentences are true. The record decides which one describes a particular person.

The second victim. “Fatalities have occurred to rescuers entering the hot zone” (ATSDR). The guideline calls for positive-pressure self-contained breathing apparatus and says rescuers “should have a safety line during rescue operations because of the extremely rapid toxic action of hydrogen sulfide.” OSHA puts it plainly: “First responders must be trained and properly protected before entering areas with elevated levels of hydrogen sulfide” (OSHA). The coworker who goes in after a fallen friend without air is the pattern these warnings are written against.

The lines the law draws. For workers, OSHA lists a general-industry ceiling of 20 parts per million and a peak of 50 “(up to 10 minutes if no other exposure during shift),” and a construction limit of 10 parts per million over eight hours; NIOSH recommends a ten-minute ceiling of 10 and puts the level immediately dangerous to life or health at 100 (OSHA; NIOSH). Where the gas may be present, OSHA’s guidance tells employers to “Conduct air monitoring prior to and at regular times during any work activity where hydrogen sulfide exposure is possible,” and in a confined space to “Test (monitor) the air in the space continuously during work operation” (OSHA). A broken rule is evidence of the standard of care, not a verdict by itself (Negligence Per Se). For the worker inside the fence, the claim usually runs through workers’ compensation, and through the courts against an employer without it or against a third party; when a worker is killed, Texas law also leaves room for the surviving spouse or the heirs of the body to seek exemplary damages when the death was caused by the employer’s intentional act or omission or by its gross negligence (Labor Code Section 408.001; On the Job). For the family outside the fence, the question is dose (The Dose).

Where the proof is. Not in a test for the gas itself. The guideline’s sheet for patients says that “Specific tests for the presence of hydrogen sulfide in blood and urine generally are not useful to the doctor” (ATSDR). After a severe exposure, the same sheet says, blood and urine tests “may show whether the brain, nerves, heart, or kidneys have been injured,” which is proof of the injury, not of the dose. So the proof of the exposure is the air and the clock: the readings from fixed and personal monitors, each with a place and a time; the permit for the space and the air test that went with it; the minute a person collapsed or a symptom began; the 24 hours of observation the guideline calls for after a serious exposure, and the chart that records them; and, for anyone who smelled the gas and then stopped smelling it, when that happened, written down while it is fresh. Most of that is in the employer’s or the plant’s hands, and it does not keep itself (Spoliation).

The smell is the first warning, not the last one. When it goes away, the question is not whether the gas left. It is whether the nose did.
