Empty

Leer esta página en español →

To a driver, empty means the load is off. To the federal hazardous materials rules, an empty tank is still carrying what it carried last, until it is cleaned and purged.

What the rules say. The rules do not take the word at face value. Except as the section otherwise provides, an empty packaging “containing only the residue of a hazardous material” must be offered and transported “in the same manner as when it previously contained a greater quantity” of it (49 C.F.R. 173.29). For a tank truck the rule is concrete. A bulk packaging that must be placarded when it holds a hazardous material “must remain placarded when it is emptied,” unless it fits one of a few narrow exceptions, the first being that it is “sufficiently cleaned of residue and purged of vapors to remove any potential hazard” (49 C.F.R. 172.514). The exception names what an emptied tank still holds: residue, and vapor.

Why. Gasoline vapor burns in air only inside a band: from 1.4 percent to 7.6 percent of the air, by volume, by the figures of the federal occupational safety institute (NIOSH). Below the band the mixture is too lean to ignite; above it, too rich. And gasoline is always making vapor. Its flash point is far below zero: 45 degrees below zero Fahrenheit by the institute’s count, 36 below by NOAA’s. The vapor is heavy, too. NOAA’s chemical database warns that it “is heavier than air and may travel considerable distance to a source of ignition and flash back” (NOAA, CAMEO Chemicals). No one standing beside an emptied tank can see which side of the band the inside is on. The rules do not ask anyone to guess.

What the record shows. The federal Chemical Safety Board has investigated deaths during welding and other hot work in and around tanks, and the same fact keeps appearing: people did not know what the tank still held. As the bulletin puts it, “in all cases, the workers had no knowledge that an explosive amount of flammable vapor had accumulated.” In one case, “The workers, however, were unaware that the tank contained residual hydrocarbon liquid and vapor from an unknown prior use.” In another, “residual hydrocarbon vapor from the 2,500-gallon rear compartment ignited” (CSB). The bulletin’s subtitle is its lesson: “Effective Hazard Assessment and Use of Combustible Gas Monitoring Will Save Lives.”

In a case. Empty is a word to test, not to accept. When an emptied tanker is in a crash, or a worker is hurt cleaning, repairing or welding on a tank, the questions are concrete. What did it carry last? When was it unloaded? Was it cleaned and purged, by whom, and does any record say so? Was it still placarded? The answers come from records like the shipping papers for the last load, the cleaning records, any permit for the work, and the placards themselves. The same kind of band, for natural gas, is the arithmetic behind Odorant; what happens when a truck’s own fuel is set loose is in Saddle Tanks.

An empty tank is the one people stop being careful around. The rules treat it as loaded until it has been cleaned and purged, and a case asks whether it was.

49 C.F.R. 173.29 and 49 C.F.R. 172.514 (Cornell LII); NIOSH Pocket Guide to Chemical Hazards, Gasoline; NOAA, CAMEO Chemicals, Gasoline; U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board, Seven Key Lessons to Prevent Worker Deaths During Hot Work In and Around Tanks, Safety Bulletin No. 2009-01-SB (February 2010).
