The Yellow Bus

Leer este parte en español →

Everyone knows the yellow bus and the red arm that swings out beside it. Almost no one treats that arm as what it is: not a courtesy, but a command the state backs with a criminal charge. School is in, and the few seconds that arm governs are among the most dangerous a child spends all day. Here is the pattern as the newsrooms reported it, next to the pages that explain it.

Tuesday, September 8, a little after seven in the morning. A Northside ISD bus carrying eighteen elementary students was in a crash at Alamo Ranch Parkway and Lone Star Parkway on the far west side of San Antonio. The district said no one aboard was hurt. It was not the district’s first this fall: on August 20, two of its buses were in separate crashes the same morning, one of them carrying forty-seven children, and again no one was hurt. No injuries either time, and together they are the pattern: buses full of children back on the road at everyone else’s hours, as reported by KSAT.

The backdrop. The Texas Department of Transportation counted 877 crashes in Texas school zones in 2025, twelve percent more than the year before, and 2,358 crashes involving school buses the same year. Distracted driving and speeding were the most common contributing factors on that list. The TxDOT Back to School page carries the numbers and the plea behind them.

What the pattern says. A child on foot near a school is the most exposed person in traffic, and not for being careless. A child is shorter than the hood that hides him, quicker off a curb, slower to judge a closing car, and hurt in ways an adult is not, because a child is not a small adult; that is the whole of The Body at 13. The driver’s half is Looking, Not Seeing: the eye that stares straight at a stopped truck and misses it will slide right past a small figure at the road’s edge.

What the law does with it. A school-zone speed limit and the red stop arm on a bus are not etiquette. They are safety statutes, each written to protect one class of people, children, from one kind of harm, being struck near a school. Break a rule built that precisely and it is not merely evidence of carelessness. It is the carelessness. That is negligence per se, and Negligence Per Se explains how an unexcused violation of such a statute becomes the breach itself, so the fight moves to causation, not blame. Texas requires a driver to stop before a bus with its signals on and not move until the bus does, with fines that climb on a second offense and a Class A misdemeanor if someone is seriously hurt; it also bars a handheld phone in an active school crossing zone. Failure to Yield sits beside all of it, and the car accidents and serious injury pages, with Before You Call, cover how a case for an injured child is worked.

No child, family, or driver from these crashes is named here, and nothing on this page assigns fault in an open investigation.
