Herbert Wachtell

Leer esta página en español →

Herbert Wachtell’s name comes first on the door of the firm he helped found in 1965, and he came to it as the litigator. He is here for a quieter skill than a closing argument: he knew the rules of the courthouse well enough to write the book on them.

He graduated from New York University in 1952, Phi Beta Kappa, and from its law school in 1954, Order of the Coif, in the first class of Root-Tilden Scholars. He was an editor of the NYU Law Review and took a master’s degree in law at Harvard in 1955. From 1955 to 1957 he was an assistant United States attorney for the Southern District of New York. Before the firm existed he had already struck out on his own as a litigator, and three NYU graduates at another firm, Martin Lipton, Leonard Rosen and George Katz, had been sending him their litigation. When their firm broke up in 1964, the four of them, joined by Jerome Kern, opened their own in January 1965. All of the original lawyers, including two young associates, had gone to NYU Law. His firm’s biography lists him as a fellow of the American College of Trial Lawyers and of the American Bar Foundation, and as a member of the American Law Institute.

The partnership with Lipton started with an edit. Recalling how he met Lipton, Wachtell told the NYU Law magazine: “I remember a tall, skinny guy who wrote a Law Review piece that I proceeded to edit.” Lipton’s version is that their friendship “got off to a rocky start” when Wachtell took his first Law Review note and completely rewrote it.

Then the book. Wachtell wrote New York Practice Under the CPLR, published by the Practising Law Institute in six editions, from 1963 to 1986. The CPLR is New York’s Civil Practice Law and Rules, the rules for how a lawsuit moves through New York’s courts, from the papers that start it to the deadlines that can end it. A trial lawyer who has written that book does not learn the rules from the other side’s motion. He reads the motion and knows what it left out.

Why he is here. Because cases are lost on procedure before anyone hears the facts: the claim never pleaded, the deadline missed, the defendant not served before the time ran out, the case filed in the wrong place (see Forum). Texas has its own book of rules and its own statutes on where a case may be filed, and the lawyer who knows them cold wins fights the other side did not know it was in. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside John Quinn and Morgan Chu. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Edit hard, even the work of a future partner. Know the rules better than the lawyer across the table, well enough that you could write them down. And be the lawyer other lawyers send their cases to; that is how his firm began.

Knowing the rules rarely makes a lawyer famous. It keeps the lawyer in the case until the trial starts.
