The Crossing

Leer este parte en español →

A vehicle and a train meet at a rural crossing. The train could not have stopped; a loaded freight at highway speed needs something like a mile. So the case is never about who had the right of way. It is about the crossing itself, and a federal rule decides which questions a jury ever gets to hear.

The pattern. A pickup and a train reach the same place at the same time on a county road, and the train always has the right of way, because it cannot do anything else. The federal rail agency says that “Nationally, more than 2,000 incidents and 200 fatalities at grade crossings occur each year,” and that a grade-crossing collision “is the second leading cause of rail-related deaths in the United States” (FRA, Highway-Rail Grade Crossing Safety). The agency’s own counts, as compiled by Operation Lifesaver, put 2024 at 2,268 collisions, 259 deaths and 779 injuries, and 2025, still preliminary, at 2,274, 284 and 773 (Operation Lifesaver, Collisions, Fatalities and Injuries by Year). This page names no one, is pegged to no crossing, and decides nothing about any of them. It is here because the questions a family wants to ask after a crossing wreck are not all open to them, and knowing which ones are is the whole case. The Grade Crossing is the physics; Ungated and The Track are the earlier chapters.

What the federal line closes. Two of the most natural claims run into a federal wall, and the Supreme Court built it in two cases. The first is the speed claim. In 1993 the Court read the federal rule that sets a top speed for each class of track and held that it “should be understood as covering the subject matter of train speed with respect to track conditions,” “including the conditions posed by grade crossings,” so that federal law pre-empts a negligence action “only insofar as it asserts” “that petitioner’s train was traveling at an excessive speed.” The train there was doing between 32 and 50 miles an hour on track “for which the maximum speed is 60 miles per hour,” and the claim that it should have been going slower did not survive, though the Court said in a footnote that the case “does not present, and we do not address,” whether a claim that the crew should have slowed or stopped “to avoid a specific, individual hazard” survives. The same opinion left the family’s other claim alive, because the plan to put gates at that crossing had been shelved and the money sent elsewhere: “These facts do not establish that federal funds ‘participate[d] in the installation of the [warning] devices’” there, and so that claim “is not pre-empted” (CSX Transportation v. Easterwood). The second case closed that door where the money did arrive. In 2000 the Court held that the federal regulations “‘cover the subject matter’ of the adequacy of warning devices” “installed with the participation of federal funds,” and so “establish a federal standard for the adequacy of those devices” “that displaces state tort law addressing the same subject.” The crossing there had crossbucks and advance signs put in with federal money, and the court said the rest did not matter: “Whether the State should have originally installed different or additional devices,” “or whether conditions at the crossing have since changed” “such that automatic gates and flashing lights would be appropriate,” “is immaterial to the pre-emption question” (Norfolk Southern Railway v. Shanklin). So the first question in the warning-device case is not whether the crossing needed gates. It is who paid for what is standing there now, and that is a matter of record.

What stays open. Congress added a clarification in 2007 that keeps three doors open. “Nothing in this section shall be construed to preempt an action under State law seeking damages” “for personal injury, death, or property damage alleging that a party” “has failed to comply with the Federal standard of care established by a regulation or order issued” by the Secretary of Transportation “covering the subject matter as provided in subsection (a) of this section,” or “has failed to comply with its own plan, rule, or standard that it created” “pursuant to a regulation or order issued by either of the Secretaries; or” “has failed to comply with a State law, regulation, or order that is not incompatible with subsection (a)(2)” (49 U.S.C. Section 20106). A railroad that broke the federal rule, or its own, can be sued for it. And where the Shanklin rule does not reach, at a private crossing like the one in the 2024 case below, or at a public one whose devices federal money did not install, the condition of the crossing itself is Texas ground. The Texas Supreme Court restated the rule in 2024: “A railroad crossing is extra hazardous when, because of surrounding conditions, it” “is so dangerous that persons using ordinary care cannot pass over it in safety” “without some warning other than the usual [crossbuck] sign,” and at such a crossing “the railroad company must” “‘take extra safety measures to protect those using the crossing.’” The court listed what counts, “visual obstructions like trees and buildings,” “the volume of train and vehicular traffic, the angle of the intersection,” “the grade and curvature of the road leading to the crossing, and any history of similar accidents at the crossing,” and then set the bar where it sits: “The standard for categorizing a railroad crossing as extrahazardous is high,” and a crossing qualifies “only if such factors establish that a reasonably prudent person” “exercising ordinary care could not safely use it without extraordinary warning devices.” In that case, a private crossing with a crossbuck and a stop sign where a pickup driver was killed, the court held the family’s evidence “legally insufficient to support” “a finding that this crossing was extra-hazardous” and reinstated judgment for the railroad (Union Pacific Railroad v. Prado). The door is open; it is narrow; and it is proved with the crossing’s own conditions, not with the word dangerous.

The driver’s duty. It is still in the case, and it divides the fault rather than ending it. A driver “approaching a railroad grade crossing shall stop not closer than 15 feet or farther than 50 feet” “from the nearest rail if,” among other triggers, “a clearly visible railroad signal warns of the approach of a railroad train or other on-track equipment,” if “a crossing gate is lowered, or a flagger warns of the approach or passage of a train or other on-track equipment,” or if “an approaching railroad train or other on-track equipment is plainly visible to the operator” “and is in hazardous proximity to the crossing”; at a crossing with only crossbucks the driver “shall yield the right-of-way to a train or other on-track equipment in hazardous proximity to the crossing” “and proceed at a speed that is reasonable for the existing conditions”; and driving around a closed gate is an offense (Transportation Code Section 545.251). A broken traffic rule is evidence of negligence and not a verdict on it (Negligence Per Se), and Texas weighs the driver’s share against the railroad’s: a claimant recovers nothing “if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001) and below that line the award is reduced “by a percentage equal to the claimant’s percentage of responsibility” (Section 33.012), which is Comparative Fault. Failure to Yield is the entry on why the right of way is a starting point and not a result.

What the law asks. The answer is in the crossing, and the crossing keeps records. The paper that says who installed the devices and whose money paid for them, which the Shanklin rule makes the first question. The locomotive’s event recorder and the horn data, which The Track explains the federal horn rule produces. The sightlines and the brush, measured and photographed before anyone clears them; the angle of the approach; the history of other collisions at that crossing, which Texas counts only when the circumstances were similar. The driver’s own speed and stop, in the vehicle’s recorder. All of it changes fast, and most of it belongs to the railroad, which is why the first letter goes out before the brush is cut (Spoliation). A crossing wreck is not decided by who had the right of way, because the train always did. It is decided by what the crossing was, and that was written down before the train arrived.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any one crossing. A dispatch keeps the record of a rule most families learn only after the fact: the train was going too fast and there should have been gates are the two sentences a crossing case usually cannot say, and the case that can be won is written in the records of the crossing itself.
