Low-Side, High-Side

Leer esta página en español →

A motorcycle that loses its grip goes down one of two ways. It slides out from under the rider and falls on its low side, and he goes with it, or the tire catches again and the bike snaps upright and throws him over the high side. Riders use the two phrases the way surgeons use the names of fractures: the words say what broke, and how.

The budget. A tire has one budget of grip, and everything the rider asks of it draws on the same account: leaning, braking, accelerating. The manual most state licensing agencies hand to new riders says it plainly: “Any time a motorcycle is leaned over, the amount of traction available for braking is reduced. The greater the lean angle, the more the possibility of the tires losing traction” (Motorcycle Safety Foundation). Gravel, sand, a wet patch or a line of diesel on a curve shrinks the budget without warning, and the surfaces the manual lists as poor traction, gravel roads, the places “where sand and gravel collect,” wet pavement, are the surfaces a rider meets while leaned over.

The low side. When a tire asks for more grip than the surface has, it lets go. The lean that was holding the bike up has nothing under it, and the motorcycle falls toward the inside of the turn, its low side, and slides. The rider usually slides with it, often with the bike on his leg for the first yards, then on his own, hands and hips and knees against the pavement until friction ends it. The physics is forgiving in one way: if nothing stops the slide, his speed bleeds off along the ground instead of into a single blow. The injuries follow the geometry. Among motorcyclists treated at the country’s level I and level II trauma centers, injuries to the lower extremities were the most common of all, in an estimated 47 percent of the patients, and fractures of the tibia and fibula, the two bones of the lower leg, were about 95 percent of the leg injuries (NHTSA).

The high side. The other fall starts the same way and ends worse. Under hard braking or too much throttle the rear tire slides out, and the motorcycle begins to turn sideways under the rider. If the tire then regains its grip, because the rider let go of the brake or the slide reached clean pavement, the bike stops sliding before the rider stops moving. It snaps upright and over, and the rider is thrown across the top of it, the high side, into the air ahead of the machine, often head and shoulder first, with the motorcycle tumbling behind him. The manual’s warning is specific: “A skidding rear tire is a dangerous condition that can result in a violent crash and serious injury or death.” Once the rear wheel locks, “your ability to change direction is lost,” and the brake must be released to regain control, but “if the rear wheel is out of alignment with the front, there is a risk of a high-side crash” (MSF). A front wheel that locks gives less time and a simpler rule: “release the front brake immediately and completely,” then reapply it smoothly. The high side is the fall most likely to produce the injuries a helmet exists for, which is what The Left Turn says a helmet buys, and the one in which a thrown rider lands wherever the throw puts him, which is what Thrown Clear describes for a body in the air.

Why he could not steer out of it. Above walking speed a motorcycle turns by leaning, and it leans by being steered, for an instant, the other way: press the left handgrip, lean left, go left (MSF). The instinct of a frightened rider runs the other direction: to steer away from the thing he is afraid of, which stands the bike up and runs it wide, and to grab the brakes hard while leaned over, the one bill the traction budget cannot pay in full. The manual’s answer to a stop that has to happen in a curve is to get the motorcycle upright first, “as perpendicular to the road as possible, then brake,” and if there is no room for that, to brake “smoothly and gradually” with less force than on a straight. Its answer to a curve taken too fast is to slow down before the curve, and its description of the pattern is blunt: “Riders often try to take curves or turns too fast. When they can’t hold the turn, they end up crossing into another lane of traffic or going off the road. Or, they overreact and brake too hard, causing a skid and loss of control.” The Cage explains what is missing around the rider once that happens.

The count. The federal study that reconstructed 351 motorcycle crashes in Orange County, California found that left-turn scenarios were the most common crash configuration, “followed by falling to avoid crash and running off the roadway,” and that single-vehicle crashes were 23.8 percent of its cases (FHWA). In 2024, 2,125 riders died in single-vehicle crashes nationally, 40 percent of them alcohol-impaired, and 24 percent of the motorcycles in fatal crashes hit a fixed object, against 16 percent of passenger cars (NHTSA). The manual’s own numbers on who falls: “Most riders are riding slower than 30 mph when a crash occurs,” and “More than half of all crashes involve riders with less than five months of experience on their motorcycle” (MSF).

Where the law comes in. A rider who went down alone is the case Single-Vehicle describes, and the two falls say where to look. A low side asks what took the traction away: the gravel left after a chip seal, the diesel at a stop line, the edge of the pavement, the car that never touched him. A high side asks what made him brake that hard: the car that pulled out, the debris, the dog. Texas does not end the case because no one else was hit, and whether he wore a helmet goes to the injuries, not to the cause (the exemption in Transportation Code Section 661.003, and the Nabors rule The Left Turn walks through). What answers those questions is the surface the day of the crash, the tires and the bike as it lies, the scrape marks on the pavement that reconstructionists read like handwriting, and the first photographs, which is why Spoliation starts the morning after.

A low side is a fall. A high side is a throw. The road decides which one a rider gets, and it keeps the evidence of why for about as long as it takes to sweep it.
