Who Hired Them

Leer este parte en español →

A family loses someone to an eighteen-wheeler, and the company that owned the truck turns out to be a name on a door, with one or two trucks and the least insurance the law allows. The question that decides the case is not only who was driving. It is who put that truck on the road.

The pattern. A loaded tractor-trailer crosses into traffic on a Texas highway, and a person in a smaller vehicle does not survive. The driver worked for a carrier the family had never heard of. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The chain. Freight moves through more hands than the side of a trailer shows. A shipper hires a carrier. The carrier may pass the load, through a broker, to a second carrier, and the second may hire a third, through a broker of its own, which supplies the truck and the driver. That is not a hypothetical. It is the chain the Fifth Circuit described this year in a Texas case about a tractor-trailer that jackknifed, crossed into oncoming traffic and killed the motorist in the vehicle it struck (Crane v. Penske). For years a broker’s answer to a claim like that was that it did not drive the truck or own it, and that federal law preempted any state claim about the carrier it chose. Some courts agreed, including the trial court in that case.

The exception. The federal statute that bars the states from enacting or enforcing laws related to the prices, routes and services of motor carriers and brokers, which the courts call the FAAAA, has a safety exception: that bar “shall not restrict the safety regulatory authority of a State with respect to motor vehicles” (49 U.S.C. 14501(c)(2)(A)). On May 14, 2026, in Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, the Supreme Court reversed a federal appeals court in an opinion by Justice Barrett for a unanimous Court (Supreme Court docket). In August the Fifth Circuit read the decision this way: “the Supreme Court ruled that the FAAAA’s safety exception to preemption applied to negligent-hiring claims against brokers.” It reversed the trial court and sent the claim against the broker back (Fifth Circuit opinion). It did not decide whether the broker owed a duty under Texas law; that question went back to the trial court, which had not reached it.

The search, one link further up. This is the search that Trucking Company Liability already runs, carried one more link. The driver answers for the driving. The company he drove for answers for his driving on the job (Respondeat Superior), and for trusting him with the truck if it knew or should have known he was unlicensed, incompetent or reckless (Negligent Entrustment). In Crane the court also held that the carrier the shipper hired, which had taken control and responsibility for the truck under the federal leasing rules, was the driver’s statutory employer, though his paycheck came from a company further down the chain (Statutory Employer). And after Montgomery, federal law no longer blocks a claim that a broker chose a carrier carelessly; the claim in Crane was that the broker hired a carrier without first checking its qualifications as a motor carrier. Whether Texas law puts that duty on a broker is the open question Crane sent back to the trial court.

The shares. Naming the broker does not let anyone else out. Texas has the jury assign a percentage of responsibility to the injured person, to each defendant, to each settling person and to each responsible third party (Section 33.003). A defendant found more than 50 percent responsible is jointly and severally liable for the damages the claimant can recover; one at 50 percent or less generally pays only its own percentage (Section 33.013). When the carrier at the bottom of the chain cannot pay, its share goes unpaid unless another company in the chain is found more than 50 percent responsible or answers for the driver as his employer. That is why the rest of the chain matters.

What lasts. The proof is paper, and the companies hold it: the load confirmation, the contracts and leases between the carriers, the broker’s file on what it checked before it handed over the freight, and the driver’s hours in the logbook. Those records are the case, and they are easiest to get before anyone has a reason to tidy them (Spoliation).

A truck crash is a chain, not a collision. Ask who was driving. Then ask who hired him. Then ask who hired them.
