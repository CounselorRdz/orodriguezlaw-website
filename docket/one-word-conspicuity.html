Conspicuity

Leer esta página en español →

At night a trailer is only as visible as the light it carries or gives back. The law counts the lamps and measures the tape, and when a rider or a driver goes into the back of one in the dark, the first question is not how fast he was going. It is what there was to see.

Conspicuity. The engineers’ word for how well a vehicle announces itself to the eye, and in federal law the name of a particular thing: the strips of red and white reflective sheeting that run along the sides and across the back of a big trailer. Texas writes the federal lighting standard into its own code. Lighting and reflective equipment on a vehicle “must comply with” either “the current federal standards in 49 C.F.R. Section 571.108” or “the federal standards in that section in effect, if any, at the time the vehicle or motor vehicle was manufactured” (Transportation Code Section 547.3215). That standard, No. 108, requires a conspicuity system on “Each trailer of 2032 mm or more in overall width, and with a GVWR over 10,000 lbs.” (trailers built for living or office use excepted): retroreflective sheeting “of no less than 50 mm wide,” two inches, alternating red and white, along each side, where it “need not be continuous as long as not less than half the length of the trailer is covered,” and “applied across the full width of the trailer” at the rear, where it is mounted “not less than 375 mm and not more than 1525 mm above the road surface at the strip centerline,” about 15 to 60 inches, with the side strips as close to that band as practicable, and two pairs of white 300-millimeter strips at the upper rear corners that draw the top of the box against the dark (49 CFR 571.108). The trucking rules reach back to the old fleet: trailers “manufactured prior to December 1, 1993, which have an overall width of 2,032 mm (80 inches) or more” and a rated weight of 10,001 pounds or more had “until June 1, 2001, to comply,” with the strips in the same places, their centerline “between 375 mm (15 inches) and 1,525 mm (60 inches) above the road surface” (49 CFR 393.13). Over the tape sit the lamps, among them the ones every commercial trailer must carry: two red tail lamps, two red stop lamps, two red rear reflex reflectors, two rear turn signals, a license plate lamp, and on the wide ones three red identification lamps and two red clearance lamps that trace the trailer’s outline (49 CFR 393.11, Table 1).

The number. The government measured the tape after it required it. The agency’s 2001 study found that the tape “reduces side and rear impacts into heavy trailers by 29 percent” in the dark, with the largest effect, an estimated 41 percent, where the road was dark and unlit; that “Impacts resulting in fatal or nonfatal injuries to at least one driver are reduced by 44 percent”; that it “did not significantly reduce crashes during daylight”; and that once every heavy trailer carries it, “the tape will be saving an estimated 191 to 350 lives per year” (NHTSA, Morgan, 2001). Two inches of tape is the difference between a shape at the edge of the headlights and a shape that announces itself at a distance that leaves time to brake. What happens when it does not is the geometry Underride describes, a rear frame at chest height meeting a hood built to crush far lower, and the arithmetic Past the Light sets out: low beams that reach somewhere between 125 and 460 feet, against a vehicle covering 103 feet a second at 70.

The small trailer. The federal tape rule stops at 80 inches of width and 10,000 pounds of rated weight. The trailer behind a pickup, the landscaper’s rig, the boat on its carrier: the tape is not required, and what the law asks of them is the lamps. A trailer on a Texas road must carry at least two taillamps showing a red light plainly visible from 1,000 feet behind it, and in a combination “only the taillamps on the rearmost vehicle are required to emit a light” at that distance, which is to say the trailer’s lamps are the ones that count, because the truck’s are hidden behind it (Section 547.322). It must be equipped “with at least two red reflectors on the rear of the vehicle,” each “mounted at a height from 15 to 60 inches” and visible at night “from 100 to 600 feet when directly in front of lawful lower beams of headlamps” (Section 547.325). And every required lamp must be lit “at nighttime,” which the code defines as “the period beginning one-half hour after sunset and ending one-half hour before sunrise” (Section 541.401), and “when light is insufficient or atmospheric conditions are unfavorable” enough that “a person or vehicle on the highway is not clearly discernible at a distance of 1,000 feet ahead” (Section 547.302). A trailer plug that was never connected, a lamp burned out, a reflector painted over or hidden behind a load: each is a statute broken.

The news. Saturday, October 17, about 1:49 a.m., in the 12700 block of Interstate 35 North in San Antonio, a motorcyclist struck the back of a trailer and fell from the motorcycle; he was pronounced dead at the scene. “Witnesses told police the motorcyclist was driving at a high rate of speed when he attempted to switch into the right lane.” The trailer’s driver remained at the scene; “Police said there are no criminal elements to the crash,” and “The crash remains under investigation” (KSAT). The report does not say what the trailer was, what was pulling it, whether its lamps were lit, or whether it carried tape. Nothing here decides that crash. But the report is a model of the pattern: the first fact anyone records is the rider’s speed, and the trailer is a word.

No criminal elements. That phrase settles what the police will do, not what the law says about the lamps. A civil case runs on Chapter 33, the subject of Comparative Fault: the jury assigns each person a percentage of the responsibility. A rider’s speed goes on one side of the ledger. A trailer running dark, or a big one running without its tape, goes on the other, and because the lighting statutes were written to protect exactly the people behind the trailer, breaking one is, unless excused, negligence in itself, the rule Negligence Per Se explains. A claimant found more than half responsible recovers nothing; one found half or less recovers his damages reduced by his share. Where the rider died, the claim belongs to his family (Wrongful Death). The evidence is the trailer itself: its lamps, its wiring and its plug, its reflectors, its tape or the bare steel where tape should be, its registration and its inspection, and the photographs the officers took that night before it was hooked back up and driven home, which is why the first letter in these cases asks that it not be (Spoliation).

Why the word matters. Conspicuity moves the question from the rider’s speedometer to what there was to see, and the law has already answered what there had to be: two red lamps visible from 1,000 feet, two reflectors visible from 600, and on a big trailer a band of red and white that the government measured in lives. A trailer that met every rule leaves the case about the rider. A trailer that did not makes it a case about the trailer.
