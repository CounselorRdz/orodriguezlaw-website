High-Centered

Leer esta página en español →

Some railroad crossings sit on a hump. A car rolls over without a thought. A long, low trailer can catch on the top and sit there across the rails with nowhere to go. Truckers have a word for it: high-centered.

The crossing. The federal highway agency’s crossing handbook puts the danger in one line: “Vehicles or trailers low to the ground relative to the distance between axles pose the greatest risk of becoming stuck at crossings due to contact with the track or highway surface.” It explains that track maintenance may have raised the track over time as ballast was added beneath the ties, and that this can leave a crossing with “a ‘humped’ profile that may adversely affect the safety and operation of highway traffic over the railroad” (FHWA). The geometry is simple. The longer the gap between axles and the lower the frame, the less hump it takes for the underside to reach the crest and catch there.

The sign. There is a warning for it. “At locations where the profile is sufficiently abrupt to potentially immobilize vehicles, a Low Ground Clearance Grade Crossing (W10-5) warning sign” can be posted, telling “drivers of long wheelbase vehicles or drivers of vehicles that have a low ground clearance that they might encounter a hang-up situation” (FHWA). A sign is a warning. It does not lower the hump.

The driver’s rules. Federal rules sort commercial vehicles at crossings into two groups. Buses carrying passengers and certain hazardous-materials vehicles must stop “within 50 feet of, and not closer than 15 feet to, the tracks,” then listen and look (49 CFR 392.10). Every other commercial vehicle must approach “at a rate of speed which will permit said commercial motor vehicle to be stopped before reaching the nearest rail” (392.11). And the commercial license rules make a conviction for a hang-up grounds for disqualification on its own. A driver can be disqualified when he “fails to negotiate a crossing because of insufficient undercarriage clearance,” or “fails to have sufficient space to drive completely through the crossing without stopping”: no less than 60 days for a first conviction, 120 days for a second within three years, and no less than a year for a third within three years (49 CFR 383.51).

The Texas rule for low equipment. Texas has its own rule for the lowest loads. It covers equipment and structures with a vertical body or load clearance “of less than one-half inch per foot of the distance between two adjacent axles or less than nine inches measured above the level surface of a roadway,” among others. The operator may not cross “unless the operator has given notice to a station agent of the railroad” and given the railroad reasonable time to protect the crossing, and must then stop between 15 and 50 feet from the nearest rail, look and listen, and not go until it is safe (Transportation Code Section 545.255).

Who answers. The driver and the company that sent the load are the first questions, and a broken rule above is evidence of the standard of care their conduct is measured against, not a verdict by itself. They are not the only questions. In 2017 a motorcoach grounded on a humped crossing in Mississippi, under a low ground clearance sign, and a freight train struck it. Four passengers died. The federal safety board found that “the frame of the motorcoach came into contact with the pavement, and the vehicle became stuck on the crossing,” and it placed the probable cause on the failure of the railroad and the city “to coordinate and take action to improve the safety of the Main Street grade crossing, a high vertical profile crossing on which motor vehicles were known to ground frequently,” and it found that inadequate federal guidance on how to reduce the risks of such crossings contributed (NTSB). A crossing that is known to catch vehicles is a fact about the crossing, and the people who own and keep it. A claim against a city or the state runs through the Texas Tort Claims Act and its limits (Special Defect).

The evidence. The marks on the crossing where the frame dragged, the sign or the lack of one, the profile of the road measured before anyone regrades it, the train’s own recorders and video, and any history of other vehicles caught at the same spot. The physics of what the train does next is in The Grade Crossing. The records go first (Spoliation).

A hump in the road is a small thing until something long and low tries to cross it. Then it is the difference between a crossing and a trap.
