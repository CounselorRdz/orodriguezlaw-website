Safe Clearance

Leer esta página en español →

Safe clearance is the room on the far side. Before a commercial driver rolls onto a railroad crossing, the rule asks one question: is there space to get all the way across without stopping? If the answer is no, the driver waits where he is.

The rule. The federal regulation is one sentence: no driver of a commercial motor vehicle “shall drive onto a highway-rail grade crossing” without having sufficient space to drive completely through the crossing without stopping (49 CFR 392.12). It took effect on October 25, 2013, and it covers commercial motor vehicles, which include vehicles used in interstate commerce to carry passengers or property with a gross vehicle weight rating or weight of at least 10,001 pounds, as well as vehicles carrying certain hazardous materials. The agencies wrote it to reduce crossing crashes, and the final rule notes that the proposal had identified 21,208 grade crossings as “likely having limited clear storage distances” (78 Fed. Reg. 58915).

Every driver. The federal rule is written for commercial drivers. Texas has rules for everyone. An operator may not stop, stand or park a vehicle “on a railroad track,” unless stopping is necessary to avoid conflict with other traffic or to obey the law, a police officer or a traffic-control device (Transportation Code Section 545.302(a)(8) and (f)). When a signal warns of a train, a gate is down, a flagger signals, or a train is plainly visible and in hazardous proximity, the driver must stop between 15 and 50 feet from the nearest rail and stay stopped until permitted to proceed and it is safe to do so, and in a prosecution under the section, proof that a plainly visible train was in hazardous proximity is prima facie evidence that it was not safe to proceed (Section 545.251).

Why the line backs up. Clear storage distance is the space between the tracks and the place where traffic on the far side stops. When it is short, a line waiting at the next light or sign can reach back across the rails. TxDOT’s railroad preemption manual names “vehicular queues” that “routinely extend onto and/or past the grade crossing” as a source of problems and calls queue management “critical to grade crossing safety.” Its list of queue prevention strategies is made of signals, pre-signals and queue cutter signals, and, citing Institute of Transportation Engineers guidance, matches them to the length of the storage space: a pre-signal where the clear storage distance runs from the length of the design vehicle to 200 feet, a hybrid or queue cutter design from 200 to 400 feet, and an actuated queue cutter beyond 400 (TxDOT). The 2009 edition of the federal traffic control manual adds a sign: a DO NOT STOP ON TRACKS sign should be installed when an engineering study finds a significant potential for vehicles to stop on the tracks, and should be used where a stop or yield sign beyond the crossing makes it likely that lines of vehicles will reach past the tracks (MUTCD, Section 8B.09).

The other clearance. Safe clearance is the room ahead. There is also the room underneath. A humped crossing can catch a long, low trailer even when the road beyond is empty, and the commercial license rules treat a hang-up and a crossing without enough space to clear as separate grounds for disqualification (see High-Centered).

The case. It asks two sets of questions. One is about the driver: whether there was room on the far side when he started across, and whether he could see that there was not. The other is about the crossing: how long the storage space is, whether a sign or a signal called for there was in place, whether the light beyond the tracks was timed so the line would clear, and who knew vehicles were being caught. When a court adopts a safety rule as the standard of care, an unexcused violation establishes the breach, but it is not a verdict by itself: causation and damages still have to be proved (Negligence Per Se), and fault is divided among everyone the jury finds responsible (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003; see Comparative Fault). A claim against a government runs through the Tort Claims Act (Special Defect); a claim against a railroad meets the federal limits in The Crossing. The records of the signals, the crossing and the train go first (Spoliation).

Safe clearance is two words for a habit: look past the rails before you start. The law asks the driver to wait for room, and the crossing’s owners to leave room to wait.
