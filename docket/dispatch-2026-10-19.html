The Track

Leer este parte en español →

A train does not share the road. It has one line, no steering, something like a mile of stopping distance, and a horn the federal rule requires at crossings and nowhere else. A person on foot on the track between crossings is alone in a way no pedestrian on a street ever is, and Texas law has a word for him that decides almost everything that follows.

Monday about 1:05 p.m., a pedestrian and a train collided at 3101 Holmes Road, on the city’s south side, Houston police said. The man was pronounced dead at the scene. “Details are limited,” the station reported, and officers remained at the scene investigating (KPRC). The report is four sentences long. It does not say whether the man was at a crossing, on the track between crossings, or on the right-of-way beside it; which railroad owns the line; what kind of train it was or how fast it was moving; or whether a horn sounded. Each of those is what the rest of this page is about, because each one changes what the law asks.

The geography of the horn. Federal rule fixes where a train must announce itself, and the edge of the rule is the point. The horn on the lead locomotive must be sounded “approaching a public highway-rail grade crossing,” in a pattern of two long blasts, one short and one long, “repeated or prolonged until the locomotive occupies the crossing,” and it must begin “at least 15 seconds, but no more than 20 seconds, before the locomotive enters the crossing,” with a good-faith allowance to 25 seconds when the engineer cannot judge the arrival time and a shorter sounding allowed for a train starting from a stop near the crossing; a train running over 60 miles an hour may not start it “more than one-quarter mile (1,320 feet) in advance” of the crossing (49 CFR 222.21). The horn itself must produce “a minimum sound level of 96 dB(A)” and no more than “110 dB(A) at 100 feet forward of the locomotive in its direction of travel” (49 CFR 229.129). That is the whole federal requirement. Away from a public crossing the federal rule is silent; it leaves the railroad’s own operating rules in place and says it imposes no duty to sound the horn in an emergency. A separate provision lets the engineer sound the horn “to provide a warning to animals, vehicle operators, pedestrians, trespassers or crews on other trains in an emergency situation if, in the locomotive engineer’s sole judgment, such action is appropriate in order to prevent imminent injury, death, or property damage” (49 CFR 222.23). So between crossings, the warning a person on the track gets depends on an engineer seeing him, and by the time an engineer makes out a figure on the rail ahead the train is already well inside the distance it needs to stop, the arithmetic The Grade Crossing lays out for a car at the gates. Inside a quiet zone, which the city’s own rail presentation describes with the word “Realistically” as a “reduced train horn area,” the routine horn at the crossing goes away as well.

The eye. People step in front of trains they have looked at, and part of that is a measured property of vision. The size-speed illusion, first put forward by Herschel Leibowitz in 1985, is that “larger objects appear to be moving more slowly than smaller ones,” and the laboratory has confirmed it with trains: observers routinely judged a train slower than a car at the same speed, and by the published estimate a train would have to travel “between 85 km/h and 93 km/h to be perceived as travelling at the same velocity as a car at 80 km/h” (Petzoldt, 2016, reporting Clark, Perrone and Isler, 2013). A train reads as farther off and slower than it is. It is the misread The Grade Crossing describes at a crossing and Looking, Not Seeing describes on a highway, and it does not care whether the person looking is behind a windshield or on foot.

The number. The federal rail agency’s word for a person on the track away from a crossing is trespasser, and it defines trespassing as “illegally entering or remaining on a railroad right-of-way”; its count is blunt: “Trespassing is the leading cause of rail-related deaths in the United States,” and “Nationally, more than 500 trespass fatalities occur each year” (FRA). Houston is built on rail. The Gulf Coast Rail District’s 2022 overview to the city’s council committees counted “More than 700 crossings within the City of Houston,” approximately 1,200 at-grade crossings in the region, and 2,200 trains a week on the regional network (Gulf Coast Rail District overview). Every one of those trains runs between crossings as well as through them.

The word. Texas law has its own version of the word, and it decides whether there is a case. The statute defines a trespasser as “a person who enters the land of another without any legal right, express or implied,” and provides that an owner, lessee or occupant of land “does not owe a duty of care to a trespasser on the land and is not liable for any injury to a trespasser on the land, except that an owner, lessee, or occupant owes a duty to refrain from injuring a trespasser wilfully, wantonly, or through gross negligence” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 75.007). To a trespasser, in other words, ordinary carelessness is not enough; the family would have to prove gross negligence, and the railroad’s lawyers know it. The hinge is the word implied. Long before the statute, Texas juries were allowed to find that a stretch of track the public used as a path, with the railroad’s knowledge, made the person on it a licensee rather than a trespasser, and to measure the train crew’s conduct toward him by ordinary care. In a 1952 case from Wichita Falls, two young hitchhikers, boys in the opinion’s words, walking back along a path on the right-of-way at night were struck as they crossed the tracks on an embankment; one was killed and the other seriously hurt. The jury found that the place was commonly used by the public as a pathway and that the railroad knew or should have known it, that the survivor was on the premises as a licensee, and that the crew had failed to keep a proper lookout, ring the bell, blow the whistle or burn the headlight. The court of civil appeals threw the verdict out, holding he had been careless as a matter of law. The Supreme Court of Texas reversed, because “reasonable minds might reach opposite conclusions” on whether he had been careless at all, which made it the jury’s question, and sent the case back to the lower court on the one question of whether the award was too large (Gifford v. Fort Worth & Denver City Railway Co.). The 2011 statute says it does not create or increase anyone’s liability, and it kept the words express or implied. Whether a worn path the railroad has tolerated for years is an implied right under it is the first fight in any such case, and the answer sets the standard for everything after: ordinary care, or gross negligence. The three categories the law uses for people on someone else’s land are the subject of Invitee.

What the investigation asks. Where exactly the man was, which is why the first document in any such case is the railroad’s own map of the line and its mile markers. Whether there was a crossing, and what kind: gates and lights, or a crossbuck. Whether there was a path, how long it had been there, and who used it, which neighbors and aerial photographs answer. What the locomotive’s event recorder kept: a train operated faster than 30 miles an hour must carry one on the lead locomotive, it must hold “the most recent 48 hours of operation,” and on a lead locomotive “originally ordered on or after October 1, 2006 or placed in service on or after October 1, 2009” the recorded elements include train speed, direction, time, distance, throttle position, the brakes and “Horn control handle activation”; the older recorders keep the speed, direction, time, distance, throttle and brakes but not the horn (49 CFR 229.135). What the engineer and the conductor said in their statements, and when the horn first sounded, which the recorder on the newer units can confirm or contradict. And what the medical examiner finds, which is the inquest.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on Holmes Road. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a person on foot and a train meet: where on the line it happened, whether the federal rule required a horn there, whether anyone could have seen him in time, whether the place was a path the railroad knew about, and which of two very different duties the railroad owed.
