First in the Nation

Leer este parte en español →

From January through June of 2026, an estimated 1,851 people died in traffic crashes in Texas, more than in any other state. The number is an estimate about Texas roads. It is not about any one crash.

What NHTSA reported. In November 2026 the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration published its early estimate for the first half of the year. Nationally, an estimated 16,750 people died, a decrease of about 2.5 percent from an estimated 17,175 in the same months of 2025, and the fatality rate fell from 1.05 to 1.02 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled. Texas went from an estimated 1,856 deaths to 1,851, down 0.3 percent, and its rate went from 1.20 to 1.19. California, with 1,825, and Florida, with 1,439, came next (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 875).

Count and rate. A count tells you how many. A rate, deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, tells you how dangerous each mile was. By rate, Texas, at 1.19, was above the national 1.02, but ten states were higher, led by New Mexico at 1.47, and Oregon matched Texas (NHTSA, Table 3).

What the estimate is. These are statistical projections, not final counts. NHTSA allocates each region’s projected count among its states using early notification data, says the state figures are subject to change slightly as the federal fatality counts come in and as federal estimates of miles traveled are finalized, and says it will refine them when it publishes projections for the first nine months of 2026 in late December (NHTSA).

What the number does not do. A statewide count cannot tell anyone what happened in one crash. It does not say who was driving too fast for the conditions, who ran the light, or whether a belt held. Those are the questions a Texas case asks, one crash at a time, on its own evidence (see Reasonable and Prudent, Comparative Fault and Spoliation).

The physics under the count. The energy a car carries grows with the square of its speed, which is why a small difference in speed can make a large difference in a crash (see Seven Times the Energy).

Behind every one of those 1,851 is a family with questions the statistics cannot answer. Texas is first in the nation in the estimated count. In a courtroom, the count is never the case (see Wrongful Death).

Sources. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, Early Estimates of Motor Vehicle Traffic Fatalities and Fatality Rate for the First Half (January-June) of 2026, DOT HS 813 875 (November 2026), including Table 3. The figures are NHTSA’s projections and may be revised. Text and figures as published at the time of this publication.
