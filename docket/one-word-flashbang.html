Flashbang

Leer esta página en español →

A flashbang is built to blind and stun, not to wound. Whether it wounds depends on where it lands and who is lying there.

What it is. In the words of one federal appeals court, “The flash-bang grenade is a light/sound diversionary device designed to emit a brilliant light and loud noise upon detonation,” and “Its purpose is to stun, disorient, and temporarily blind its targets” (Boyd v. Benton County). Police use it to buy seconds at a door. The question in court is who paid for those seconds.

What it can do. In the Oregon case that produced that description, officers serving a search warrant in the early morning hours had been told that five to eight people might be sleeping inside. On the facts as the court took them for summary judgment, one officer “reached inside the door of the dark apartment and, without looking, tossed the flash-bang” near the front wall, where a woman was sleeping on the floor; she “suffered burns on her forearm when the device ignited” (Boyd). In a Kansas City case, a SWAT team that knew the suspect “was already in custody” threw one into a living room where “the only people inside were three women (two of them elderly) and a two-year old girl.” The girl “suffered Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (‘PTSD’) from the blast” and, the court wrote, “significant developmental regression” (Z.J. v. Kansas City Board of Police Commissioners).

The rule. Force is judged by the factors the Supreme Court named: “the severity of the crime at issue, whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of the officers or others, and whether he is actively resisting arrest or attempting to evade arrest by flight,” from “the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene” (Graham v. Connor). Applied to a flashbang, the first appeals court held in 2004 that “it cannot be a reasonable use of force under the Fourth Amendment to throw it ‘blind’ into a room occupied by innocent bystanders” absent a strong governmental interest, careful consideration of alternatives, and steps to reduce the risk of injury. It named one alternative: “a controlled evacuation followed by a search.” The officers still received qualified immunity, because neither the Supreme Court nor the Ninth Circuit had yet given guidance on when a flashbang violates the Fourth Amendment (Boyd). In 2019 the second court went further and affirmed the denial of qualified immunity to the SWAT team at summary judgment, holding only that “it was clearly established in 2010 that the use of flash-bang grenades is unreasonable where officers have no basis to believe they will face the threat of violence and they unreasonably fail to ascertain whether there are any innocent bystanders in the area it is deployed.” In the sentence just before, it set a limit: “We do not hold that every unreasonable use of a flash-bang grenade is a violation of clearly established law” (Z.J.).

The questions. Read together, the two decisions ask the same things. Did the officers have a real reason to expect violence? Did they know, or try to find out, who was inside? Did they look before they threw? Was there another way in? In a case, those answers come from records like the operations plan, the briefing notes, the radio traffic and any body-camera video from the door.

In Texas. Both decisions come from other federal circuits: the Ninth, in a case from Oregon, and the Eighth, in a case from Missouri. Neither binds the federal courts in Texas, which sit in the Fifth Circuit, and this entry does not predict how that court would rule. For the way the Fifth Circuit handles qualified immunity in force cases, see Sligh v. City of Conroe and Immunity.

A flashbang is supposed to end in a second and a ringing in the ears. When it ends in a burn, or in a two-year-old who goes backward in her development, the case asks what the officers knew about the room before they threw.

Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989) (Cornell LII). General information, not legal advice. Texts as published at the time of this publication.
