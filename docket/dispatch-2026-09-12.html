On the Job

Leer este parte en español →

Texas keeps a count of the workers who do not come home from the job, and in 2024 the count reached five hundred fifty-seven. Two things kill them more than anything else: the vehicles they work around, and the equipment they work under. Here is what the state’s own numbers say, next to the pages that explain what a worker’s case is actually made of.

The backdrop. The Texas Department of Insurance, which keeps the state’s census of fatal work injuries, counted 557 Texas workers killed on the job in 2024. Transportation incidents were the single largest cause, 242 deaths, forty-three percent of the total. Contact with objects and equipment, the category that covers being struck by, caught in, or crushed under a machine, took another 91, sixteen percent. Construction alone accounted for 128 of the deaths, nearly a quarter. Nationally, the Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 5,070 fatal work injuries the same year.

The pattern, this month. In the first days of September the same two causes wrote the same two stories. In El Paso, a worker was killed when heavy machinery fell on him during maintenance, and OSHA was called to the site to investigate, as reported by KFOX. Near Silsbee, in East Texas, a sanitation worker was thrown from a garbage truck as it veered off the road and overturned, then struck by it as it landed, the Department of Public Safety said, as reported by KFDM. No one here is named. Two workers, two of the state’s leading categories, one week.

What the pattern says. A body is not built to hold a load a machine or a truck can carry. When either one comes down on a limb or a torso, the injury is not only the break. It is the muscle crushed against itself, the pressure that builds in a sealed compartment, and the flood of toxins released back into the blood when the weight is finally lifted. That is the whole of The Crush, which is why the minutes a worker spends pinned can matter as much as the weight that pinned him, and why a collapse that buries a worker, the subject of The Trench, is a race against the same clock.

What the law does with it. Texas is the only state that lets an employer opt out of workers’ compensation. An employer that opts out is a non-subscriber, and a worker it injures can sue it directly, in court, for full damages, while it loses the old defenses that blame the worker, under Labor Code § 406.033. Where compensation does apply, it changes the target without ending the case. Comp bars a worker’s suit against his own subscribing employer, but it touches no one else on a crowded job site, not the equipment maker, not the property owner, not the other company’s crew. Workers’ compensation is a floor, not a ceiling: it closes the door on one defendant and leaves open the one the case usually turns on. Sorting out who is who, early, is the work, and the industrial accidents hub, its Third-Party Claims and Non-Subscriber pages, and the Before You Call companion cover how it is done. OSHA’s standards set the baseline for the work, and the citations that follow an incident are evidence a company chose the schedule over the safety it owed, which is what Custom is about.
