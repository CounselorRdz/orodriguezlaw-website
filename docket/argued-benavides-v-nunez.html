The Fifth CircuitArgued · No. 24-20445

Benavides v. Nunez

SERIES: The Qualified Immunity Appeal · Part 1 of 3.
COURT: U.S. Court of Appeals, Fifth Circuit · on appeal from the Southern District of Texas.
PANEL: Elrod, Engelhardt, Guidry (writing).
DECIDED: July 16, 2025. Appeal dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.
WHY IT MATTERS: A deputy lost his appeal before the merits, by making the one argument the court is not allowed to hear.

A sheriff's deputy had a rare right: to appeal before the trial even happened. He used it to argue about what the facts were. That single choice ended the appeal before it started.

The Setup

The night after Christmas 2019, in Harris County, a woman called 911 to report a break-in and locked herself and her children upstairs. Her father, Stephen Benavides, got there before the deputies, went in, and found no intruder. No one called dispatch to say the emergency was over. Deputies arrived with weapons drawn, believing they were walking into a home invasion. Benavides opened the front door from the inside. As Deputy Nunez moved his firearm from one hand to the other, the weapon discharged, and the round struck Benavides in the thigh. Body-camera video showed Benavides was unarmed and made no threat.

That left one fact in genuine dispute, and it was the fact that decided everything: was the shot intentional, or was it an accidental discharge as the gun moved hand to hand? The district court said a reasonable jury could go either way, denied Deputy Nunez qualified immunity, and set the case for trial. The deputy did not wait for the trial. He appealed.

The Question

Here is the thing most people miss about this appeal, and it is the whole point of the case. The question in front of the Fifth Circuit was not whether the deputy deserved immunity. It was whether the court was even allowed to hear the appeal at all.

Denial of qualified immunity is one of the few pretrial rulings a defendant can appeal immediately, before a final judgment. But that right comes with a wire attached, and the wire is easy to trip.

The Law Beneath

An immediate appeal of a qualified-immunity denial exists to test one thing: the law. Taking the plaintiff's version of the facts as true, did the official violate clearly established law? That is a legal question, and a court of appeals can answer it before trial. What the court of appeals cannot do, on this kind of early appeal, is decide whose story is right. The Supreme Court drew that line in Johnson v. Jones, and the Fifth Circuit enforces it with a two-word distinction: the court may review whether a disputed fact is material, but not whether the dispute is genuine. Materiality is a question of law, does this fact matter to immunity. Genuineness is a question of evidence, is there really a dispute, and that one belongs to the district court and, ultimately, the jury.

There is a single narrow escape hatch. Under Scott v. Harris, if a video blatantly contradicts and utterly discredits the plaintiff's account, the court can disregard the plaintiff's version. Here the body-camera footage cut the other way. As the panel put it, the video "supports at least two reasonable interpretations," so the exception did not apply.

The Argument

Watch what the deputy's briefing did, because the craft lesson lives right here. His central argument was that the evidence overwhelmingly established the discharge was accidental. Read that again. He was asking the appellate court to look at the record, reject the district court's finding that the intentional-versus-accidental question was genuinely disputed, and adopt his version instead. That is an attack on genuineness. And genuineness is precisely the thing the court had already told him it could not touch on this kind of appeal.

There was a version of this appeal that would have been heard. If the deputy had said, assume it was intentional, as the district court did, and even then the law was not clearly established that this conduct crossed the line, that is a legal argument about clearly established law, and the panel could have decided it. He did not run that argument. He ran the factual one. So the court never reached immunity at all.

How It Landed, And The Lesson

The Fifth Circuit dismissed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. In the panel's words, "we lack the jurisdiction to review the district court's decision that a genuine issue of fact exists." The case goes back down, and qualified immunity stays alive to be decided at trial under the right jury instructions. The deputy did not lose immunity. He lost the appeal, which is a different and more avoidable thing.

The durable lesson works for both sides of the docket, which is why it belongs in a field guide and not a brief. An early appeal from a qualified-immunity denial is a narrow tool with one job: to ask whether the official wins on the law even if every disputed fact is resolved against him. The moment the appeal turns into an argument about what really happened, it is not just weak. It is jurisdictionally dead on arrival. Before anyone takes that appeal, the only question that matters is the honest one: can I win taking everything the other side says as true? If the answer is no, the courthouse the appeal is trying to reach does not have its doors open.
