Off the Road

Leer esta página en español →

A loaded truck drifts out of its lane, crosses the shoulder and leaves the road. No other vehicle touched it. The crash report will call it a single-vehicle crash, and the first reading is that the driver did it to himself. The truck, the logs and the carrier’s records are where that first reading gets tested.

The count. In 2024, 5,340 people died in crashes involving large trucks, and 920 of them, 17 percent, were people inside the trucks; 560 of those 920 died in crashes involving no other vehicle (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 816, Table 2). IIHS, classifying the same deaths its own way, puts the single-vehicle number at 531, or 58 percent (IIHS).

How the study reads a crash. The Large Truck Crash Causation Study investigated a nationally representative sample of fatal and injury crashes involving large trucks from 2001 to 2003 (FMCSA, Report to Congress). It coded three things in each crash, assigning the first two to the vehicle whose action made the crash inevitable. The critical event: “The action or event that put the vehicle or vehicles on a course that made the collision unavoidable.” The critical reason: “The immediate reason for the critical event (i.e., the failure leading to the critical event).” And the associated factors that were present. Of the large trucks in the study’s sample, 32 percent were assigned the critical event “Running out of the travel lane, either into another lane or off the road” (FMCSA, Analysis Brief). That count includes trucks that drifted into another lane, not only trucks that left the road.

Four kinds of failure. Of the trucks the study coded with the critical reason, 87 percent of the reasons were the driver’s, 10 percent the vehicle’s and 3 percent the environment’s. It sorted the driver’s into four kinds, each given as a share of all the trucks coded with a critical reason, so together they make up the 87 percent. Non-performance, 12 percent: “The driver fell asleep, was disabled by a heart attack or seizure, or was physically impaired for another reason.” Recognition, 28 percent: the driver “was inattentive, was distracted by something inside or outside the vehicle,” or did not see what was there. Decision, 38 percent: for example “driving too fast for conditions, misjudged the speed of other vehicles, or followed other vehicles too closely.” Performance, 9 percent: “the driver panicked, overcompensated, or exercised poor directional control” (Analysis Brief).

What rides along. An associated factor was present; it did not have to be the cause. Across the estimated 141,000 large trucks in fatal and injury crashes that the study represents, the brief lists brake problems for 29 percent, traveling too fast for conditions for 23 percent, unfamiliarity with the roadway for 22 percent, roadway problems for 20 percent, over-the-counter drug use for 17 percent, inadequate surveillance for 14 percent and fatigue for 13 percent. And it adds a caution worth keeping: “Crash reconstruction experts rarely conclude that crashes are the result of a single factor” (Analysis Brief). The data are more than twenty years old; the categories still describe the questions.

Reading a departure. Each kind of failure points to a different set of questions. A truck that drifts off with no braking and no steering raises the first two: was the driver asleep, sick, or looking at something else? A truck that brakes or slides first raises the third: was it going too fast for the curve, the rain or the load? A truck that leaves the road after a hard steer back raises the fourth, the one the study called overcompensating. The answers are kept in the engine data, the hours records, the phone, the marks on the road and the driver’s medical history (see Black Box and ELD Data, The Other Clock and Sudden Incapacity).

The rules. Two federal rules speak to the most common answers. A driver may not drive a commercial motor vehicle while his ability or alertness is so impaired, or so likely to become impaired, through fatigue, illness or any other cause as to make it unsafe, and a motor carrier “shall not require or permit” him to (49 CFR 392.3). And every motor carrier must systematically inspect, repair and maintain the vehicles under its control, and “Parts and accessories shall be in safe and proper operating condition at all times” (49 CFR 396.3; see Brake Fade).

Not alone. Single-vehicle describes the scene, not the cause (see Single-Vehicle). A truck can be sent off the road by a carrier that let a tired or sick driver keep going, a brake nobody fixed, a load stacked or tied down wrong (Securement), or a road edge that caught a tire (The Drop-Off). What happens next to the people inside is the subject of Threshold and Saddle Tanks. When the driver is the one who dies, his family’s claims are in When the Driver Dies.

No other vehicle is a fact about the scene. Why the truck left its lane is a different question, and the truck, the logs and the carrier’s records are where the answer is kept, until someone lets them go (Spoliation).
