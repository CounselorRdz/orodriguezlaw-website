Mayday

Leer este parte en español →

A mayday call is a plea for help. In a courtroom it becomes something else: proof of what the people aboard knew, and when they knew it. Texas keeps a dead person’s own claim alive after death, and the claim pays for what that person consciously felt, the pain and the fear of the last minutes. The hardest part of that claim is proving the minutes happened. A recorded radio call is the rare case where the proof speaks for itself.

On Sunday, about 12:50 in the afternoon, a Learjet 60 that had left Austin-Bergstrom International Airport for the Bahamas nose-dived into a neighborhood in Palm Beach County, Florida, and struck the ground beside a busy road in a fireball that appeared to just miss homes. Nine people were aboard, and the nation’s transportation secretary said everyone on board was killed; there were no immediate reports of anyone on the ground hurt, and authorities were evacuating about 600 people from a nearby mobile home community (AP). The plane had been heading east at 35,000 feet when the pilot reported ice accumulating on the jet and turbulence, told controllers “We have light, clear ice that is manageable and moderate turbulence,” in recordings captured by LiveATC.net, and asked to climb 2,000 feet. Then, according to air traffic control recordings and flight tracking data, the descent began and grew, first 10,000 feet a minute, then 20,000, then up to 30,000. The pilot radioed “mayday, mayday” and “we’re descending 10,000 feet per minute,” then “We are in an overspeed condition descending 20,000 feet per minute,” and then “We’re going to crash into the ground.” The plane banked sharply to the right before it crashed steeply into the ground (AP via PBS NewsHour). The Federal Aviation Administration is supporting the National Transportation Safety Board’s investigation. Authorities did not say what caused the jet to go down, and nothing on this page does either. Other coverage has begun to name the people aboard; they are not named here.

What ice does to a wing. The crew’s own word for the ice was manageable, and whether it stayed that way is the investigators’ question, not this page’s. What ice can do in general is written in the FAA’s pilot guide. “Even a thin layer of ice at the leading edge of a wing, especially if it is rough,” “can have a significant effect in increasing stall speed,” because “the Angle of Attack (AOA) at which a stall occurs (the stall angle) is much lower with ice than without ice,” and the tail collects ice faster than the wing does: an aircraft’s tailplane “will be a better collector than its wings” and “On most aircraft, the tailplane is not visible to the pilot” (FAA, AC 91-74B). The guide also says what certification does not promise: “It is always possible to encounter an unusual condition for which the aircraft has not been certificated,” and the example it gives is liquid water content outside the certification envelopes, shown by a very rapid rate of accumulation. Overspeed is the other edge of the same narrow band. A jet has a maximum operating speed, written as a speed at lower altitudes and as a Mach number up high, and those limits “always define the maximum operating speed of the airplane”; past them, “The handling qualities of a jet may change significantly,” and the nose-down tendency the handbook calls tuck has to be caught early “to prevent entry into a steep, sometimes unrecoverable, dive” (FAA, Airplane Flying Handbook, Chapter 16). Ice on the Wing walks through the science, as science and not as a finding about this flight.

The arithmetic of the fall. A descent of 10,000 feet a minute is about 167 feet a second, roughly 114 miles an hour straight down before any forward speed is counted. At 20,000 feet a minute it is about 227 miles an hour; at 30,000, about 341. From 35,000 feet, even at the fastest rate on the recording the whole way, the fall takes more than a minute, and the rates on the recording built up over time, so it took longer than that. The figures are arithmetic, not a measurement of this flight. They matter for one reason: the law that follows asks whether a person knew, and for how long.

The law. Texas splits a death into two claims, which Wrongful Death explains. The family’s claim is “for the exclusive benefit of the surviving spouse, children, and parents of the deceased” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 71.004), for an injury caused by another’s “wrongful act, neglect, carelessness, unskillfulness, or default” (Section 71.002). The second claim belongs to the person who died. A claim for personal injury “does not abate because of the death of the injured person,” and it “survives to and in favor of the heirs, legal representatives, and estate of the injured person” (Section 71.021). What that survival claim pays for is written in the jury charge the Texas Supreme Court reviewed in 2023, in a case about a highway beam that fell on a pickup on a Texas interstate: “the conscious physical pain and” the “emotional pain, torment, and suffering experienced by” the man who died “before his death,” which the court read as two things, “the mental anguish he suffered in anticipation of his injury” and the pain after it. The court then said what the proof has to show. “Evidence that there was time for” the man “to anticipate the injury is not evidence that he actually did so.” The plaintiffs had to prove he “actually suffered the damages claimed,” and “There must instead be some evidence that damages were actually suffered”; if he was knocked unconscious at once, “then he did not suffer physical or mental pain following the impact.” In that case the beam fell in a fraction of a second, no witness saw the driver brake or swerve, the medical examiner would not say he was more likely than not conscious, and the court held there was no evidence for any of the survival damages and reversed them. It also left one question open, in a footnote: whether mental anguish awards “for the split-second anticipation of injury” “should not be permitted at all” (United Rentals North America, Inc. v. Evans, No. 20-0737 (Tex. May 12, 2023)).

That is where a mayday call changes a case. The rule in Evans is that the time to know is not proof of knowing. A crew that reports a descent rate, declares an overspeed and says on a recorded frequency that the airplane is going to hit the ground has told the world what it knew, in its own voice, with a timestamp, and the fall it describes, at the rates on the recording, ran longer than a minute, not a split second. For the passengers the proof is different, because no one in the cabin was on the radio: it is the descent profile and how long it lasted, the forces a body feels when a jet accelerates downward and banks hard, what the crew said over the cabin speaker if the voice recorder survived, and the inference a jury may draw from all of it, which Evans says must be more probable than not and not a guess. The point of the page is the rule, not the result: the survival claim is only as strong as the record of the last minutes, and in this crash that record exists. Conscious is the entry on how it is built. Which state’s law governs a crash in Florida with people from wherever they were from is its own question; this page says what Texas law provides.

What the case asks. The air traffic control recording and its timestamps, and the radar and tracking data that put the altitude and the descent rate on the same clock. Whether the jet carried a cockpit voice recorder and a flight data recorder, and whether either survived the fire. The weather the crew was flying in, from the reports it made and the radar returns. Who owned the airplane and who was operating the flight, which are often two different answers. The airplane’s anti-ice systems, their maintenance records and the flight manual’s limits on flight in icing. And the medical examiner’s findings on each person, because Inquest explains that the file on a death is already open before any lawsuit is. Each of those is evidence that can be overwritten, lost or thrown away, which is why Spoliation is the first letter, and why the witness who will say what the numbers mean is an Expert the court has to admit.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides why the jet came down. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law does with a recording like that one: in Texas the person who died has a claim of their own, it pays for what they consciously endured, and the proof of it is whatever was said and recorded in the minutes before the end.
