Custodial Death

Leer esta página en español →

When someone dies in a Texas jail or in police custody, the law starts paperwork the family never sees: a notice to a judge, a report to the attorney general, and, in a county jail, an investigation by an agency that does not run the jail.

What custody means. For the report the law requires, a person is “in the custody of a peace officer” when the person is under arrest by a peace officer, or under the physical control or restraint of one (Code of Criminal Procedure Article 49.18). That reaches beyond a cell: a death during an arrest, or while a person is restrained, is a death in custody too. Custody explains why the word matters to the duty of care.

The notice. When a person confined in a penal institution dies, the sheriff or other person in charge “shall as soon as practicable inform the justice of the peace of the precinct” where the institution is (Article 49.18). What the justice of the peace does with that notice is the subject of Inquest.

The report. When a person dies in a peace officer’s custody, as a result of a peace officer’s use of force, or while held in a jail, correctional facility or state juvenile facility, the director of the law enforcement agency or of the facility must investigate the death, “file a written report of the cause of death with the attorney general no later than the 30th day” after the death, and “make a good faith effort to obtain all facts relevant to the death and include those facts in the report.” The attorney general must make the report available to any interested person, except any part the attorney general decides is privileged (Article 49.18). For a facility operated by or under contract with the Texas Department of Criminal Justice, the notice to the justice of the peace does not apply, and the report does not apply in the circumstances described in Government Code Section 501.055(b)(2).

The outside investigation. For a county jail, the law takes the investigation away from the agency that runs it: “On the death of a prisoner in a county jail, the commission shall appoint a law enforcement agency, other than the local law enforcement agency that operates the county jail, to investigate the death as soon as possible.” The commission is the Texas Commission on Jail Standards. If the appointed agency presents evidence of a conflict of interest that cannot be mitigated and the commission agrees, the commission appoints another (Government Code Section 511.021).

What the report is not. The report states a cause of death and the facts the agency gathered. It is the agency’s account, written by the agency that had custody, and it is not a finding of fault. A family’s questions usually reach past it: the intake screening, the medical requests and how they were answered, the cell checks and the logs that record them, the video, the restraint, the time between the first sign of trouble and the first call for help. Those records sit with the jail, the medical contractor and the investigating agency, and the duty to keep them does not wait for a lawsuit (Spoliation). The standard a court applies to a claim for a death in custody is its own question; see Deliberate and Jail Death and Custody.

A death in custody produces paper on a schedule the law sets. The family’s first job is to know that the paper exists, who holds it, and when it is due.

Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 49.18 and Tex. Gov’t Code 511.021 (Texas.Public.Law, verified May 26, 2025). General information, not legal advice. Texts as published at the time of this publication.
