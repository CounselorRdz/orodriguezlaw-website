Slow Moving

Leer este parte en español →

The orange triangle on the back of a tractor says one thing: this vehicle is slow. The danger it warns about is not the tractor’s slowness. It is your speed, closing on it faster than your eye will admit.

No single crash is the occasion today. Harvest is moving equipment onto Texas farm roads, and the pattern that comes with it is one this page has never run: a vehicle the law itself calls slow, met from behind by traffic doing the limit. No one is named here, and nothing on this page is about any crash. It is about the arithmetic, the three ways it goes wrong, and what Texas asks of both drivers.

Why a slow vehicle is a fast problem. Closing speed is set by the difference between two speeds, not by either one alone, which is the point Closing Speed makes with the numbers. Texas calls a vehicle slow-moving when it is built to go no faster than twenty-five miles an hour, and it gives the same name to the implement of husbandry towed behind one (Transportation Code Section 547.001). A tractor or a combine at fifteen or twenty on a farm road posted at sixty is not a slow problem. It is a forty mile an hour closing problem, or worse, and the eye reads the shape ahead as nearly parked until the gap is gone. The state’s insurance department puts it in a picture: a driver going fifty-five, a football field behind a vehicle doing twenty-five, closes that distance, by the department’s count, in about five seconds (TDI); by the arithmetic, thirty miles an hour of closing speed is forty-four feet a second, and a hundred yards are gone in under seven. Three crashes repeat. The rear-end, which the department calls one of the most common farm-vehicle collisions on the road. The pass that meets oncoming traffic head-on, which it says can be deadly. And the one it says causes most collisions between tractors and cars: the operator pulls to the right to set up a wide left turn, the driver behind reads the drift as an invitation to pass, and the turn and the pass arrive in the same instant, the motorcycle left turn of The Left Turn with the roles reversed. More than seventy percent of farm-vehicle collisions happen between three and six in the afternoon, on roads posted over fifty, which the department reads as a visibility and line-of-sight problem, and which is also the hour when harvest traffic and the school-and-commute run share the same two lanes. By the research the department cites, thousands of collisions a year across the country involve farm equipment, and about fifty tractor operators a year die in collisions with motor vehicles or trains (TDI), on rural roads that carried forty-three percent of the nation’s traffic deaths from 2017 to 2021 though only a fifth of its people live along them (U.S. DOT), and Texas has more than 426,000 miles of them.

What the law asks. That is the turn. A thing the law calls slow can carry a fast danger, and the duty runs both ways across the center line. Texas tells the equipment to be seen. A slow-moving vehicle must display the emblem, with a reflective surface made to be clearly visible in daylight or at night by standard automobile headlamps from at least five hundred feet, mounted base down on the rear at a height that does not hide it, and kept in a clean, reflective condition (Section 547.703); a towed implement needs its own only when the tractor’s is blocked. The department adds what the statute assumes: the fluorescent center is for daylight, the reflective border is for headlights, the triangle rides point up between two and six feet off the ground, a red taillight has to show for five hundred feet, and the operator should signal a turn at least a hundred feet before it. Texas tells the driver behind to hold a speed that is reasonable and prudent for what is actually ahead, with regard for the actual and potential hazards then existing (Section 545.351), and to pass only where the road and the law allow, which is Centerline. When a missing or mud-caked emblem and an unsafe pass both put a crash on the road, Texas does not pick one. It has the jury set a percentage of responsibility for everyone who contributed (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003) and bars the injured person only when his own share passes half (Section 33.001), which is Comparative Fault. The cause of any one crash is a separate question for the evidence, and the evidence is in the skid, the point of impact, and whether the triangle was there and clean.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash.
