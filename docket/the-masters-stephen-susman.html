Stephen Susman

Leer esta página en español →

Stephen Susman won cases in front of juries for decades and spent his last five years making sure there would still be juries to win in front of. He is here for the second job as much as the first.

He was born in Houston. He graduated magna cum laude from Yale in 1962 and at the top of his class from the University of Texas law school in 1965, where he was editor-in-chief of the Texas Law Review. He clerked for Judge John R. Brown on the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit and then for Justice Hugo Black on the Supreme Court, and it was with Justice Black, the Civil Jury Project says, that he was “indoctrinated in the value of trial by jury.” He joined a large Houston firm, took a year away to teach law at the University of Texas, and in 1980 founded Susman Godfrey, which his firm describes as one of the first boutique law firms to limit its practice to commercial litigation. He won complex cases, his firm says, “for both plaintiffs and defendants, large and small.” He died on July 14, 2020.

The firm was built, in its own telling, on the idea of handling large commercial cases on a contingency basis, and the firm describes his core belief in words any trial lawyer can test a case against: “if a lawyer was not willing to take a case on contingency, the case was not worth litigating.” In 2017 he told The American Lawyer that his goal had been a firm that “focuses on results instead of billable hours.” When he started, his firm notes, contingent fees were “largely limited to personal injury cases,” and a partner later credited him with pioneering them in commercial litigation.

Then the second job. In 2015 he launched the Civil Jury Project at NYU School of Law and served as its executive director, pro bono. He said at the start that it would examine “why there has been such a steep drop-off in the number of civil jury trials,” and he put the stakes in one line: “We are at a critical moment in American history for the jury.” The project describes itself as the only academic center in the country that studies why jury trials are disappearing.

What he wanted was a jury that understood the case it was deciding. With Richard L. Jolly he wrote an empirical study of eight courtroom practices, and the project still lists them: limits on the length of trials, substantive instructions on the law at the start of trial, questions from jurors, written questionnaires before jury selection, short opening statements before jury selection, interim arguments by counsel during the trial, the two sides’ experts heard back to back, and jurors discussing the evidence before deliberations. The reasons the study gives come back to jurors. Letting them ask questions “helps jurors better understand the facts and evidence presented in the case,” and “Most jurors report that being able to ask questions keeps them awake and engaged.” Shorter trials matter because “the quality of jurors who are willing or able to serve increases as the length of the anticipated trial decreases.” The conclusion is a careful one: “these techniques may help halt the disappearing jury trial.”

He also asked lawyers to make the fight smaller. He kept a website, TrialbyAgreement.com, where lawyers and judges could trade ideas about innovative discovery and trial procedure, and he urged courts to encourage the parties to agree on their own rules to streamline their trials. In the mid-1990s, as chair of the Supreme Court of Texas’s discovery advisory committee, he succeeded, the project says, in having Texas adopt rules that limited discovery.

None of it was a substitute for the work. In a tribute on the Senate floor in July 2020, printed in the Congressional Record, a senator said he “prepared painstakingly, mastering the facts, so that he could back up every single thing he said in front of a jury,” and that he was happy to share the spotlight with younger lawyers, and in fact insisted on it. At NYU he taught a course called How to Try a Jury Case Intelligently. What he hoped to leave, he said, was this: “I hope that my legacy will be that I restored an interest in the legal system in trying cases in front of a jury.”

Why he is here. Because he was a Houston lawyer who believed the jury box is where a case should be decided, and every injury case is built to be decided there. Because his own study, published in 2017, named Texas: it reported that “only Nevada and Texas prohibit preinstructions,” the instructions on the law he wanted jurors to hear at the start of trial. In the instructions the Supreme Court of Texas approved for civil juries in 2011, jurors may take notes “if taking notes will help focus your attention on the evidence,” but they are told not to discuss the case with anyone during the trial, “not even with the other jurors, until the end of the trial,” and the instructions say nothing about jurors asking questions of witnesses. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates whose work he studied, with Fred Bartlit, Joe Jamail and Lisa Blatt. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Take the case you would take for a share of the result, and only that case. Agree with the other side on everything that does not matter, so the jury hears only what does. And prepare until you can back up every sentence you say in the courtroom, because a juror has to understand you before a juror can rule for you.

So when someone says a jury will not follow your case, that it is too technical, too medical or too long, remember a Houston lawyer who spent his last years arguing that the trouble was usually the trial and not the jurors. Make the trial shorter, plainer and clearer. Then trust the people in the box.
