Shelter in Place

Leer esta página en español →

Shelter in place is the shortest emergency order there is. Three words on a phone, or no words at all and a siren, and a town goes indoors. It is advice about air, it is good for a limited time, and after the all-clear it becomes something else: a term in federal law, and a dated fact in whatever comes next.

Shelter in place. The federal disease-control agency files it under a plainer instruction, to stay put and seal off the space, and says what it means: “It means that leaving the area or building during a chemical emergency may place you in danger,” and “It is a way to keep you as safe as possible until the emergency is under control.” The steps are the same in every version. Get inside. “Close and lock all windows and doors.” “Turn off the fan, air conditioner, and furnace. Close the fireplace damper.” Choose a room “on the highest floor possible,” and listen for instructions (CDC). A Texas city on the Houston Ship Channel explains it the way its residents hear it: when harmful agents are in the air, “evacuation may be dangerous,” so “you may be told to find shelter indoors wherever you are. This is called Shelter-in-Place” (City of Baytown). Another puts the rule for its sirens in capitals, “SIRENS ALWAYS MEAN SHELTER IN PLACE,” with the warning that follows from it: “Do not exit structures to listen to the siren system” (City of Deer Park).

Why indoors works, and for how long. The closest study of the question was done for the towns around the Army’s chemical weapons stockpiles, and one of its findings is the reason for the order: “the peak concentration of agent vapors in a shelter will almost certainly be lower than that outside the shelter while the plume passes.” Another is the one people do not hear. Sheltering “usually offers only temporary protection because hazardous vapors accumulate in shelters when air is exchanged between the shelter and the exterior environment as the vapor plume passes.” Every house leaks. Engineers count the leak in air changes per hour: “A tight shelter might have as few as 0.2 ACH, whereas leaky shelters might have 5.0 ACH,” the report says. At 0.2, a house trades a fifth of its air with the outside every hour. At 5, it trades a houseful every twelve minutes. So what is outside gets in, more slowly and less of it, and then the cloud moves on and the arithmetic turns around: “Afterward, the concentration of agent inside the shelter is greater than that outside the shelter,” and “If people remain in the shelter after this time, they will continue to be exposed to the higher concentration of agent vapors in the shelter.” The report adds a line worth keeping: “a tighter shelter slows the escape of hazardous vapors that build up inside, turning an initial advantage into a liability” (Argonne National Laboratory). That is why the order has two halves. An Oak Ridge report says of the second: “It is important for you to avoid this additional exposure by opening the shelter promptly when emergency officials give the all-clear signal” (Oak Ridge National Laboratory). Go in fast. Open up when told, and not before: the judgment that the air outside is now the cleaner air belongs to the people with the monitors. “When it is safe to leave, you will get the ‘all clear’ from police, fire or other local officials” (CDC).

Who gives the order, and on what. Federal law requires every community’s emergency plan to include “Methods for determining the occurrence of a release, and the area or population likely to be affected by such release,” “Evacuation plans,” and “Procedures providing reliable, effective, and timely notification by the facility emergency coordinators and the community emergency coordinator to persons designated in the emergency plan, and to the public, that a release has occurred” (42 U.S.C. 11003). The plant’s own first notice of a reportable release must include, to the extent known, the “Proper precautions to take as a result of the release, including evacuation,” unless the local coordinator already has that information under the emergency plan (42 U.S.C. 11004). In Texas the power to move people in a disaster is given locally to a mayor or a county judge, who “may order the evacuation of all or part of the population from a stricken or threatened area” and, in a disaster area, may “control the movement of persons and the occupancy of premises in that area” (Government Code Section 418.108). The words shelter in place are not in that section. In practice the instruction comes from a city’s or a county’s emergency managers, quickly, and often on what the plant has told them. The alert system run by the plants east of Houston says so itself: residents “should contact and follow instructions provided by local emergency managers” (East Harris County Manufacturers Association). An order can be only as early, and only as accurate, as the information behind it.

What the words do in federal law. Sheltering in place is not only advice. For a plant covered by the federal Risk Management Program rule it is a category of harm. The plant must keep a five-year accident history of releases from covered processes that caused deaths, injuries or significant property damage on site, “or known offsite deaths, injuries, evacuations, sheltering in place, property damage, or environmental damage.” For each one it must report, among other things, the “Date, time, and approximate duration of the release,” the “Chemical(s) released,” the “Estimated quantity released in pounds,” the “Known offsite impacts,” and the “Initiating event and contributing factors if known” (40 CFR 68.42). And it must “hold a public meeting” “no later than 90 days after any RMP reportable accident at the stationary source with any known offsite impact” (40 CFR 68.210). So when a release from a covered process sends a neighborhood indoors, the evening is not over at the all-clear. It has become an entry in a federal record, and the plant owes a public meeting.

What the order is in a case, and what it is not. It is a public record with a time on it: officials judged the outside air a risk to this area, from this minute to that one. That fixes when, where and how long. It does not prove what any one person breathed, and Texas asks that question in quantities, which is the subject of The Dose. Nor does the lack of an order prove the air was clean. An order can be drawn around a plant’s industrial neighbors and stop short of the houses beyond them, and an alert is only as fast as the report behind it: “Plant operators try to post urgent messages within 15 minutes of an event, however at times it may take longer” (EHCMA). For the people outside the fence, the Texas law of nuisance is one place a question like this is asked, and the Texas Supreme Court’s definition shows how high the bar is. A nuisance is “a condition that substantially interferes with the use and enjoyment of land by causing unreasonable discomfort or annoyance to persons of ordinary sensibilities attempting to use and enjoy it,” and the word “refers not to a defendant’s conduct or to a legal claim or cause of action but to a type of legal injury involving interference with the use and enjoyment of real property.” Whether an injury of that kind brings liability depends on how it was caused, intentionally, negligently or, in limited circumstances, by an abnormally dangerous activity, and on whether the interference was substantial, because, in words the Court quoted from a treatise, the law “does not concern itself with trifles” (Crosstex North Texas Pipeline v. Gardiner). One evening indoors may be a trifle and may not be. That is a question of fact, answered on the whole record. For the people who work inside the fence the rules are different again, and On the Job carries them.

The evidence. The order is itself a record: the alert with its time, and the all-clear with its time. The plant’s messages on the alert system, if there were any. What the household did and when: who was home, when the doors were shut, when the air conditioning went off and when it came back on. What anyone felt, and at what hour. The public records come later and are worth looking up: in Texas, the state’s database of air emissions events, and for a covered plant, its accident history. The first hours are the ones that get lost (Spoliation).

Shelter in place is a bargain with two sides. The town goes inside when it is told to. What is owed back to the town is to be told quickly, to be told what it was, and to be told when it is over.

The arithmetic: five air changes an hour is one every twelve minutes. This is general information, not legal advice or emergency instruction; in an emergency, follow your local officials.
