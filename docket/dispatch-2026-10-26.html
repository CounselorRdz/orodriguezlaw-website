The Other Clock

Leer este parte en español →

A truck can be perfectly legal and still be driven at the worst hour the body has. The logbook measures how long a driver has worked. It does not measure what time it is. The body keeps its own clock, and the rule that governs trucking does not read it.

Before dawn on Monday, a pickup and a semi came together on Interstate 10 westbound near mile marker 53, close to the Tornillo exit in far east El Paso County. The state transportation department listed the collision as verified at 4:14 a.m.; the county sheriff’s office confirmed that one person died; the lanes were shut down, and no other information was immediately available (CBS4 El Paso). No one has been named, no cause has been given, and nothing on this page says fatigue had any part in it. The hour is here for its own sake, because a crash at a quarter past four in the morning sits in the overnight trough of a curve every human body runs on, and because the law that decides how long a trucker may drive has nothing to say about which hours those are.

What the rule counts. The federal hours-of-service rule is written in hours, not in clock time. “A driver may not drive without first taking 10 consecutive hours off duty.” “A driver may not drive after a period of 14 consecutive hours after coming on-duty following 10 consecutive hours off-duty.” Inside that window, “A driver may drive a total of 11 hours,” and “driving is not permitted if more than 8 hours of driving time have passed without at least a consecutive 30-minute interruption in driving status.” Then the weekly caps: no driving after having been on duty “60 hours in any period of 7 consecutive days” for a carrier that does not run every day of the week, or “70 hours in any period of 8 consecutive days” for one that does (49 CFR 395.3). Every one of those limits starts counting when the driver comes on duty. Not one of them asks what time the sun is doing. A driver who starts at six in the evening can lawfully be on his eleventh hour of driving at five in the morning, and the record that Logbook describes will show a clean week.

The other clock. The body does not count that way. Europe’s road-safety observatory, summarizing the research on shift work, puts it in two sentences: “Fatigue-related crashes tend to occur in two distinct periods of the day,” “between midnight and 6 am, and between about 2 pm and 4 pm,” and “These periods coincide with typical low-points in our daily pattern of alertness, or circadian rhythm.” The same page reports what the studies behind it found about two of the things that make a driver tired, how long he has been at it and what hour it is: “time of day has a larger impact on driver fatigue than time on task.” One of the cited studies found “the risk of being in a single-vehicle accident at 3 o’clock in the morning was 50% above the baseline after four successive night shifts” (European Road Safety Observatory, Shift workers). The National Transportation Safety Board, which investigates the crashes, uses a phrase for the overnight trough, “the nighttime window of circadian low,” and it has asked the regulators to “Incorporate scientifically based fatigue mitigation strategies into the hours-of-service regulations for passenger-carrying drivers who operate during” that window, a recommendation written for bus drivers; the truck rule quoted above has no counterpart. Its count of the cost rests on a 2014 study by the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety that used detailed crash investigations “to estimate that about 21 percent of fatal crashes involved a drowsy driver,” and it explains why the official numbers run lower: “Drowsy driving does not leave telltale signs and, as a result, it is widely believed to be underreported on police crash forms” (NTSB, Most Wanted List, Fatigue). A 1997 paper in Nature set out to equate the impairment from fatigue with the impairment from alcohol, and found that “moderate levels of fatigue produce higher levels of impairment than the proscribed level of alcohol intoxication” (Dawson and Reid, Nature); what alcohol does to the last two seconds before a crash is the subject of The Reaction, and the comparison is about fatigue in general, not about any driver on I-10 on Monday.

The arithmetic. What fails at that hour, in the Safety Board’s words, is the ability “to stay awake, alert, and attentive to the demands of safely controlling a vehicle,” and the road does not pause for it. At seventy miles an hour a vehicle covers about 103 feet every second, so a lapse of three seconds is more than 300 feet with no one steering, roughly the length of a football field, on a lane twelve feet wide. The figures are arithmetic, not a measurement of any crash. They are the same lesson Looking, Not Seeing draws from a driver who is awake and still does not register the stopped truck in his lane: the time to act can be gone before the driver knows it is going.

The law. This is where the piece turns back to The Clock, which read a crash through the hours-of-service rule. The rule is a floor, and a logbook that is legal on its face is where the questions start, not where they end. Texas forbids every driver, trucker or not, “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” (Transportation Code Section 545.351), and a driver who is nodding at the wheel is not driving reasonably at any speed. When a crash happens in the window, the honest inquiry reaches past the log to the records that show the driver’s real last day: the electronic log itself and its edits, the fuel and toll receipts, the phone, the dispatch messages, the dash camera, the delivery windows the carrier set. Fatigue leaves no mark on the pavement, which is why the Safety Board says it is believed to be underreported, and why the records are the case. A jury that hears one divides it in whole-number percentages among the parties and anyone designated as responsible, the driver and the company that scheduled him included (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003).

What the case asks. When the driver came on duty, and when before that he last slept, which the electronic log records and the receipts and the phone confirm or contradict (the black box). Whether the carrier’s schedule could be run inside the rule at all, and whether it could be run inside the rule without putting the driver on the road between midnight and six in the morning, which is the question Trucking Company Liability is built around. Whether the braking and steering in the last seconds, which the engine recorder keeps, look like a driver who saw what was coming or one who did not. And whether all of that was preserved, because an electronic log can be edited and a camera overwritten, and Spoliation explains what it costs the party that let it happen.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on I-10 on Monday. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law counts and what it does not: the rule keeps one clock, the body keeps another, and between midnight and six in the morning the two disagree.
