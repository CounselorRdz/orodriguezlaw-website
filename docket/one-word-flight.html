One WordEntry 049

Flight

A crash is one wrong. Leaving is a second, and the law treats the leaving as its own kind of harm.

Texas puts an affirmative duty on every driver in a wreck that hurts someone: stop, stay, give your information, and help. Driving away from an injury crash is not merely cold. It is a crime, a felony when a person is hurt or killed. That is the criminal side, and it is serious on its own.

The civil side is quieter and just as important. Flight is evidence. A jury is allowed to ask why a person runs, and the usual answer, a consciousness of fault, is exactly what can turn ordinary carelessness into something a jury is permitted to punish. Leaving the scene is one of the facts that can support a claim for exemplary damages, the damages meant not to repay a loss but to answer conduct the community will not tolerate.

And leaving creates a practical problem the law already anticipated. When the driver is never found, the injured person is not left with nothing. Uninsured-motorist coverage, the coverage on the injured person's own policy, is built for this exact situation, the driver who caused the harm and vanished. It steps into the empty chair.

So the word does double work. On the criminal docket, flight is a charge. In the civil case, it is proof of the mind behind the act and a trigger for the coverage that can make the injured person whole when the one responsible is gone.

The lesson: do not treat the driver’s disappearance as the end of the case. It is often the strongest fact in it.

On the driver’s duty after an injury crash, see Texas Transportation Code Section 550.021 (Collision Involving Personal Injury or Death). Exemplary damages are governed by Chapter 41 of the Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code. This is general information, not legal advice.
