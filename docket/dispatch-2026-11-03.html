The Operator

Leer este parte en español →

The first question after a crash is almost never the one people ask first. They ask what went wrong. The law asks who was in charge. When a small jet goes down, the name on the registration and the name in the brochure are rarely the name that matters. What matters is who held the authority to start the flight, run it, and call it off. The rules give that a name, operational control, and the party that had it is the operator. The operator is the one the law looks to first.

The pattern. The Safety Board’s investigation page for the jet that left Austin-Bergstrom for the Bahamas on Sunday says the agency has launched a go team, names the company that operated the airplane, and gives the route; everything on it is marked preliminary and subject to change (NTSB, investigation ERA27MA026). Austin’s public radio station reported from the Board’s first briefing that the agency described the flight as a Part 135 operation, the rules that cover on-demand commercial flying such as charters and air taxis, and that the airplane was operated by a company doing business under a charter brand; the station names the people who died, and they are named in that coverage and not here (KUT). This page names no one, no company, and decides nothing about why the jet came down. It is here because many flights that look private are not: a business jet can sit on a charter company’s certificate, be booked through a broker, flown by crew the company employs, and sold to a passenger as a seat for hire. When a flight like that ends badly, the family is handed a tangle of company names, and the law has a way of cutting through it.

The word. The regulations define it in one sentence. “Operational control, with respect to a flight, means the exercise of authority over initiating, conducting or terminating a flight” (14 CFR 1.1). The same section defines a commercial operator as “a person who, for compensation or hire, engages in the carriage by aircraft in air commerce of persons or property,” and it defines operate broadly, as to “use, cause to use or authorize to use aircraft,” “with or without the right of legal control (as owner, lessee, or otherwise).” That last clause is the whole point. Who owns the airplane is one question. Who operated the flight is a different one, and it is the one that counts.

Two sets of rules. Federal aviation law draws a line between flying your own airplane and carrying people for money. Part 91 “prescribes rules governing the operation of aircraft within the United States” (14 CFR 91.1); it is the general rulebook, and private flying lives under it. Carrying people for hire needs a certificate first: “No person may operate as a direct air carrier or as a commercial operator without, or in violation of, an appropriate certificate and appropriate operations specifications” (14 CFR 119.5), and Part 119 applies to “each person operating or intending to operate civil aircraft” “As an air carrier or commercial operator, or both, in air commerce” (14 CFR 119.1). Part 135 then governs “The commuter or on-demand operations of each person who holds or is required to hold an Air Carrier Certificate or Operating Certificate under part 119” (14 CFR 135.1), and it adds duties that private flying does not carry: crew limits, so that “during any 24 consecutive hours the total flight time of the assigned flight when added to any other commercial flying by that flight crewmember may not exceed” “8 hours for a flight crew consisting of one pilot” or “10 hours for a flight crew consisting of two pilots” (14 CFR 135.267); maintenance rules “in addition to those in other parts of this chapter” (14 CFR 135.411); and a named chain of command for the flight itself: “Each certificate holder is responsible for operational control and shall list, in the manual required by § 135.21, the name and title of each person authorized by it to exercise operational control” (14 CFR 135.77). Which set of rules applied to a flight is not a technicality. It sets the standard of care the operator owed the people in the cabin, and under Part 135 the regulation itself says who was answerable for the flight, by name and title, in a manual the company had to keep.

Who answers. The certificate holder answers for the flight the way a motor carrier answers for the 18-wheeler it dispatched, which Respondeat Superior and Trucking Company Liability explain: not because its name was painted on the door, but because it controlled the thing that caused the harm and employed the people running it. A broker who only arranged the trip usually did not hold operational control, and the regulation’s definition of operate, with or without the right of legal control, is written so that a company cannot answer the question with a lease. The rest of the case runs the way every transport case runs. The person who died has a claim of their own and the family has another, which Mayday walked through, and a Texas jury would divide responsibility among everyone with a hand in it, the operator, the owner if it is someone else, the maker of a part if the evidence points there, in whole-number percentages (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003). Which law governs a crash in another state is its own question; this page says what the federal rules provide and what Texas law does with them.

What the case asks. Whose certificate the airplane was on, and what the operations specifications allowed. Who is listed in the manual as authorized to exercise operational control, and who actually released the flight. The crew’s duty and rest records, measured against the limits above. The maintenance program and its records. The contract the passenger signed, and the one the broker signed with the operator. And the records of the flight itself: the air traffic recording, the tracking data, the weather, and the recorders if they survived. Every one of those can be lost, which is why Spoliation is the first letter.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides any crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the rules say about the first question: before the cause is known, the law already asks who was flying it, in the sense that counts, because in these cases responsibility follows control, not title.
