Alfred Thayer Mahan

Alfred Thayer Mahan was a Navy officer who changed how the great powers thought about war by asking a simple question: what actually decides who wins at sea. His answer, that command of the sea belongs to the fleet that concentrates its strength and forces a decision, reshaped the strategy of every major navy before the First World War. It is also, stripped of the salt water, one of the cleanest lessons in strategy a trial lawyer can steal.

Mahan was born in 1840 and spent his life in and around the United States Navy, but his real weapon was not a ship; it was a book. As a lecturer and then president of the Naval War College, he studied the wars of the age of sail and, in 1890, published The Influence of Sea Power upon History. It argued that the nations which rose to greatness did so by controlling the sea, and that control of the sea was won not by scattering ships to guard every coast and convoy, but by massing them into a fleet strong enough to defeat the enemy's fleet and take command of the whole board.

That is the idea worth taking. Mahan was relentless about the concentration of force. The temptation, then as now, is to spread yourself thin, to answer every threat, to defend every point, to be a little bit strong everywhere. Mahan said that is how you end up strong nowhere. Power comes from massing your strength at the decisive point and winning there, because command of the decisive point gives you everything the scattered points were trying to protect. Win the fleet action and you do not have to guard every harbor. The harbors are yours.

Translate that to a case. A lawsuit has a hundred possible fights, motions, objections, witnesses, side issues, and a lawyer can spend himself equally across all of them and arrive at trial exhausted and blunt. The Mahanian lawyer finds the decisive point, the one issue or witness or fact that, once won, decides the rest, and he concentrates his force there. He does not fight everywhere. He fights where winning wins the war. Everything else he holds, delays, or gives up cheaply, because it was never where the case was going to be decided.

Mahan's influence was so total that the admirals of Europe and Japan built their fleets around his ideas, and some of them learned the wrong half. Concentration and the decisive battle can curdle into a fixation on one grand showdown, a belief that there must always be a single climactic clash. The subtler reading, and the right one, is that you concentrate on what is decisive, which is not always a battle and not always big. Sometimes the decisive point is quiet: a document, an admission, a threshold ruling. The discipline is not loving the grand collision. It is knowing where the real leverage sits and putting your weight there.

Mahan died in 1914, just as the war he had helped shape the navies for began, and his book outlived the battleships it launched, because it was never really about ships. It was about the oldest question in strategy: where do you put your strength. Everywhere is nowhere. Find the point that commands the rest, mass your force, and win it. Command the sea, and the coastline takes care of itself. Command the decisive issue, and the case does too.
