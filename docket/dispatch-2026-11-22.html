The Bus in the Middle

Leer este parte en español →

A bus carries strangers who had no say in how it was driven. When a bus is caught in the middle of a chain reaction, its passengers have more than one place to look, and Texas law holds the bus, toward its own passengers, to a standard no other driver at the intersection has to meet.

What was reported. Shortly after 2 a.m. Saturday, at Hardy Road and North Sam Houston Parkway, a truck going east on Hardy Road ran a red light and struck a vehicle, which then collided with a METRO bus, METRO said, and the impact caused the bus to strike a pillar. The bus driver, five passengers and the drivers of the two other vehicles were hospitalized with non-life-threatening injuries, and METRO said the incident remains under investigation (ABC13). No one has been named. This page decides nothing about who was at fault.

The chain. A chain-reaction crash has a beginning and a middle. A driver facing a steady red signal must stop, and a vehicle that is not turning must remain standing until a signal to proceed is shown (Transportation Code Section 544.007(d)). A vehicle that is struck and sent into the next one may have done nothing wrong at all. Texas does not make an injured passenger pick one driver: the jury assigns a percentage of responsibility to each claimant, each defendant, each settling person and each designated responsible third party (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003; see Comparative Fault and Responsible Third Party).

The carrier. The bus is held to more. In 2020 the Supreme Court of Texas held that San Antonio’s public transit authority “is a common carrier that owes its passengers the duty to exercise a high degree of care,” which means acting as “a very cautious and prudent person” would act under the same or similar circumstances (VIA Metropolitan Transit v. Meck; see Common Carrier). Every other driver at that intersection owed ordinary care. The higher duty is not a guarantee; the Court said it does not make carriers “strictly liable as insurers.” Transit authorities still contest the rule: in Meck the authority asked the Court to overrule it, the majority declined to take that up in that case, and a concurring opinion for four of the nine justices said Texas should abandon it. Whether the bus met it in this crash is a question for the evidence, and by METRO’s account this crash began with a truck that ran the light.

The public door. When the bus belongs to a public transit authority, the claim against it runs through the Tort Claims Act. METRO is a public transit authority, and the Supreme Court of Texas has read its immunity under Chapter 451 of the Transportation Code (Metropolitan Transit Authority v. M.E.B. Engineering, 2006). That chapter makes a metropolitan rapid transit authority a governmental unit under the Tort Claims Act (Section 451.052(c)). The Act waives immunity for an injury caused by an employee’s negligence within the scope of employment when the injury arises from the operation or use of a motor-driven vehicle and the employee would be personally liable under Texas law (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.021), and in Meck the Court held that the waiver reaches a carrier’s breach of the high-degree-of-care duty. The Act also carries two limits worth knowing on the first day. It caps the damages a governmental unit can be made to pay (Section 101.023). And it requires notice of the claim within six months, describing the injury, the time and place, and the incident, unless the governmental unit already had actual notice (Section 101.101; see Notice and The One Door).

Everyone else. The claim against the truck driver, whom METRO’s account places at the start of the chain, and against that driver’s employer if the driver was on the job, is the ordinary kind (see Respondeat Superior). If that driver carried too little insurance or none, a passenger’s own auto policy may still answer, because uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from an uninsured or underinsured driver (Insurance Code Section 1952.101; see Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims). The bus driver was hurt too, on the job. A worker hurt by someone else’s driving may pursue workers’ compensation benefits and, separately, damages from the third party who caused the injury, subject to the comp carrier’s right of subrogation (Labor Code Section 417.001; see Third-Party Claims).

What lasts. The record of a crash like this is spread among a transit agency, the owner of the truck and the insurers of everyone involved, and it is easiest to keep before it is lost (see Spoliation).

A passenger on a bus chose a route, not a driver. The law’s answer is to ask more of the one who drives for a living, to hold each driver to the share a jury assigns, and to start a clock on the day of the crash.
