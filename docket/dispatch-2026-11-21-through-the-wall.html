Through the Wall

Leer este parte en español →

A car leaves the road and comes through the wall of a house, and the person who was doing nothing but sitting in a room is the one who is hurt. The law still starts where it starts in every wreck, with the driver: why did the car leave the road?

The pattern. A vehicle crosses a curb, a yard and the front wall of a home. The people inside had no seat belt, no airbag, no crumple zone and, most of the time, no warning. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The wall. A car is built around the people in it. A house is built against the weather. When the wall gives way, whatever energy the car still carries goes into the room, and the energy of a moving car grows with the square of its speed (Seven Times the Energy). The car spends its crumple zone and its airbags on the people inside it. The house has nothing to spend on the person on the couch. That is how a driver can climb out of the car while the person who was home cannot. A house is a roadside object with a person behind it (The Fixed Object).

The first question. It belongs first to the driver, though in a run-off-road case the answer can also lie in the road, the vehicle or another driver who forced the car off (Single-Vehicle). The Federal Highway Administration counts a crash that happens after a vehicle “crosses an edge line or a center line, or otherwise leaves the traveled way” as a roadway departure, and from 2016 to 2018 roadway departures averaged 19,158 deaths a year, about half of all U.S. traffic deaths (FHWA). Texas measures a driver against the conditions as they were: no one may drive “at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” (Section 545.351; Reasonable and Prudent). A speed the curve did not allow, a driver asleep at the wheel, a driver who had been drinking: any one of them can be a failure of ordinary care. The jury still decides. The wall is evidence, not a verdict.

The medical defense. A Houston court of appeals has said that “unforeseeable loss of consciousness is a complete defense” to a claim that a driver negligently caused a crash (Gomez v. Cooke). The word that matters is unforeseeable. The defense covers a collapse the driver could not have seen coming; a driver who had warning signs is back to the ordinary question of care. So the case can turn on what warning the driver had about his own health before he turned the key (Sudden Incapacity; The Blackout).

The share. Almost every crash case comes down to an argument over how to divide the fault. Texas has the jury assign a percentage of responsibility to each claimant, each defendant, each settling person and each designated responsible third party for causing or contributing to the harm, but no question about anyone’s conduct may go to the jury without sufficient evidence to support it (Section 33.003; Comparative Fault). A person sitting in their own living room was not driving, not speeding and not anywhere they should not have been. There is usually nothing to put in their column, and without evidence the question is not asked.

Who answers. When the driver is legally responsible, the driver’s liability coverage comes first. Texas requires it to cover both bodily injury and “damage to or destruction of property of others”; the same policy answers for the people and for the hole in the house, up to its limits (Section 601.072). If the driver fled and was never identified, or carried too little insurance or none, the family’s own auto policy may answer for their injuries: uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage is part of a Texas auto policy unless a named insured rejects it in writing, and it protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from an uninsured or underinsured driver (Insurance Code Section 1952.101; Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims). When the driver is never identified, the code requires that “actual physical contact” occurred between the unknown driver’s vehicle “and the person or property of the insured” (Section 1952.104). A car that came into the family’s living room has usually touched one or the other. If the collapse truly could not have been foreseen, the driver is not legally responsible, and neither his coverage nor the family’s uninsured motorist coverage pays for his conduct (Sudden Incapacity).

What lasts. The tire marks across the yard, the gouges at the curb, where the car came to rest, and, if the car carries an event data recorder, what it kept: the speed and whether the brake was on in the seconds before the crash (Delta-V). A neighbor’s doorbell camera may have the rest. All of it is easiest to collect before the car is towed and the wall is patched (Spoliation).

A person is supposed to be safe inside their own house. When a car takes that away, the question was never what they were doing there. It is why the car left the road.
