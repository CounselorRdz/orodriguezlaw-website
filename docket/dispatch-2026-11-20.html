Before It Sinks

Leer este parte en español →

A car that goes into the water does not sink at once. It floats, often for about a minute, and that minute is the best chance anyone inside will get. The way out is a window, not a door.

The pattern. A vehicle leaves the road at night and goes into a bayou, a canal, a flooded underpass or a river below a bridge. For a moment it floats. The people inside reach for the doors, because that is what doors are for. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The minute. Gordon Giesbrecht, a retired University of Manitoba professor who has run more than 80 vehicle submersion tests with people inside, puts it plainly: a sinking vehicle “may float on the water surface for up to 3 minutes, but you only have 1 minute to exit the vehicle safely.” It floats, his page says, only until the water reaches the bottom of the side windows, about a minute (University of Manitoba). The drill he recommends, SWOC, has four steps: seat belts off, windows open, out immediately, children first. The advice is to get out first, not to call 911 first and not to wait for the car to fill. An estimated 400 people die each year in submerged vehicles across North America, roughly one in ten drownings (NPR).

The door. Instinct says door. The researcher says “Use the window, not the door.” Once the water outside presses on the door, the pressure makes it very difficult, if not impossible, to open (University of Manitoba). By then the minute is gone.

The breath. The water does not have to be icy to take a breath. The National Weather Service says sudden immersion brings gasping and rapid breathing that “occurs during the first 2-3 minutes or more,” that the gasp can be “triggered by water as warm as 77°F (25°C),” and that “This uncontrolled rapid breathing can quickly create a drowning emergency if you inhale water and cannot stay afloat” (NWS). A gasp taken under water is water, not air. Cold Shock explains why.

The glass. The usual advice, break the window, has a catch. In 2019 AAA tested six escape tools, three spring-loaded and three hammer style: “only four were able to shatter the tempered glass and none were able to break the laminated glass.” AAA said 1 in 3 vehicle models from 2018 had laminated side windows, that most of those still had at least one tempered window, that a label in the bottom corner of the side window tells which glass it is, and that a hammer-style tool “will be ineffective underwater” (AAA).

The two questions. After a car goes into the water, the law asks about the driver, and it also asks two other questions. First: what should have kept the vehicle out of the water? A guardrail, a barricade, a clear zone at the edge of the road. High Water asked it at a flooded crossing, and The Clear Zone asked it at the edge of a ditch. When the road belongs to a city, a county or the state, the question runs through the Tort Claims Act, which caps damages, sets a short notice deadline and bars many claims about how a road was designed or which safety features were chosen (Special Defect). Second: did anything about the vehicle keep the people from getting out? That is a question about the vehicle itself, the kind Crashworthiness explains. And if someone did not get out, what the person consciously suffered in the water belongs to the survival claim brought for the estate, and it has to be proved; the family’s own losses are a separate wrongful death claim (Conscious; Wrongful Death).

What lasts. The road and the vehicle answer both questions, and both change fast. Rails get repaired, banks get regraded, and a car pulled from the water gets dried, stripped or crushed. Keep them before they go (Spoliation).

A car in the water is a clock, not a crash. The first minute belongs to the people inside. Everything after it belongs to two questions: what should have kept the car on the road, and what kept them from getting out.
