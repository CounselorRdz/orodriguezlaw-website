The Fifth CircuitDecided · No. 22-40518

Sligh v. City of Conroe

COURT: U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, on appeal from the Southern District of Texas (Houston).
DECIDED: November 21, 2023, No. 22-40518 (per curiam; King, Smith, Elrod, JJ.), cited as 87 F.4th 290. Affirmed the dismissal of every claim.
WHY IT MATTERS: The panel found, in a published opinion, that on the complaint and the video, sending a police dog to bite an unarmed woman in a mental-health crisis, without a clear warning, was an excessive use of force in violation of the Fourth Amendment. The officer still won, because the law was not clearly established when he did it. The Fifth Circuit has since cited the opinion as 87 F.4th 290.

A 911 call about a woman who was suicidal and had hurt herself. A police dog sent to bite her after she pulled free from a deputy. A bite that, by the Fifth Circuit’s count, lasted at most 64 seconds. The court said the force was unconstitutional. Then it said the officer was immune, and that the city was not liable.

The scene

At 1:39 a.m. on July 5, 2018, Sligh’s partner “called 911 to report that Sligh was suicidal, had hurt herself, and had left her house on foot,” and told the dispatcher she was “unarmed and not a violent person.” Two officers found her: a canine handler, “a police officer employed by the City of Conroe,” with his dog, Thor, and “a deputy employed by the Montgomery County Sheriff’s Office.” The court took the facts from the body-camera video wherever it contradicted the complaint.

As Sligh began to approach the handler, he said loudly, “The dog will bite you!” The deputy ordered her to put her hands behind her back; she cursed and slapped at his arms while trying to pull away. “Sligh and Montes physically struggle for about 11 seconds, at which point Sligh breaks free from Montes’s grip.” Then: “Sutton then releases Thor with a bite command, and Thor bites Sligh as Sutton commands her to get on the ground.” Beginning eight seconds after the bite command, the handler repeatedly ordered the dog to release; the dog did not immediately obey. Thirty-six seconds after the first bite command, he grabbed and pulled the dog’s collar. “Thor releases Sligh around 64 seconds following the first bite command.” The complaint alleged bites to her upper thigh, the back of her upper leg and her ankle (slip op.).

The district court

Judge Charles Eskridge dismissed the case in August 2022. He read the same video as showing that Sligh posed an “obvious threat to at least one human life,” her own, wrote that the handler’s “use of the canine under these circumstances can’t be said to be unreasonable,” and dismissed every claim with prejudice (opinion and order).

The rule

Force in an arrest is judged under the Fourth Amendment by what is reasonable, and the Supreme Court has named the factors: “the severity of the crime at issue, whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of the officers or others, and whether he is actively resisting arrest or attempting to evade arrest by flight,” all judged “from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, rather than with the 20/20 vision of hindsight” (Graham v. Connor).

What the court held

Two of the three factors favored Sligh. The first: “The officers were called based on concerns about Sligh’s mental health, not a crime she was suspected of committing.” The second: “the officers received no indication that Sligh was violent, armed, or otherwise posed a threat to others.” The third cut against her: “The Video shows that Sligh actively resisted seizure, did not follow verbal commands, and engaged in a physical struggle with Montes.”

Weighing them, the panel held that “the decision to sic Thor on Sligh constituted an excessive use of force in violation of the Fourth Amendment.” It pointed to what the officers could have done when she slipped free, which the court called a break in the action: escalate their use of physical force “in a more measured manner,” or provide “a clear warning that they would employ a dog bite if Sligh did not comply.” Instead, the court wrote, “Sutton sicced Thor on Sligh without warning.”

Why the officer still won

Qualified immunity asks two questions: was there a violation, and was the law clearly established when it happened (Immunity). Sligh won the first and lost the second: “we do not find that Sutton’s violation of Sligh’s constitutional right was clearly established.”

The case Sligh relied on most was Cooper v. Brown (2016), where a dog kept biting a man suspected of a misdemeanor DUI for one to two minutes, and the Fifth Circuit held that “permitting a dog to continue biting a compliant and non-threatening arrestee is objectively unreasonable.” The Sligh panel distinguished it on both counts: Cooper “involved a nonresisting plaintiff and an intentionally prolonged application of force,” and “Sligh actively resisted seizure.” The length of the bite got its own footnote: “We need not and do not address the issue of whether the prolonged duration of Thor’s bite also constituted excessive force.”

The city and the training

A city answers under Section 1983 for its own policy or its own failure, not simply because it employed the officer. Conroe’s canine policy allowed a dog to be “released off leash to apprehend any suspect evading arrest that is known to be armed, has demonstrated violence or the potential for violence, or has committed a felony offense.” The panel reasoned that the policy “limits the offensive employment of canines to a subset of suspects evading arrest, which does not apply to Sligh,” so a policy the handler was not following could not be “a moving force” behind the violation.

The training claim failed for a reason every civil-rights lawyer meets. The Supreme Court allows liability for inadequate training “only where the failure to train amounts to deliberate indifference” (City of Canton v. Harris), and the panel, citing Connick v. Thompson, put the rule this way: “A pattern of similar constitutional violations by untrained employees is ‘ordinarily necessary’ to demonstrate deliberate indifference for purposes of failure to train.” Sligh “rests the entirety of her conclusory argument on the single present incident.” One bite, however bad, was not a pattern. The claim against the deputy as a bystander failed too, and so did the disability claims.

Only as good as the handler

A police dog’s behavior reflects its training, and the record in a dog case is the record of the training. When NBC News covered the district court’s ruling, a University of South Carolina criminal justice professor said, “Five or more release commands from the handler is too many by any certification standard” (NBC News). A 2020 investigation by The Marshall Project and its partners found that “There aren’t federal regulations” for police dogs and that each department sets its own policy (The Marshall Project). In Sligh the panel treated the seconds after the release commands as force the handler did not intend, and it held that unintended continued force did not violate clearly established law. That is why, in a case like this, the training file, the certification records and the dog’s history of releasing on command are evidence: they speak to whether a long bite was an accident or a known risk.

Where it sits now

Three Fifth Circuit cases now mark the ground. Cooper (2016): a compliant man held by the dog for one to two minutes, a violation that was clearly established, no immunity. Sligh (2023): a resisting, unarmed woman in a mental-health crisis, bitten without a clear warning, a violation that was not yet clearly established, immunity. Thomas v. Bruss (2026): a 43-second bite in Harris County after which the district court found the handler “used constitutionally excessive force,” and two officers who stood by received immunity because the law had not clearly required them to intervene in that window (slip op.). In each one the case turned on seconds: when the warning came, when the person stopped resisting, when the dog let go. The video counts them. The law asks whether an earlier case had already counted them the same way.
