Serious Violation

Leer esta página en español →

After a worker is hurt, the word on the government’s citation sounds like a verdict. It is not one. Serious is a finding about what could have happened and about what the employer knew, written to a clock and priced by a formula, and the statute that created it says in so many words that it changes no one’s rights in a civil case. What it does change is the evidence.

Serious violation. The federal safety act defines the word in one sentence with two halves. “For purposes of this section, a serious violation shall be deemed to exist in a place of employment” “if there is a substantial probability that death or serious physical harm could result from a condition which exists” “or from one or more practices, means, methods, operations, or processes which have been adopted or are in use” “in such place of employment unless the employer did not, and could not with the exercise of reasonable diligence,” “know of the presence of the violation” (29 U.S.C. Section 666(k)). The first half is about the harm, and the operative word is could: the question is not whether anyone was hurt but whether the condition was the kind that kills or maims if it goes wrong. The second half is about the mind of the company: a condition the employer did not know of and could not have found with reasonable diligence is not a serious violation, however dangerous. So a serious citation says two things at once. The hazard could have killed someone, and the employer knew or should have known it was there. A violation “specifically determined not to be of a serious nature” is the other kind, and the statute treats it differently: for a serious violation an employer “shall be assessed a civil penalty,” for the other kind it “may be” (Section 666(b), (c)).

The citation. When an inspector finds a violation, the Secretary “shall with reasonable promptness issue a citation to the employer.” “Each citation shall be in writing and shall describe with particularity the nature of the violation,” and “the citation shall fix a reasonable time for the abatement of the violation.” It is not a private letter: each citation “shall be prominently posted” “at or near each place a violation referred to in the citation occurred.” And it runs on a clock: “No citation may be issued under this section after the expiration of six months following the occurrence of any violation” (29 U.S.C. Section 658). Six months is the whole window. A hazard the agency does not cite within six months of the violation is not cited, and the civil case has to prove it with its own evidence, which is one more reason the first letter goes out early (Spoliation).

The number. The statute sets the scale in figures written in 1990, and an inflation rule has raised them since. As posted by the agency for penalties assessed after January 15, 2026, the maximum for a serious violation, an other-than-serious violation or a posting violation is 16,550 dollars per violation; a failure to abate runs up to 16,550 dollars per day beyond the abatement date; and a willful or repeated violation runs up to 165,514 dollars per violation (OSHA, Penalties). The agency’s 2026 memo adds its own minimum proposed penalties, 1,085 dollars for a serious violation, 11,823 for a willful one and 4,256 for a repeat, and notes that “There are no inflation-based increases to OSHA civil penalties for 2026, so 2025 penalty amounts remain in effect for 2026” (OSHA memorandum, May 21, 2026). Between the floor and the ceiling the number is set by a formula that is mostly about the company: the agency proposes it, and on contest the Commission assesses it, giving “due consideration to the appropriateness of the penalty with respect to the size of the business,” “the gravity of the violation,” “the good faith of the employer” and “the history of previous violations” (Section 666(j)). Which is why two identical trenches can draw two different figures, and why a small number on a citation can say a small employer or a clean record as easily as it says a small hazard. The criminal side is lighter than most families expect: a willful violation that causes a worker’s death is punished, on a first conviction, by a fine, whose figure in the statute has not been updated since the act was written and is not relied on here, “or by imprisonment for not more than six months, or by both” (Section 666(e)). The weight of a workplace death is carried in the civil case or not at all.

The contest. The citation a family reads about in the first week is a proposal. Once it is served, “the employer has fifteen working days within which to notify the Secretary” “that he wishes to contest the citation or proposed assessment of penalty”; if no one contests, “the citation and the assessment, as proposed, shall be deemed a final order of the Commission” “and not subject to review by any court or agency” (29 U.S.C. Section 659). The notice must be “postmarked within 15 working days” “of the receipt by the employer of the notice of proposed penalty,” and it “shall specify whether it is directed to the citation” “or to the proposed penalty, or both” (29 CFR 1903.17). Workers have a narrower right of their own, to file a notice “alleging that the period of time fixed in the citation for the abatement of the violation is unreasonable” (Section 659(c)). A contested citation goes to a hearing before the review commission, whose order comes out “affirming, modifying, or vacating the Secretary’s citation or proposed penalty,” so what comes out can differ from what went in. So the document that matters is not the press release in week one. It is the final order, months later, and the file behind it.

What it is not. The act is explicit about its own reach. “Nothing in this chapter shall be construed to supersede or in any manner affect any workmen’s compensation law” “or to enlarge or diminish or affect in any other manner the common law or statutory rights, duties, or liabilities” “of employers and employees under any law with respect to injuries, diseases, or death of employees” “arising out of, or in the course of, employment” (29 U.S.C. Section 653(b)(4)). A citation does not give the worker a claim, and the absence of one does not take a claim away. The claim stands on Texas law, and it has three shapes. Against an employer that carries workers’ compensation, “Recovery of workers’ compensation benefits is the exclusive remedy of an employee” “covered by workers’ compensation insurance coverage or a legal beneficiary against the employer,” with one door left open for a death: the statute “does not prohibit the recovery of exemplary damages by the surviving spouse or heirs of the body” “of a deceased employee whose death was caused by an intentional act or omission of the employer” “or by the employer’s gross negligence” (Labor Code Section 408.001; Gross Negligence). Against an employer that opted out, Texas strips the old defenses: “it is not a defense that” “the employee was guilty of contributory negligence,” “the employee assumed the risk of injury or death” or “the injury or death was caused by the negligence of a fellow employee,” though “the plaintiff must prove negligence of the employer” or of its agent (Labor Code Section 406.033; Nonsubscriber Work Injuries). And against everyone else on the site, the contractor, the owner, the maker of the machine, the claim is ordinary negligence (Third-Party Claims; Statutory Employer). In each of those the serious citation does one job. It is evidence of what the standard was and that the company knew or should have known the hazard was there, the same work Custom describes for the industry’s own practice; a broken safety rule is evidence of the standard of care, and the jury still decides the breach (Negligence Per Se).

The evidence. The citation, with its particulars and its abatement date. The notice of contest, if there was one, and the final order, which is the version that counts. The agency’s file behind them, and the rule the citation had to name, since each citation must include “a reference to the provision of the chapter, standard, rule, regulation, or order alleged to have been violated” (Section 658(a)). The posting, which puts the citation on the wall where the crew could read it. And the standards the citation names, which are the rules the company was measured against: the trench that needed a Competent Person, the edge that needed The Harness, the process that needed the safety management Hours Later describes. On the Job counted the Texas workers killed in a year; this page is about the paper that follows each one of them.

Why the word matters. Serious is the government’s word for a hazard that could kill and an employer that should have seen it, and nothing more. It does not pay a family, it does not decide a case, and it can be modified or vacated on contest without the hazard getting any smaller. Read as evidence rather than as a verdict, it is one of the most useful documents in an industrial case, and it is made within six months of the violation or not at all. Nothing here is about any one workplace; the words are the same at every site, and what a particular citation proves is for its own file to say.
