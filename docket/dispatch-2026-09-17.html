The Work Zone

Leer este parte en español →

A work zone looks like the workers’ danger, a crew in vests a few feet from moving traffic. The state’s own count says otherwise. When Texas adds up the people killed in its work zones, almost all of them are the drivers and passengers passing through, not the workers standing still. Texas just closed the books on another year of it. Here is the pattern the agency reported, next to the pages that explain why the person moving is the one who dies.

The count. The Texas Department of Transportation recorded more than twenty-eight thousand crashes in Texas work zones in 2025, and 203 people were killed in them. Seven of the dead were roadside workers. The rest, the overwhelming majority, were drivers and their passengers. That is the agency’s own finding, stated plainly on its work-zone safety page: most of the people who die in a Texas work zone are the ones driving through it.

What the pattern says. A work zone is a place where the road narrows without much warning and the room to react disappears. A lane ends, traffic stops short, and a barrel, a barrier, or the back of a parked truck stands where open pavement was a moment before. At highway speed the distance it takes to stop is longer than the eye believes, and the thing now in the lane does not move; it takes the whole of the impact and hands it back to the car and the body inside. That is what The Fixed Object is about, and it is why the driver, not the worker off to the side, is so often the one killed. The Reaction explains the rest: the time between seeing a hazard and stopping for it is fixed, and a work zone is built to steal the distance that time needs.

What the law does with it. So a work zone is the rare hazard the law warns you about in writing before you reach it, and that warning changes the standard of care. Texas makes it an offense to disobey the signs, signals, and markings that lay a work zone out, or to drive around a barricade (Transportation Code Section 472.022), and when workers are present the fine doubles. A speed that is lawful on open highway becomes a breach the moment it passes a posted work-zone limit, which is what Negligence Per Se is about: the statute sets the standard, and the jury decides whether the driver met it. The duty runs to everyone inside the zone, the crew and the next car alike. And when a work zone is set up without the taper, the signs, or the barrier the traffic-control plan called for, the questions turn to whoever laid it out. The car accidents and serious personal injury pages, with their Before You Call companion, cover how a work-zone case gets built.
