Phil Corboy

Leer esta página en español →

Before Phil Corboy picked a jury, he knew every piece of paper in the file. That is most of the reason he is here. The rest is what he built on it, for his clients and for the lawyers who came after him.

His college years were interrupted by the war and never finished, and he still graduated first in his class from the law school of Loyola University Chicago in 1949. He went to work as an assistant corporation counsel for the City of Chicago and, at twenty-six, argued his first case for the city before the Illinois Supreme Court. Then James Dooley, the leading plaintiffs’ personal injury lawyer of his day, asked him to recommend someone his age for the practice, and Corboy recommended himself. He tried his first jury trial in November 1950. He made his career on the plaintiffs’ side, in the firm that became Corboy & Demetrio, and his firm counts among his work every major airplane disaster since the 1950s and the families of the victims of the Tylenol poisonings, a representation it credits with leading to tamper-resistant packaging for pain relievers. He died at his home in Chicago on June 12, 2012. He was eighty-seven.

The rule came down through the people he trained. A partner at his firm, writing in the Chicago Bar Association’s CBA Record after his death, remembered that Corboy “would lock himself in a hotel room for a week before trial to study the case,” and that the point, in Corboy’s own words, was to “know every piece of paper in that file before picking a jury.” The same lawyer wrote that Corboy “would know more about the law that applied to the case on trial than anyone in the courtroom.” The same pages list among his traits “Meticulous preparation.”

Preparation is what made the presence possible. “When Phil Corboy walked into a room, everyone knew he was there,” that partner wrote, and he recalled an opponent who said Corboy “completely controlled that courtroom.” A judge remembered that “his ability to get to the hearts of jurors was almost unbelievable,” and a lawyer who worked with him wrote that he “could connect with jurors on a personal level.” Terrence Lavin, then an Illinois appellate judge, who clerked at the firm, said, as the ABA Journal reported it: “He was the Jack Nicklaus of trial lawyers, he was a guy who was great across the decades.”

He was not easy. A lawyer who once worked for him wrote that he “was at times hard to get along with.” But a lawyer he trained put the order of things simply: “The client always came first.” His firm quotes him on why he stayed in the work: “to have an opportunity to do the right thing for each client is why I remained a trial lawyer.” When a lawyer who had started at his firm in 1980 tried, years later, to thank him for the mentoring, Corboy answered, “we all stand on the shoulders of others,” and that is a fair description of what he built for the ones coming up behind him. His firm was often called the Corboy College of Law. In 1995 he created the Philip H. Corboy Fellowship in Trial Advocacy at Loyola, and after a 2009 gift from him and his wife, Loyola renamed its law building the Philip H. Corboy Law Center in recognition of his career.

He was the first personal injury lawyer elected president of the Chicago Bar Association, a president of the Illinois Trial Lawyers Association, chair of the American Bar Association’s Section of Litigation and of the National Institute for Trial Advocacy, and a member of the first committee the Illinois Supreme Court appointed on pattern jury instructions for Illinois.

Why he is here. Because an injury case is a case of paper before it is a case of people: the logbook, the inspection reports, the test a trucking company had to give after a deadly crash or explain in writing (see The Test He Refused), the records that are gone unless someone asks for them in time (Spoliation). The lawyer who knows every page is the lawyer who notices the page that is missing. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Joe Jamail and Stephen Susman. And because of three things a trial lawyer can take from him. Study the file until nothing in it can surprise you, and lock the door if that is what it takes. Know the law of your case better than anyone else in the courtroom. And keep the order straight: the client comes first, and the lawyers coming up behind you will stand on your shoulders the way you stood on someone’s.

So when a case feels too big for the time you have, remember the hotel room. The jury will see a lawyer who seems to know everything. What they are seeing is a week with the file.
