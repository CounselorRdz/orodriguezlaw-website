The First Second

Leer este parte en español →

A blown tire starts its work in the first second, before the driver can do anything about it. The vehicle pulls toward the failure, and the person at the wheel is reacting to a truck that has already changed under him. When the law looks back at a crash like that, the first question is not whether the driver kept control. It is whether the failure was one somebody should have prevented.

Last Friday afternoon, about 3:09, on U.S. 281 about six miles north of Premont in Jim Wells County, a 1999 pickup towing a trailer loaded with a tractor was heading south when, state troopers said, its left front tire blew out. The truck struck a 2025 pickup and pushed it into the center median, then rolled and stopped in the inside lane, and the trailer came loose and rolled over the guardrail into the median. The driver died at the scene; one of his two passengers was flown to a Corpus Christi hospital, the other was not hurt, and neither was the driver of the second pickup. Troopers from the Alice highway patrol office are investigating, and the only cause stated so far is the tire (KRGV). The man who died is named in that coverage and not here, and nothing on this page decides why the tire failed. It is here because “the driver lost control” is a sentence written on crash reports every day, and a tire that comes apart at highway speed is one of the surest ways it gets written about a driver who did nothing wrong.

What a tire does when it fails. The federal highway safety agency carries, in the proceedings of its international vehicle-safety conference, a study by two engineers, run on a paved test surface, of what happens when a tire’s tread separates from its carcass, the failure that can come with no loss of air at all. They ran the failures on real vehicles at speed and measured what the vehicle did: “The vehicle responds by pulling to the side of the tread-separating tire.” The peeling tread creates drag at that wheel, which the authors expected “would generate a yaw moment that produced the heading change,” and the loose rubber adds “additional lateral forces” of its own. Two findings matter most in a courtroom. The first is about time: “The longer the tread takes to come off, the greater the vehicle response,” so “Partial tread separations result in a significant vehicle response due to the continuous duration of the event.” The second is about speed: “Higher speeds result in a greater vehicle response.” The forces, the authors wrote, “can be large enough to cause loss of control regardless of driver response,” and they closed with a sentence for every adjuster who has ever written the words driver error: “it is a significant over-simplification to implicate driver behavior as the sole cause of a vehicle’s loss of control” (Arndt and Arndt, in NHTSA’s ESV proceedings). That study separated rear tires. Friday’s was a front, and what it measured is not a measurement of this crash; what it shows is how fast a failing tire changes a vehicle, faster than The Reaction says a nervous system can answer. What a pickup does once it goes over is what The Roll describes from the inside.

The number on the sidewall. The tire that failed had a birthday, and it is molded into the rubber. “You can determine how old your tire is by looking on the sidewall for your DOT Tire Identification Number (TIN).” “The last four digits of the TIN indicate the week and year the tire was made,” so a code ending in 0308 means the third week of 2008. The agency lists what “can lead to a flat tire, blowout, or the tread coming off your tire” before the tread is gone, “not having enough air in your tires and failing to rotate your tires,” and “Sustained high temperature (for example, driving long distances in hot weather),” and it says that “Tires are not safe and should be replaced when the tread is worn down to 2/32 of an inch,” the depth at which a penny shows the top of Lincoln’s head, while “Some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires that are six to 10 years old, regardless of treadwear.” The count: “In 2024, a total of 511 people died on the road in tire-related crashes” (NHTSA, Tires). A South Texas highway, a pickup from 1999, and a trailer carrying a tractor are what the coverage gives. What it cannot give is the date on the sidewall, and no one at the scene can read it until the tire is picked up.

The law. The doctrine a driver reaches for after a blowout is Sudden Emergency: a person who meets a sudden peril not of his own making is measured by what an ordinarily prudent person would do in that instant, not by what a reconstruction shows he could have done with a week to think. A true blowout is the textbook case for it. But the doctrine has a floor, and the floor is in its own words: the emergency cannot be one the driver made. A tire worn past its tread, years past the date on its sidewall, run soft in the heat, or a trailer loaded heavier than the truck was built to pull, is an emergency with an author. Texas forbids every driver “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” (Transportation Code Section 545.351), and what a vehicle is pulling is a circumstance. When the people hurt in a crash like this bring a case, whether they rode in the truck or were struck by it, the jury divides it in whole-number percentages among the parties and anyone designated as responsible for the failure, “whether by negligent act or omission, by any defective or unreasonably dangerous product,” or both (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003): the driver, whoever loaded the trailer, whoever inspected the tires or did not, and the tire’s maker if it came apart from a defect rather than from age. A claimant whose own share passes fifty percent recovers nothing (Section 33.001; Comparative Fault), which is why “the driver lost control” is the defense’s favorite sentence, and why the first job is to find out whether the tire gave him anything to control.

What the case asks. The tire, first and whole: the failed one and its three mates, their DOT codes, their tread depths, the pressure the survivors still hold, and whether the failure is a blowout or a tread that peeled, which an engineer can tell from the carcass and no one can tell from a photograph. The trailer and its load, and what the pickup was rated to tow. The road, for the gouge marks that show where the rim first touched pavement and how far the truck traveled before it turned. The recorder in the 2025 pickup, which likely kept the closing speed and the braking in the seconds before the hit (Delta-V). And the preservation letter, the same day, because a wrecked pickup and a shredded tire go to the salvage yard within the week, and Spoliation is the only thing that brings them back. Inspection is the entry on what it means when the failure had a signature that someone could have read in time.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on U.S. 281 on Friday. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a tire lets go: what it did to the truck in the first second, what was written on its sidewall, who was supposed to look, and whether the emergency had an author.
