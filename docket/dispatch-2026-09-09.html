Over the Barrier

Leer este parte en español →

Two motorcyclists died in San Antonio in two days going over the wall of an elevated freeway ramp. Different ramps, different nights, the same class of structure and the same end. Police cited speed in both. Both investigations are open, and nothing on this page decides them. What the two share is the wall, and the wall is worth understanding.

Sunday, September 6, around midnight. Near U.S. Highway 281 and Interstate 35 on the North Side, police said a motorcyclist traveling northbound at a high rate of speed struck the highway wall, was ejected from the bike, and landed in a parking lot below the elevated roadway. He died at the scene. KSAT has the report.

Monday, September 7, just before 7:30 p.m. On the ramp from Interstate 35 South to Interstate 10 West near Camaron Street, the elevated curve north of downtown that San Antonio calls the Finesilver Curve, police said a motorcyclist failed to make the curve, hit the retaining wall, and was thrown over it to the ground below. He died there. Multiple witnesses are helping the investigation, and the ramp reopened by 10 p.m. KSAT and News 4 San Antonio have the reports.

The backdrop. The Texas Department of Transportation counted 557 motorcyclists killed on Texas roads in 2025, about two every three days, and the state runs a standing campaign asking drivers to look twice for them. Most of those deaths involve another vehicle. These two, as reported, did not. They involved a wall.

What the pattern says. A concrete barrier on a freeway ramp is built to a national crash-test manual, and the manual’s pass criterion is four words: contain and redirect the vehicle. The vehicles in the test are a small car and a pickup at sixty-two miles an hour, and a single-unit truck at fifty-six. There is no motorcycle in the test. The research program that writes the manual’s guidelines says so in its own words, and it opened a project to change that because fixed objects along the road carry a far higher risk of death for a rider than for anyone inside a car. The top of the wall sits about hip-high to a seated rider. A car meets it low and is turned back into its lane. A rider meets it low too, but he is not attached to his machine; when the machine stops, he continues, and on a curve he continues over the top. That is the engineering of Redirect, and the science of what happens to a body after it clears the wall, which is a fall, not a collision, is Over the Top. Why the rider has no cabin to begin with is The Cage.

What the law does with it. Honestly, not much, in most of these. The road belongs to the state, and the height of a wall and the radius of a curve are design decisions the Tort Claims Act leaves to the state’s discretion. A rider’s own speed is comparative fault. What the law does look at is condition and notice: a wall segment left displaced from an earlier hit, a curve advisory sign that was down, lighting the department had been told about, and the department’s own crash history on the ramp. Two riders over the same class of wall in the same city within forty-eight hours is the kind of fact that makes a records request worth sending, and it is the kind of fact this page will follow. The car and motorcycle pages explain how a road case is worked, and Before You Call answers the questions families bring first. No one from either crash is named here, and nothing here assigns fault to anyone.
