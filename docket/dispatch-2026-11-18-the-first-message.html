The First Message

Leer este parte en español →

At about 8:48 Tuesday night, by the company’s account, a boom carried across Deer Park and La Porte. Shortly before 9, the plant posted a message naming three things: explosion, fire, and a process upset. By midnight the first answers were in, and some of them changed as the night went on. This page uses the night to explain how these events unfold, what each early message can and cannot tell you, and what comes after. It decides nothing about what happened.

What was reported. The company’s message on the area’s plant alert system said “explosion, fire, Process/Unit upset occurred” at its facility on Independence Parkway South, which has a La Porte mailing address but sits in Deer Park’s extraterritorial jurisdiction (Houston Chronicle). The company put the time at about 8:48, and the City of Deer Park said it was monitoring reports of a potential tank fire (KPRC). Deer Park posted that “Per Clean Harbors’ recommendation, it is not advised to shelter in place at this time,” and La Porte said “no protective actions are necessary for La Porte residents.” Just after 10:30, La Porte reported that “The fire is reported to be out by responders on scene.” The Harris County Fire Marshal’s Office confirmed to FOX 26 that one injury was reported, and the station said authorities did not say how serious it was (FOX 26). An earlier report had said “No injuries or evacuations were immediately reported” (ABC13). Officials “have not said what may have been inside of the tank that reportedly exploded,” and no cause has been given (FOX 26). The reports above were last read at about 2 a.m. Central on November 18; the next paragraph adds what was reported later that day. The story is still developing.

What came next. On November 18 the Harris County Fire Marshal’s Office reported its first findings. KPRC reported that “hydrocarbon vapors ignited inside a vessel used to treat hazardous liquid waste,” and that investigators found evidence of “two minor fires involving the facility’s hazardous liquid waste treatment process” in the week before, which “involved flammable hydrocarbon vapors escaping from the treatment process and catching fire.” The office did not give the dates of the earlier fires (KPRC; FOX 26). KPRC reported that the office’s origin-and-cause investigation was complete while officials kept evaluating the conditions that let the vapors ignite, and that the vessel had a pressurized nitrogen system; the office “has not explained whether the nitrogen system was operating properly.” FOX 26 reported that “The specific ignition source and the circumstances that allowed the vapors to catch fire remain undetermined,” that the formal report was still pending, and that understanding how the safeguards worked may require further engineering, operational or regulatory review. County Judge Lina Hidalgo said the records available described a tank holding mostly water and trace amounts of chemicals, and said, “What we have on paper is nothing that would cause this kind of an explosion” (KPRC). The worker taken to the hospital was reported in stable condition (FOX 26) and expected to be OK (KHOU, via Yahoo). KPRC reported that the company had said normal operations would not resume until the investigation and necessary remediation were addressed, and the City of Deer Park said Clean Harbors was conducting its own investigation (FOX 26).

Two smaller fires in the same process the week before are the kind of fact the law looks at later, because what a company knew about a hazard, and when, is a question many injury cases ask (Notice). By itself it decides nothing about Tuesday night.

The place. The company describes the site as one that “manages a broad range of hazardous and industrial waste,” and is “Able to manage drums, bulk shipments, lab packs, cylinders and direct-burn streams” (Clean Harbors). A federal approval describes one of its incinerators, called Train 1, as including a “rotary kiln, afterburner, pollution control system,” and authority for “commercial storage and disposal by incineration of liquid and solid polychlorinated biphenyls” (EPA). A plant that takes in many kinds of waste is a plant where the first question, what was burning, matters most and takes longest to answer. We read two state enforcement orders for the site. In both, the company generally denied the allegations: one over storing hazardous waste in four tanks before it had authorization and the required financial assurance, and one over wastewater limits for metals and other pollutants, and a month of missed sampling (TCEQ, 2018 docket; TCEQ, 2021 docket). Neither involves a fire or explosion, neither is about Tuesday night, and two orders are not a full history of the site.

Stage one: the first message. Along the ship channel the first word often comes from the plant itself. The alert system “is a system for community members to view posted messages and alerts from EHCMA member facilities,” operators “try to post urgent messages within 15 minutes of an event,” and “community members should contact and follow instructions provided by local emergency managers” (East Harris County Manufacturers Association). The first message is the company’s own description, written in minutes, before anyone knows much. It tells you something happened and where. It does not tell you what, how much, or why.

Stage two: the first firefighters. At many plants along the channel, the first people fighting a fire are the plant’s own; the mutual-aid group says it is not meant to be a member’s “first responder to minor emergencies.” That group exists to “respond to industrial fire alarms and extinguish fires in the Greater Houston Metropolitan area,” on the idea that “pooling of available resources of its members will provide a more efficient and effective response for firefighting,” and its plan says “Each company participating in this plan must reserve manpower, material, and equipment for its own protection” (Channel Industries Mutual Aid). When a fire outgrows one plant, neighboring plants bring foam trucks.

Stage three: inside or out. Whether a neighborhood stays indoors, leaves, or carries on is a decision for local officials, made on what the plant reports and what the monitors read, and remade as both change (Shelter in Place). On Tuesday, Deer Park cited the company’s recommendation, and both cities pointed to air monitoring. La Porte reported that “Harris County Pollution Control and Deer Park’s third-party air monitoring teams are on scene,” with “no detectable readings at this time,” and that “Winds continue to move from the northeast, away from the City of La Porte” (FOX 26). A reading of nothing is a fact about that instrument, in that place, at that minute, for the chemicals it was built to detect. It is good news, and it is not the same as a full accounting of what was in the air, which is the subject of The Dose. That is why the decision is revisited through the night, and why Until More Information described the same wait a week ago.

What a tank fire nearby once became. Not this one, and nothing here says Tuesday was like it. In 2019 a tank farm fire in Deer Park began with “the release of flammable butane-enriched naphtha vapor from the failed Tank 80-8 circulation pump.” The federal Chemical Safety Board found the tank was “not equipped with remotely operated emergency isolation valves” and had no flammable gas detection, that the design “allowed the fire to spread to other tanks within the tank farm,” and that “The fire burned for three days until it finally was extinguished,” with a shelter-in-place at one point for all of Deer Park. Its chair said: “This disastrous event could have been prevented if proper safeguards had been in place at the facility” (CSB). Some of the recurring questions after a tank fire are about equipment: whether a leak could be detected, whether it could be stopped from the control room, whether the layout let one fire become several.

The people inside the fence. The workers at plants like this one are employees and contractors, and this page runs in Spanish too, for the workers and families who read it that way. Across the country, 1,229 Hispanic or Latino workers died from work injuries in 2024, 842 of them born outside the United States, and their rate “dropped to 4.3 fatalities per 100,000 FTE workers in 2024 from 4.4 in 2023” (BLS; BLS release). OSHA’s policy on its training standards includes a language requirement: “an employer must instruct its employees using both a language and vocabulary that the employees can understand,” and “if an employee does not speak or comprehend English, instruction must be provided in a language the employee can understand” (OSHA). What a worker was taught, and in what language, can be evidence. Whether a hurt worker’s claim runs through workers’ compensation, a suit against an employer without coverage, or a claim against another company on the site depends on who employed the worker and who else was responsible (When the Driver Dies; Third-Party Industrial Claims).

What lasts. The alert messages and their times, the cities’ posts, the monitors’ data, any report the company files with the state, which Texas keeps in a searchable database of air emissions events, and the company’s own records of what was in the unit that reportedly exploded and who was near it. The first night’s accounts change; the records are what stay (Spoliation).

The first message is written in minutes. The full account takes months. In between, a neighbor follows the officials, and a family with someone hurt writes down what they were told, by whom, and when.
