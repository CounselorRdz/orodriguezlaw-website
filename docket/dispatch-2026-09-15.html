Gone

Leer este parte en español →

When a driver hurts someone and leaves, two things happen at once. The law treats the leaving as a wrong of its own, separate from the crash. And a case begins that does not end when the search does. Houston police worked more than one fatal hit-and-run this week. Here is the pattern as the newsrooms reported it, next to the pages that explain what the law does with a driver who is gone.

Friday, before dawn, southwest Houston. Officers found a person in the median at a busy intersection off Wilcrest Drive. That person was taken to a hospital and pronounced dead, and the driver was gone. As of the report the case was open and no one had been arrested, as reported by ABC13.

Monday, late at night, north Houston. Police said a person who may have been in the roadway was struck by a sedan on Little York Road, and the sedan did not stop. The HPD Vehicular Crimes Division took the case, and the driver had not been found, as reported by KPRC. No one here is named.

The backdrop. The state keeps the toll. The Texas Department of Transportation recorded 4,150 people killed on Texas roads in 2024, about one every two hours. The share who die after the driver leaves keeps climbing: the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety counted 2,872 hit-and-run deaths in 2023, seven percent of all crash deaths and the highest share on record, out of more than nine hundred thousand crashes in which a driver left the scene. About one in four of the people killed while walking or on a bicycle die in a crash where the driver drove away.

What the pattern says. A driver leaves for a reason, and the law has always known it. Flight is evidence: a jury may ask why a person ran, and the usual answer, a consciousness of fault, is what can turn ordinary carelessness into conduct a jury is allowed to punish. That is the whole of Flight. And the people most often left on the pavement are the ones with no steel around them. A person on foot has no hood to crush and no cabin to ride inside; the body is the crumple zone, which is what The Wrap and the Ground is about, and it is why nearly a quarter of the walkers and riders who are killed are killed by someone who did not stay.

What the law does with it. Leaving is a crime by itself in Texas. A driver in a crash that hurts or kills someone has a duty to stop, return, and remain, and to give aid (Transportation Code Section 550.021). That is the criminal side, and it turns on whether the driver is caught. The civil side does not. When the driver is never found, the case does not die with the search; it turns to the victim’s own uninsured-motorist coverage, the part of a Texas auto policy that answers for a driver who cannot be identified or cannot pay. Every Texas insurer must offer it, and it can be dropped only in writing, so a family often carries more of it than it knows. So the driver being gone is not the end of the case. It is the moment the case changes rooms. The uninsured and underinsured motorist page, the car accidents page, and its Before You Call companion cover how those claims are built.

No one in this crash is named here, and nothing on this page assigns fault in an open investigation. A dispatch keeps the record of what happened on Texas roads this week, from the outlets that were there, next to the pages that explain it.
