Past the Light

Leer esta página en español →

Headlights were never meant to show you the road. They show you the next two hundred feet of it, and at highway speed that is less road than the car needs to stop. Everything about driving at night, and most of what goes wrong in it, lives in the gap between those two distances.

The reach. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety rates headlights on a test track by how far the beams light the pavement to 5 lux, a little brighter than the end of twilight on a clear day, measured on straightaways and on curves at 40 to 50 mph. Across the vehicles it has tested, low beams reach from 125 feet to 460 feet, and the average, under 180 feet when the ratings began in 2016, had passed 200 by 2021 (IIHS). High beams reach much farther; by the Institute’s measure, the driver of one 2024 model should be able to spot an obstacle in the left lane 202 feet ahead on low beams and 533 feet ahead on high (IIHS). Texas states the high-beam minimum in feet: the upper beam must reveal a person or a vehicle at least 450 feet ahead (Transportation Code Section 547.333). The reach is not a detail of the specification. It is the length of the only road a night driver has.

The arithmetic. A car at 45 mph covers 66 feet a second; at 55, 81; at 70, 103. A driver who sees a shape in the lane needs about a second and a half to recognize it, decide, and get a foot onto the brake, the window The Reaction describes, and in that second and a half the car does not slow at all: 99 feet at 45, 121 at 55, 154 at 70. Then braking takes its own distance, growing with the square of the speed: with hard braking on dry pavement, about 97 feet from 45, 144 from 55, 234 from 70. Add them. From 45 the car stops in about 196 feet, roughly where an average low beam ends. From 55 it needs about 265 feet; from 70, about 388, call it 390, nearly twice the light. Somewhere past 45 on low beams, the stopping distance becomes longer than the lit road, and the driver is doing what reconstructionists call overdriving the headlights: whatever appears at the edge of the beam will be struck before the car can stop, however fast the foot moves. Rain, a worn tire, a downhill grade, and the second and a half that Looking, Not Seeing shows is often longer, all stretch the stopping side of the sum. Nothing stretches the light. Closing Speed does the same arithmetic for two cars meeting, and Seven Times the Energy for what the speed does once the stop fails.

The dark object. The eye does not see distance; it sees contrast. A reflective sign jumps out of the dark at a quarter mile because it throws the beam back. A pedestrian in dark clothes, a tire tread, a deer, a trailer with its lights off, reflect almost nothing, so they appear not at the edge of the beam but well inside it, when the light is finally strong enough to lift them off the asphalt behind them, and the arithmetic above starts from there. The federal count of pedestrians says what that costs. Of 7,314 pedestrians killed in 2023, 77 percent died in the dark, 19 percent in daylight and the rest at dusk or dawn; the deadliest hours were 6 to 9 in the evening, and 74 percent of the deaths were somewhere other than an intersection, out on the road between the corners (NHTSA). The Institute’s crash study found the same thing from the headlight side: vehicles with good-rated headlights had 19 percent fewer nighttime single-vehicle crashes and 23 percent fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes than vehicles with poor-rated ones (IIHS). A low, dark thing in the lane is The Fixed Object with the one quality that makes it worse: it is met late.

The count. About half of the country’s traffic deaths happen in the dark or at dawn or dusk (IIHS). And the brightest tool most drivers own goes unused: observers from the Institute and the University of Michigan found that only 18 percent of the drivers who were far enough from other traffic to use their high beams did so (IIHS). Texas writes the courtesy into law, the low beam within 500 feet of an oncoming car and no high beam within 300 feet behind another (Section 547.333), but nothing in the statute asks a driver to keep them off on an empty road, and most do.

Where the law comes in. Texas defines nighttime as the period from half an hour after sunset until half an hour before sunrise (Section 541.401), requires every vehicle’s lamps to be lit then, and also whenever light or weather keeps a person or vehicle from being clearly seen 1,000 feet ahead (Section 547.302). The speed law never mentions headlights, and does not have to: a driver must hold a speed that is reasonable and prudent “having regard for actual and potential hazards then existing” (Section 545.351), and after dark the hazard that always exists is the edge of the beam. That cuts both ways in a case. The driver who hit the thing in the road is asked why he was going too fast to stop within his light. The one who put it there is asked why it was invisible: the truck stopped on the shoulder without the flashers the federal rules require at once and the triangles they require within ten minutes (49 CFR 392.22), the rules The Shoulder walks through, the carrier whose load came loose, the pedestrian who stepped off the curb in black. Texas divides the answer by percentage rather than ending the case at either question, and bars the injured person only if his own share passes fifty percent (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001), which is Comparative Fault. What decides the percentages is the arithmetic above, worked backward from the marks on the road: the vehicle’s recorder with its speed and the moment of braking, the beam the headlights actually threw, and how far from the object the eye could first have caught it.

Headlights light the next two hundred feet. The law asks whether that was enough for the speed, and whether the thing in the dark was there for the driver to see at all.

The reach and the crash study: Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, Good IIHS headlight ratings linked to lower crash rates (Oct. 14, 2021) (the 5 lux standard on curves and straightaways at 40 to 50 mph; low beams from 125 to 460 feet; the average from under 180 feet to over 200; about 44,000 nighttime single-vehicle crashes in 11 states); IIHS, Headlights (updated July 2026) (about half of traffic deaths in the dark or at dawn or dusk; 19 percent fewer nighttime single-vehicle crashes and 23 percent fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes with good headlights; 202 feet on low beams and 533 on high for one 2024 model; only 18 percent of drivers who could use high beams did). The pedestrian count: NHTSA, Traffic Safety Facts: Pedestrians, 2023 Data, DOT HS 813 727 (June 2025) (7,314 killed; 77 percent in the dark; 74 percent not at intersections; the evening hours). The law: Tex. Transp. Code § 541.401, §§ 547.302 and 547.333, § 545.351; 49 CFR 392.22; Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 33.001. The stopping distances on this page are arithmetic: a second and a half to perceive and react, then braking at seven-tenths of gravity on dry pavement. General information, not legal advice.
