The Load Behind the Cab

Leer este parte en español →

A truck driver spends the whole shift watching the road ahead. When a loaded truck stops hard, in a crash or a panic stop, the collision he cannot see is behind the seat. The freight keeps moving after the truck does not, and the only thing between it and his back is one wall that the rules let be weaker than most people would guess.

On Friday night, at about 8 p.m., two semi-trucks collided on southbound Interstate 35 in Love County, Oklahoma, near mile marker 12, by the weigh station at Marietta, a dozen miles north of the Red River on the freight corridor out of North Texas. The highway patrol reported the crash; the county sheriff’s office confirmed at least one death, said one person was flown to a hospital and another taken by ambulance, and both southbound lanes closed for an extended investigation (KXII; News 9). No one was named, and no cause was released. Nearly fourteen hours earlier, at about 6:20 a.m. Friday on Interstate 10 westbound near FM 365 in Jefferson County, outside Beaumont, a tractor-trailer ran into the back of a box truck that was disabled in the outside lane; a passenger in the box truck died at the hospital, two others were hurt, and the semi’s driver was not, in a crash the state police described to the station as caused by the semi’s driver while the investigation continued (KFDM). Some of the people in that crash are named in the coverage and not here, and nothing on this page decides either one. They are here for a pattern the Docket has written from the front, the flank and the roll, but never from behind the seat.

The wall. Between the cargo and the back of the cab stands the front end structure, the headerboard, and federal law says what it must do. It must rise “to a height of 4 feet above the floor of the vehicle or to a height at which it blocks forward movement of any item or article of cargo being carried on the vehicle, whichever is lower,” and it must hold “a horizontal forward static load equal to one-half (0.5) of the weight of the articles of cargo being transported on the vehicle” if it stands under six feet, or “four-tenths (0.4)” of that weight if it is six feet or taller. It must also resist “penetration by any article of cargo that contacts it when the vehicle decelerates at a rate of 20 feet per second, per second” (49 CFR 393.114). Twenty feet per second per second is about six-tenths of the pull of gravity, the deceleration of a hard stop. The wall, in other words, is built for the brakes. It is not built for the thing that stops a truck faster than its brakes can.

The arithmetic. A crash is a deceleration the brakes never reach. A truck that runs into something solid loses its speed in a fraction of a second, and whatever is loose in the trailer is still moving at the old speed until something stops it. The push is the load’s weight times the deceleration, counted in g’s. Hard braking at half a g asks a forty-thousand-pound load to lean on the wall with twenty thousand pounds, which is exactly the number the rule names. Take a crash that stops the cab at ten times gravity, an illustrative figure: it asks the same load to push with four hundred thousand pounds, twenty times what the wall was certified to hold, and the rule’s own words say what gives: the structure must have “no aperture large enough to permit any article of cargo in contact with the structure to pass through it,” a sentence written because cargo does (49 CFR 393.114). The figures are illustrative arithmetic, not measurements of any crash. What they describe is the second collision inside the first: the force the body meets is the one Delta-V measures, and what a cab does when it is loaded from behind faster than it can fold is the subject of Crush. When two trucks of about equal weight meet, neither has the mass advantage a semi enjoys against a car, and each cab takes a share of the closing speed it is never built for.

The load. The duty is the same one The Tall Load traced to the dock on Friday, read from a different direction. A driver may not operate a commercial motor vehicle, and a carrier may not require or permit him to, unless the cargo is “properly distributed and adequately secured” (49 CFR 392.9), and cargo “must be contained, immobilized or secured in accordance with this subpart to prevent shifting upon or within the vehicle to such an extent that the vehicle’s stability or maneuverability is adversely affected” (49 CFR 393.100). In Shed the danger was cargo that left the truck and landed on the road. In The Tall Load it was cargo stacked high enough to put the truck on its side. Here it is cargo that stays in the trailer and arrives at the cab as a second vehicle inside the first, and the blocking and bracing the rule requires are the only things that decide whether it arrives at the wall as one mass or as freight that had already started moving before the wall ever felt it.

The count. The Insurance Institute’s 2024 figures say who dies inside the truck. “A total of 5,340 people died in large truck crashes in 2024,” and 920 of them were occupants of the trucks themselves, 389 of those, 42 percent, in multiple-vehicle crashes. “Forty-seven percent of large truck occupant deaths in 2024 occurred in crashes in which their vehicles rolled over.” Of 781 large-truck drivers killed, 349 were wearing a belt, 293 were not, and for 139 no one knows (IIHS, Fatality Facts 2024). The cab is the most dangerous seat in the fleet, and the man in it is the one person in the crash who had no say in how the trailer behind him was packed.

The stopped truck. The Beaumont crash carries its own rule. A commercial vehicle disabled in a lane is not allowed to simply sit there: its driver “shall immediately activate the vehicular hazard warning signal flashers” and, “as soon as possible, but in any event within 10 minutes,” put out the warning triangles the rules require, ten feet, a hundred feet and, on a divided highway, two hundred feet toward the traffic coming up behind (49 CFR 392.22). Whether that was done, and when, is one of the first questions in a case like it, and it is the subject of Warning Devices. The other is the one Looking, Not Seeing explains: why a driver can look at a stopped truck and not register it as stopped until the road is gone. Texas asks every driver for “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” from no one (Transportation Code Section 545.351), and it lets a jury divide a wreck like this one among everyone who had a hand in it, the driver who did not stop, the carrier that sent him, and the truck that was stopped in the lane, in whole-number percentages (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003; Comparative Fault).

What the case asks. What the trailer carried and how it was secured, which is the bill of lading, the weight tickets, the loading photographs and the blocking and bracing, or their absence. What the headerboard was rated for, and whether the trailer had one at all, since the rule reaches only cargo “in contact with the front end structure” and allows substitute devices in its place (49 CFR 393.114). How fast each truck was going and when each one braked, which the engine recorders kept (the black box). For the truck that was stopped, when the flashers went on and when the triangles went out, if they did. What each driver’s hours were (Logbook). And whether the cab held, because a driver who survived the first collision and died in the second has a case against the people who packed the trailer, and the proof of it is the freight, which is swept up and sold before anyone thinks to ask for it (Spoliation).

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides what happened on I-35 or on I-10. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law asks when a loaded truck stops faster than its brakes could have stopped it: what was behind the seat, who put it there, what held it, and what the rule had let the wall be.
