Two a Day

Leer este parte en español →

Texas loses two motorcyclists on an average day. San Antonio lost two of them inside about forty-eight hours this weekend, both thrown from the bike, both on the freeways just north of downtown. Neither report mentions a second vehicle. This is the pattern as the newsrooms reported it, then connected to the pages that explain why a rider has so little margin and what the law does with it.

Sunday, September 6, around midnight. A motorcyclist heading north near U.S. 281 and Interstate 35 was, in the words of San Antonio police, driving at a high rate of speed when he collided with the highway wall. He was ejected and landed in a parking lot below, and was pronounced dead at the scene. The investigation is ongoing. KSAT has the report.

Monday, September 7, just before 7:30 p.m. On the elevated ramp that carries Interstate 35 South onto Interstate 10 West, the curve locals know by name, a motorcyclist went over the barricade and fell to the ground below. He was pronounced dead at the scene. Police said several witnesses stopped and are helping investigators, and the circumstances remain under investigation. KSAT and News 4 San Antonio have the reports.

The backdrop. The Texas Department of Transportation’s own count: 557 riders killed and 2,468 seriously injured on Texas roads in 2025, two deaths on an average day. More than half of the fatal crashes involve another vehicle, and thirty-seven percent happen at an intersection. The TxDOT motorcycle safety page carries the figures and the Share the Road campaign built on them.

What the pattern says. A car carries its occupant inside a structure built to fold and absorb the hit. A motorcycle carries nothing, and the rider stands in for the crumple zone; that is the whole of The Cage. What kills is usually not the first impact but the second one, the rider against whatever the bike threw him into, which is why Thrown Clear exists: being separated from the machine is not luck, it is the moment the body meets the wall, the pavement, or the ground below a ramp at the speed it was still carrying. Both crashes this weekend ended that way. Nothing here decides either of them. It explains why a rider’s crash so rarely ends in an ambulance ride instead of a scene.

What the law does with it. In the half of fatal rider crashes that involve another vehicle, the driver’s sentence is nearly always the same, I never saw the motorcycle, and Failure to Yield explains why that sentence is a confession, not a defense. In every rider case, the insurer’s first move is the rider’s own conduct, the speed, the helmet, the gear, and Comparative Fault explains that in Texas a rider’s share of the fault divides the recovery rather than ending it, until it passes fifty percent. When no other vehicle is involved, the questions turn to the road itself, the barrier, the curve, the lighting, and a claim about a road is a claim against a government, with its own short notice deadlines; the car accidents page and its Before You Call companion cover how those cases work.

No one in these two crashes is named here, and nothing on this page assigns fault in an open investigation. A dispatch keeps the record of what happened on Texas roads this week, from the outlets that were there, next to the pages that explain it.
