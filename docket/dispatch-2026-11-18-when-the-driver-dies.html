When the Driver Dies

Leer este parte en español →

When a truck crash kills the man driving the truck, his family is a crash family too. The law gives them more than one door, and which ones are open turns first on a question most people never think to ask: whose employee was he?

The pattern. A loaded rig leaves the road in the dark, through a rail or off a bridge, and the driver does not survive. To the other traffic he was a set of lights. At home he was somebody’s husband or father, and the people he left are asking what every family asks after a crash. This page names no one and decides nothing about any crash.

The scale. There were 5,070 fatal work injuries recorded in the United States in 2024, and transportation incidents, the federal category that takes in highway crashes, “continue to be the most frequent type of fatal event, accounting for 38.2 percent of all occupational fatalities in 2024.” Workers in transportation and material moving occupations had 1,391 of those deaths (BLS).

If the employer carried workers’ compensation. Then the family’s remedy against that employer is, with one exception written into the statute, the compensation benefits. Recovery of benefits “is the exclusive remedy of an employee” covered by the insurance, or of a legal beneficiary, “against the employer or an agent or employee of the employer for the death of or a work-related injury sustained by the employee” (Labor Code Section 408.001). The carrier “shall pay death benefits to the legal beneficiary if a compensable injury to the employee results in death,” at 75 percent of the employee’s average weekly wage, subject to the state’s weekly maximum (Section 408.181). The state lists who may receive them: a surviving spouse, minor children, children under 25 enrolled in an accredited college or university, dependent grandchildren and other dependent family members, and parents who were not dependents only when no eligible dependent family member survives. Burial expenses may be paid too, to the person who paid them (Texas Department of Insurance).

The exception. It is written for the death cases. The exclusive-remedy rule “does not prohibit the recovery of exemplary damages by the surviving spouse or heirs of the body of a deceased employee whose death was caused by an intentional act or omission of the employer or by the employer’s gross negligence” (Section 408.001). Gross Negligence explains how high that bar is.

If the employer carried no coverage. Then the family can sue it for negligence, and the employer cannot argue that the driver was careless too, that he assumed the risk, or that a coworker was to blame. The family must still “prove negligence of the employer or of an agent or servant of the employer acting within the general scope of the agent’s or servant’s employment,” and the statute gives the employer two defenses: that the injury was caused by an act of the employee intended to bring about the injury, or that it happened “while the employee was in a state of intoxication” (Section 406.033). On the Job walked through that difference for workers on the ground.

Everyone else. Neither answer closes the case against anyone else. “An employee or legal beneficiary may seek damages from a third party who is or becomes liable to pay damages for an injury or death that is compensable under this subtitle and may also pursue a claim for workers’ compensation benefits” (Section 417.001). The compensation carrier steps into the family’s place against that third party and has a right to be repaid from what is recovered, limited to the benefits it paid or assumed and reduced by any share of responsibility assigned to the employer. The third party may be the other driver, the company that driver worked for, a company whose part failed, or whoever built or kept the road, each on its own proof (Wrongful Death; Respondeat Superior).

The first question. If he owned his truck and hauled under a lease, whether he was anyone’s employee is the first thing to sort out, because it decides which of these doors is open. So is whether his employer carried coverage at all. The answers are in paper the company keeps: the lease, the pay records, the policy (Logbook). Each door has its own deadline (Limitations).

A man who drove for a living was somebody’s reason to listen for the truck in the driveway. What his family is owed is not a favor anyone grants. It is written into Texas law in more than one place, and the first job is finding which places apply.
