I Never Saw Them

Leer este parte en español →

A driver who stops after a crash in the dark almost always says the same thing. They came out of nowhere. I never saw them. Texas law does not ask whether he saw the person. It asks whether he was driving so that he could.

Before daylight on Wednesday, at about 5:54 a.m., a pedestrian was struck and killed in the westbound lanes of the 1600 block of East State Highway 71 near Brandt Drive, by the Austin airport. Medics tried to resuscitate the person, an adult, who was pronounced dead at the scene, and two westbound lanes were closed, the Texas Department of Transportation said (CBS Austin). Nothing has been released about the vehicle, the driver or the person who died, and no investigating agency has been named. No one is named here, the investigation is open, and nothing on this page decides it. It is here for the pattern it belongs to, which is the most common pedestrian death there is: a person on foot, on the road away from a corner, in the dark.

What the driver says. When someone is struck in the dark on a highway, the account that reaches the report first is usually the driver’s, and it is usually a version of one sentence: they came out of nowhere, I never saw them in time. Often it is true that he did not see them. That is not the end of the question. It can be the start of it. The federal count says where these crashes happen: of 7,314 pedestrians killed in 2023, 77 percent died in the dark and 74 percent died somewhere other than an intersection (NHTSA), out on the road between the corners, where no signal tells a driver that a person might be there.

What the law asks instead. Texas does not set a driver’s duty by what he happened to notice. An operator “may not drive at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing,” must drive with regard for “actual and potential hazards then existing,” shall control speed “as necessary to avoid colliding with another person or vehicle that is on or entering the highway in compliance with law and the duty of each person to use due care,” and shall drive at an appropriate reduced speed where “a special hazard exists with regard to traffic, including pedestrians” (Transportation Code Section 545.351). A dark road is a circumstance. A person who might be on it is a potential hazard. A second section adds the duty that does not depend on a crosswalk at all: an operator shall “exercise due care to avoid colliding with a pedestrian on a roadway” and “give warning by sounding the horn when necessary” (Section 552.008). The federal model manual that commercial driver tests are built on puts the speed rule in one sentence, and the physics in it applies to every car on the road: with low beams “you can see ahead about 250 feet,” and “[y]ou must adjust your speed to keep your stopping distance within your sight distance. This means going slowly enough to be able to stop within the range of your headlights” (Model Commercial Driver’s License Manual). The arithmetic says what that costs at highway speed. At sixty miles an hour a vehicle covers eighty-eight feet every second; an ordinary second and a half of seeing and reacting is 132 feet, and hard braking on dry pavement takes about 170 more, call it 300 feet to a stop, fifty feet past the end of the low beams, so a person standing at the edge of the light is hit at better than thirty miles an hour. At forty-five the same driver stops in about 200 feet, inside them. That is the whole of what traffic engineers mean by overdriving your headlights, and it is why Past the Light says a driver on an unlit road at night is betting that nothing is in the dark beyond his lights. “I never saw them in time” and “I was going too fast for what I could see” can be the same sentence told from two sides, and Looking, Not Seeing explains why a driver who was looking still did not see. What speed does to the person on foot is not in dispute: the average pedestrian’s risk of death reaches 10 percent at an impact speed of 23 miles an hour, 50 percent at 42 and 90 percent at 58 (AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety), which is why The Wrap and the Ground and The Hood Line are pages about speed as much as about the body.

When the person on foot made a mistake. Walking on a highway in dark clothing, crossing where there is no crosswalk, stepping off a shoulder: Texas weighs all of it, and then divides rather than ends. A claimant may not recover “if his percentage of responsibility is greater than 50 percent” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001); below that line the court reduces the damages “by a percentage equal to the claimant’s percentage of responsibility” (Section 33.012) and the case goes on, which is Comparative Fault. The driver’s duty of due care to a person on the roadway does not end where the crosswalk does, and a driver who leaves is a different case, the one Render Aid describes.

Why the proof is physical. Because the driver’s word is often the only spoken account, these cases are not decided by it. The height of the damage on the vehicle shows where on the body it struck. The distance the person was carried helps estimate the speed, which is the reconstruction Delta-V describes. The event data recorder may have kept the speed and the moment the brake went down, if it went down at all. The aim and condition of the headlights, the lighting on the road, the weather, and any camera that happened to be pointed at the spot are the witnesses that do not change their story under questioning. They also disappear: a vehicle gets repaired, a recorder gets written over, a scene gets swept and reopened to traffic by morning. The first job is to keep that record before it is gone, which is why Spoliation starts the morning after.
