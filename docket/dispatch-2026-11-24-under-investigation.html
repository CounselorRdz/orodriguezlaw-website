Under Investigation

Leer este parte en español →

A tanker that authorities said was hauling fuel overturned and burned Tuesday afternoon where the Eastex Freeway meets the North Loop, and the driver died. By evening some lanes were open again. The answers will take longer. Here is what was reported Tuesday, what was not, and how an investigation like this one runs.

What was reported. The crash happened about 2 p.m. Tuesday, November 24, at I-69, the Eastex Freeway, and I-610, the North Loop, in north Houston. FOX 26 reported that, according to police, the tanker was going west on I-610, took the ramp to northbound I-69, lost control and flipped, and that police said the driver did not survive (FOX 26). The Houston Chronicle reported that the tanker was hauling fuel, which was leaking, according to authorities. A Houston Fire Department deputy chief said some of the fuel spilled but there was no sign it reached a sewer or waterway, and the department said the air quality in the area was fine and no shelter-in-place order was needed (Houston Chronicle). Just before 6 p.m., Houston police said two of the three outbound lanes of the Eastex were open; the flyovers from I-610 remained closed pending inspection and cleanup, and the northbound lanes near the North Loop remained shut down (FOX 26).

What was not. None of the reports said why the tanker lost control. KHOU put it plainly: “It is unclear what caused the tanker truck to roll over” (KHOU). The driver had not been publicly identified. The deputy chief said at a news conference that the tanker’s contents and carrier were still unidentified as of about 4 p.m. (Chronicle). And the first reports did not agree on whether anyone else was involved. The Chronicle reported that no other vehicles were involved; KHOU, citing Houston TranStar, reported that two vehicles were involved; FOX 26 reported that a Jeep was following close behind the truck and that, police said, its driver escaped and was not injured. Sorting out reports like these is part of what an investigation is for.

Why a loaded truck tips on a ramp is a subject the Night Docket has taken up before: a loaded tractor tips before it slides, at about four tenths of the force of gravity (The Ramp), and a loaded tanker, with thousands of gallons carried high above the axles, is the extreme case (High and Heavy). Whether that explains Tuesday is a question for the investigators, and this Dispatch does not answer it.

How the investigation runs. The deputy chief said Tuesday afternoon that the investigation was expected to take three to four hours (Chronicle). The Chronicle did not say what that estimate covered. Whatever happens at the scene, what comes after is mostly paper, and much of it runs on deadlines the law sets.

The crash report. When a crash injures or kills someone, the officer who investigates must make a written report, and it must be filed electronically with the Texas Department of Transportation no later than the tenth day after the crash (Transportation Code Section 550.062). The report is not posted for the public. TxDOT says crash reports “are not available for online viewing by the general public.” On written request and payment of a fee, it releases them to people directly concerned in the crash or with a proper interest in it, a list that includes “any person who may sue because of death resulting from the accident”; anyone else receives a redacted copy (TxDOT). The report is the officer’s account. Fault is decided later.

The road and the bridge. When the fire is out, an interchange that burned is a question for engineers. The fire department’s deputy chief said TxDOT would look at any damage to the road and the bridge (Chronicle), and FOX 26 reported that a crew would inspect the roadway to make sure its structural integrity was not compromised in the fire.

The hazardous materials reports. A tanker hauling fuel, as authorities said this one was, brings federal paperwork with it. The federal hazardous materials rules require a telephone report to the National Response Center as soon as practical, and no later than 12 hours after the incident, when, as a direct result of a hazardous material, a person is killed or a major transportation artery or facility is closed or shut down for one hour or more (49 C.F.R. 171.15). A written report on DOT Form F 5800.1 is due within 30 days of discovery of the incident: for an incident that required the call, for an unintentional release, and for certain structural damage to a specification cargo tank of 1,000 gallons or more carrying a hazardous material, even with no release. The narrow exceptions never apply when the call was required (49 C.F.R. 171.16). Both duties fall on each person in physical possession of the material. Whether either applies here depends on facts not yet public, starting with what was in the tank. What matters to a family is that these reports go to the government, not only into a company’s files.

The truck’s own records. A modern truck keeps its own record: the engine control module logs speed, throttle and hard braking around a crash, and the electronic logging device records the driver’s hours (Truck Black Box and ELD Data). After a fire, what survived is one of the things the investigation has to find out. The carrier’s files matter too, including maintenance and the driver’s qualification file. And the duty to preserve evidence does not wait for a lawsuit to be filed (Spoliation).

The family. When a crash kills the truck’s own driver, the family is a crash family too, and which claims are open turns first on whose employee the driver was and whether that employer carried workers’ compensation (When the Driver Dies). This Dispatch names no one and decides nothing about this crash.

The first day of a crash like this gives a time, a place and a loss. The why comes later, from the report, the engineers, the federal filings and the truck’s own records. Most of that evidence starts out in someone else’s hands.
