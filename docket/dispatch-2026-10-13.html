The Precaution

Leer este parte en español →

Within hours of a rider’s death, the operator closed the same kind of ride at its parks across the country. The public read it as a confession. The law reads it as something else, and for a reason worth knowing.

Shortly before 4 p.m. on Monday, at an amusement park outside Atlanta, a man died in a fall from a roller coaster and several other people were injured; county police issued the statement, and a spokesperson for the operator said the company was “deeply saddened” and “cooperating fully with local authorities as they complete their investigation” (CBS News Atlanta). The same day, the operator temporarily closed nine roller coasters at six of its parks, one of them in Canada, and a spokeswoman said, “Out of an abundance of caution, we have temporarily closed similar attractions across our parks” (WSB-TV). No one is named here, not the man, the park or the ride; no official account of how he came out of his seat has been released, the investigation is open, and nothing on this page decides it. It is here for what the closing means in a courtroom, which is less than the public thinks and more than the defense wants.

What the closing looks like. When a company takes a thing out of service right after someone is hurt on it, the instinct is to call it an admission. They would not have shut it down, the thinking goes, if it had been safe. Juries feel that pull, and so does everyone else. It is the most human inference there is, the fix proving the flaw.

What the law does with it. Texas does not let that inference carry a case. “When measures are taken that would have made an earlier injury or harm less likely to occur, evidence of the subsequent measures is not admissible to prove: negligence; culpable conduct; a defect in a product or its design; or a need for a warning or instruction” (Texas Rule of Evidence 407(a)). The reason is not that the closure means nothing. It is that the law would rather have the precaution than the proof. If every repair, recall and shutdown became an admission, the careful company would be punished for its care, and the next one would wait. So the rule keeps the later fix away from the jury when its only use is to say the defendant was at fault.

What still gets in. The same rule has a door. The court “may admit this evidence for another purpose, such as impeachment” or, if the point is disputed, for “proving ownership, control, or the feasibility of precautionary measures” (Rule 407(a)). An operator that closes nine rides on the day of a death has shown, in its own hand, that stopping them was feasible, and a witness who swears a ride was as safe as it could be made may be confronted with what his company did that same day. The rule also carries a second half that cuts the other way for the maker: “A manufacturer’s written notification to a purchaser of a defect in one of its products is admissible against the manufacturer to prove the defect” (Rule 407(b)). The closure is neither the confession the public hears nor the nothing the defense wants. It is proof of what was possible, kept back from proving fault, which is the same shape as the argument Guard makes about a safeguard that exists and is not required.

Where the case actually is. That is the turn. In a ride case the evidence that decides it is the operator’s own, and it was made before anyone was hurt. In Texas the annual inspection belongs to the insurer: a ride must be inspected “at least once a year by an insurer or a person with whom the insurer has contracted,” and the inspection certificate and the insurance policy are filed with the state each year (Occupations Code Section 2151.101). The operator “shall maintain accurate records of each injury caused by the ride that results in death or requires medical treatment” and “shall file an injury report with the commissioner on a quarterly basis” (Section 2151.103). So the restraint checks, the maintenance logs, the sensor data and the injury reports are the witnesses, which is what Airtime said about the weightless instant a coaster is sold for, and what a body does against a restraint that fails is The Third Collision. What a company did after the fact is a narrow kind of evidence. What it recorded before is the case, and the first job is to keep that record from being lost, which is why Spoliation sends the letter the first week.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular injury. A dispatch keeps the record of what the law does with a fix that comes too late, next to the pages on the ride sold for its weightless instant and on the evidence that has to be preserved before it is gone.
