The Swerve

Leer este parte en español →

On a dark rural highway the hazard is often not another car. It is an animal that steps out of the brush with no warning, and a driver who has half a second to decide whether to hit it or turn away. What follows, too often, is one vehicle, off the pavement, on its roof.

Early Monday, about two in the morning, a vehicle on U.S. 67 south of Marfa rolled over after hitting a javelina that came onto the road. Three people were inside, none, the sheriff’s office said, with injuries that threatened their lives, and one was taken in for a possible broken arm. The sheriff’s office asked drivers to use caution at night and in the early morning, when wildlife may enter the roadway (CBS7). No one in that vehicle is named here, and nothing on this page is about that crash. It is about the pattern it belongs to.

The count. The federal highway agency’s report to Congress on wildlife crashes put the national figure at one to two million collisions a year between vehicles and large animals, about 26,000 injuries and about 200 deaths, roughly one reported collision in twenty, with deer the vast majority and the peaks in the early morning and the evening, in spring and above all in fall (FHWA). The Insurance Institute counts 210 people killed in collisions with animals in 2024, up from 89 in 1975 (IIHS). Texas keeps its own ledger: a 2019 white paper for the state highway department counted an average of 7,585 reported crashes a year involving a wild or domestic animal, and 160 people killed in them from 2010 to 2017 (CTR, UT Austin). The report to Congress also names the other version of this crash, the one in which the animal is never struck, “the driver swerves to avoid a deer and collides with a guardrail,” and it warns that “swerving to avoid a WVC may result in a higher-severity collision” than the one the driver was trying to avoid (FHWA).

Why it rolls. A rollover is rarely the tires simply letting go. Federal crash researchers find that about 95 percent of rollovers in single-vehicle crashes are tripped and only 5 percent untripped, and their definition is the whole mechanism: “A tripped rollover event occurs when a vehicle runs off the road and is tripped by a ditch, soft soil, a curb or other object causing a vehicle to roll over” (NHTSA). The sequence on a rural road is short. The driver turns hard, or the strike itself knocks the vehicle off its line, the vehicle slides sideways, and the tires leave the pavement onto a soft shoulder or the lip of a ditch. At that instant the sideways force that was sliding the vehicle meets resistance down low, at the wheels, while all the weight above keeps moving the old way, and the vehicle pivots over its own tires the way a running person goes over a curb he never saw. Height makes it easier: the same analysis finds the height of the center of gravity and the width of the track “highly correlated with rollovers,” which is why a tall pickup or SUV goes over where a low car would slide, the point Rolled made about the stability number behind every window sticker. Speed makes it worse, because the energy that has to go somewhere climbs with the square of the speed, which is what Seven Times the Energy is about. And once the vehicle is over, the crash is no longer one impact; it is the roof, the window and a belt that was built for a different direction, which is The Roll. In 2014, the year the agency’s report cites, rollovers were part of 18.9 percent of fatal crashes (NHTSA).

What the law asks. Here the law looks at the reaction as closely as the hazard. Texas has an instruction for the driver who was hit with a danger he did not create. When a person is confronted by an emergency “arising suddenly and unexpectedly, which was not proximately caused by negligence on his part and which, to a reasonable person, requires immediate action without time for deliberation,” his conduct “is not negligence or failure to use ordinary care if, after such emergency arises, he acts as a person of ordinary prudence would have acted under the same or similar circumstances” (Reinhart v. Young). That is the rule Sudden Emergency walks through, and the Texas Supreme Court lists it, with unavoidable accident, among the inferential-rebuttal instructions: not a separate defense the jury votes on, but an instruction that tells jurors they do not have to blame a party when conditions beyond that party’s control caused the crash (Dillard v. Texas Electric Cooperative). A swerve that ends on the roof is not the driver’s fault by itself. The rule has its edges, and they are written into it. The emergency cannot be one the driver’s own carelessness set up, and a speed that is fine at noon can be too fast for a dark rural road where animals are a known condition: every driver must keep to a speed “reasonable and prudent under the conditions and having regard for actual and potential hazards then existing,” and must slow down “on a narrow or winding roadway” and wherever “a special hazard exists” (Transportation Code Section 545.351). A javelina on U.S. 67 at two in the morning is an actual hazard the way a pedestrian is on a city street. And when a vehicle runs off the road and trips, the animal hides a second question: whether the drop-off, the soft shoulder or the edge the tire caught was itself a hazard the road’s keeper left in place, which is the special-defect question Single-Vehicle walks through. Texas divides responsibility by percentage rather than ending a case over one hard half-second choice, and the driver’s own share ends the claim only if it passes fifty percent (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001), which is Comparative Fault.

What the proof looks like. It is on the shoulder, for about as long as it takes to grade it: the tire marks where the pavement ends, the furrow where a rim dug in, the first photographs, the vehicle before it is repaired, and the record the vehicle itself kept of its speed and the turn of the wheel in the last seconds. The animal is almost never there to testify, which is why the questions above are answered by the road.

No one is named here, and nothing on this page decides a particular crash. A dispatch keeps the record of what the numbers say about Texas roads, next to the pages that explain why a swerve ends on the roof and why the law judges the reaction, not only the hazard.
