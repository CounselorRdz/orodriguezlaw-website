Squared

Almost every crash this strand writes about has a number in it that no one argues over, because it is the one the driver set alone. Not the weather, not the other car, not the road. The speed. Texas just counted a full year of what that number does, and speed does not add to the danger so much as multiply it. Here is the pattern the agency reported, next to the pages that explain why a faster crash is not a little worse but a great deal worse.

The count. The Texas Department of Transportation’s own figures for 2025: speeding was a factor in more than one hundred fifty thousand crashes, 1,247 people were killed, and 5,652 were seriously injured. By the agency’s reckoning speed is the leading factor in deadly crashes, tied to roughly one third of all traffic deaths in the state. Through the late summer, state troopers and city police ran a statewide enforcement push built on those numbers, the recurring Operation Slowdown, for the plain reason that no other single choice a driver makes moves the count as far.

What the pattern says. Speed is dangerous in a way that is easy to underestimate, because the harm does not climb in a straight line. The energy a crash spends, first into the steel and then into the body, rises with the square of the speed: a car going a third faster carries close to twice the energy, not a third more, which is what Closing Speed is about. And the measure that tracks injury is not the speed but the stop, the change in velocity the body absorbs in a fraction of a second, which is what Delta-V is about. The distance to stop grows the same way: the moment it takes to reach the brake is fixed, but the braking distance grows with the square of the speed, so a small increase turns a gap you could have stopped inside into one you cannot, the arithmetic The Reaction walks through. Speed is the number under all the others.

What the law does with it. Texas law meets speed in two places. A driver who passes the posted limit has handed the case a breach, the statutory violation that is the whole of Negligence Per Se. But the posted number is a ceiling, not a safe harbor. Section 545.351 of the Transportation Code says a driver may not go faster than is reasonable and prudent under the conditions then existing, with regard for the actual and potential hazards. In rain, in fog, in a work zone, in stopped traffic, the lawful speed and the safe speed are not the same number, so a driver can breach the standard without ever touching the limit. That is why I was under the speed limit answers a ticket but not a case. The car accidents and serious personal injury pages, with their Before You Call companion, cover how speed is proven, from the black box to the length of the skid.
