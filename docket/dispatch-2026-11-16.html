The Ones Who Didn’t Run

Leer este parte en español →

When a police chase ends in a wreck, the people carried out of it are not always the ones who chose to run. They are the passengers who had no say, and the drivers and people on foot the fleeing car met on the way.

The pattern. A driver will not stop for the lights behind him. An officer follows. Somewhere down the road it ends the way these endings go: a rollover, a fire, a head-on in a lane the fleeing car crossed into. When the hurt are counted, the count can include people who chose none of it: a passenger buckled in beside the driver, a family in the next car, a person waiting at a corner. This page names no one and decides nothing about any chase.

Whose choice it was. Texas makes the choice to run an offense of its own. A driver commits one if he “wilfully fails or refuses to bring the vehicle to a stop” or “flees, or attempts to elude, a pursuing police vehicle” when given “a visual or audible signal to bring the vehicle to a stop” (Transportation Code Section 545.421). In the civil case, Texas divides responsibility among the people whose conduct caused the harm: the trier of fact, usually a jury, sets a percentage for each claimant, defendant, settling person and designated responsible third party according to each one’s “causing or contributing to cause in any way the harm” (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003), and a claimant whose own share is “greater than 50 percent” recovers nothing (Section 33.001). The choice to run was the fleeing driver’s. A passenger who had no hand in it, like another driver or a person on foot who was only there, has none of that choice to be assigned, though the jury still sets every share and the other side may still ask about a seat belt (The Passenger; Comparative Fault).

The narrow door. The harder question is whether the agency whose officer gave chase can be made to answer too, and the honest answer is that the door is narrow. When a government vehicle is in the story, a Texas claim against the government goes through the Tort Claims Act (The One Door); a federal claim over the chase itself is narrower still (Terminated). And the Act steps aside for emergencies. It does not apply to a claim arising from an employee’s action “while responding to an emergency call or reacting to an emergency situation” if the action complied with “the laws and ordinances applicable to emergency action,” or, where there is no such law, if it was “not taken with conscious indifference or reckless disregard for the safety of others” (Section 101.055(2); The Siren).

The case on point. In 2022 the Supreme Court of Texas decided a case about a chase in San Antonio. During a joint task force investigation, an officer tried to stop a driver, who sped off; the chase ran onto Loop 1604 and IH-35, and the fleeing car collided with another family’s vehicle. The impact left both parents with “incapacitating injuries” and “killed two of their young children.” The Court held that under the emergency exception the Act “does not apply to” the family’s claims, and it dismissed them for lack of jurisdiction. The family bore “the burden of negating” the exception. The Court wrote that “a police department’s internal policies, in and of themselves, are not ‘laws’ or ‘ordinances,’” that “Exceeding the speed limit is part and parcel of a police chase,” and that an officer’s “speeding and swerving during a pursuit are not inherently reckless”; recklessness takes “subjective awareness of an extreme risk,” more than a “momentary judgment lapse” (City of San Antonio v. Maspero). The Court did not reach whether the injuries arose from the officer’s use of a vehicle; the exception decided the case first. A family should know that wall is there before anyone offers to climb it.

Where the case goes. The road that stays open runs through the driver who ran. His responsibility is real even when his insurance is not. When the at-fault driver carried no coverage, or too little, a Texas auto policy’s uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage “protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from owners or operators of uninsured or underinsured motor vehicles” for bodily injury and death, and it is part of the policy unless an insured named in it “rejects the coverage in writing” (Insurance Code Section 1952.101). Which policy answers for whom is its own question, and for a passenger it starts with the policies The Passenger walks through. Where a claim against a government is possible, the Act’s notice clock, six months or less, starts the day of the crash (The One Door).

The record. The proof of a chase is in the machines: the patrol car’s video, the radio traffic, the data in both vehicles, the timing of every turn. None of it keeps itself. Ask for it in writing, early (Spoliation).

The Passenger said the person riding along has no hand in the crash. The Siren and The One Door said that when the government is driving there is one door, and the emergency exception narrows it further. A chase is where those meet.
