Cicero

SUBJECT: Marcus Tullius Cicero, 106 to 43 BC. Consul of Rome, novus homo, and the greatest advocate the ancient world produced.
FILM STUDIED: The First Catilinarian Oration, delivered November 8, 63 BC, in the Temple of Jupiter Stator. Roughly forty minutes on his feet, in front of the Senate, with the man he was accusing sitting in the room.
SIGNATURE: The direct address. The strategic concession. The killing pause.

Twenty-one centuries before Lincoln did it in two minutes, Cicero was already teaching the same craft.

The setting was a real conspiracy. Lucius Catiline, a defeated candidate for consul with a following of veterans and debtors, was actively plotting to overthrow the Republic. Cicero, then consul, had intelligence of the plot but not enough hard evidence for a formal prosecution. He convened the Senate, walked in, and delivered a speech aimed at a single person: the conspirator seated among the senators.

Study the film. Three moves.

Move One: The Direct Address.

The opening line is famous, and it should be. “Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra?” How long, Catiline, will you abuse our patience? He does not begin with an argument. He begins with a name. He does not describe the accused. He turns and speaks to him. In the Roman Senate, this was staggering. Formal speeches were addressed to the body. Cicero addressed one man in front of the body, and every other man in the room understood he was now a witness to an accusation, not a listener to a speech.

The move made Catiline sit inside the argument. Every rebuttal he might have offered, every innocence he might have claimed, had to be measured against the fact that he was, in that moment, being addressed by name, in public, without denying anything.

The steal: in your next opening statement, address the defense’s central lie by name. Not obliquely. Not as “the argument you are about to hear.” Name it, and turn toward the person who will be selling it. The jury will notice who flinches.

Move Two: The Strategic Concession.

Cicero does not overstate his evidence. He concedes what he cannot prove. He says openly that some in Rome might still doubt the depth of the conspiracy, and that reasonable men could disagree about the exact scope of what Catiline planned. And then, having conceded the doubt, he uses the concession as leverage: even accepting every doubt, even granting every question, the pattern of behavior alone is enough.

This is what Scalia and Garner will teach, in Making Your Case, two thousand years later. Confront your weakest point yourself, early, in your own words. Cicero was doing it in a temple.

The steal: in your next argument, name the weakest part of your case out loud. Say what a reasonable juror might legitimately doubt. Then show why the case still holds. The lawyer who never concedes anything is presumed to be hiding everything.

Move Three: The Killing Pause.

The First Catilinarian is a speech built on silences. Cicero would ask a question, then not answer it. He would name a rumor, then let it hang. He would describe what Catiline had allegedly said in a private meeting, then stop and let the Senate look at Catiline’s face. In a room of politicians, the silences were louder than the sentences.

The historical record says that during the speech, the senators seated near Catiline moved away from him. The image that survives is Catiline sitting alone on a bench that had been full at the start of the oration. Cicero never called for it. The pauses did.

The steal: rehearse the silences in your closing the way you rehearse the sentences. The pause after a devastating question is when the jury decides. Do not fill it with your voice.

A Warning from the Film.

Cicero won that day. Catiline left Rome the next morning. But Cicero also spent the rest of his life defending the constitutional order against men who did not accept it, and in 43 BC, on the orders of Mark Antony, he was killed. His head and hands were nailed to the Rostra, the speaker’s platform in the Roman Forum, as a warning to advocates who used their voices against power.

The warning is worth carrying: rhetoric wins arguments. It does not, by itself, save republics. The system has to defend the advocate, or eventually the advocate cannot defend the system.

The Drill.

Take your next opening. Add one direct address to a specific person. Add one open concession of your weakest point. Add one pause where you would have rushed. Time yourself. That is a Cicero opening in a Texas courtroom.

Twenty-one centuries. The temple is gone. The moves are still working.
