Brake Fade

Leer esta página en español →

A brake is a machine for turning motion into heat. Everything about a long grade follows from that sentence, because on a hill the motion is not the truck’s alone. The hill keeps making more of it, and the brakes have to turn it all away or keep it as temperature.

The conversion. A loaded truck at 60 miles an hour carries a quantity of energy that its brakes must take out of it to stop: for an 80,000-pound rig at 88 feet a second, about 9.6 million foot-pounds, all of which becomes heat in the drums and linings in the few seconds of a hard stop. On level ground that is the whole job, and the drums cool before the next one. A descent is different in kind. Every foot a truck drops hands its brakes another 80,000 foot-pounds, so a thousand feet of elevation delivers about 80 million foot-pounds, more than eight times the energy of a stop from 60, not in a few seconds but continuously, mile after mile. Hold that truck to a steady 35 miles an hour down a 6 percent grade and it is descending about three feet every second, which is roughly 450 horsepower of heat that something has to absorb the whole way down. The engine, the air and the tires take a share. The rest is the brakes’. Seven Times the Energy is the same arithmetic on the flat; this is what it becomes when gravity joins it.

The temperature. The Federal Highway Administration worked the problem through a decade of studies, from 1979 to 1989, and came out with a thermometer. Its Grade Severity Rating System rests on the finding that “brake temperature at the bottom of a hill is mainly dependent on grade length and steepness, truck weight, and truck speed,” and on “a mathematical model that predicts brake temperature during descent.” The researchers, “After observing the onset of brake fade for a number of brake linings with typical amounts of imbalance,” “determined that 500 °F (260 °C) is an appropriate value for the maximum allowed brake temperature,” a ceiling chosen to “allow for emergency stops at the end of the downgrade, when brake temperatures are highest, without a significant loss in braking efficiency.” From the model comes a sign, the weight-specific speed sign, which tells a driver the fastest he can come down a given hill at his weight without crossing that line: the report’s sample reads “5 AXLES OR MORE / WEIGHT MAX SPEED / 65000-70000 35 / 70000-75000 25 / 75000-80000 15,” and in the report’s sample computer output for one particular grade an 80,000-pound truck gets a maximum safe speed of 14 miles an hour. “If these speeds are not exceeded, the system brakes will have sufficient braking capacity to bring the truck to an emergency stop at any point along the decline.” The speeds “are advisory in nature because they are impossible to enforce: police cannot easily assess gross truck weights” (FHWA, 1989). That is what fade is: not a part breaking, but a lining passing the temperature at which it still grips, so that the same pedal pressure slows the truck less, the driver presses harder, and the harder pressure makes more heat. The commercial driver’s manual describes the end of that curve plainly: brakes that get too hot fade, needing harder and harder pressure, and if the hard braking continues the truck may not be able to slow or stop at all (CDL manual, Section 2.16).

The engine. Because the brakes cannot carry a hill alone, the manual makes the engine the brake. “You must use the braking effect of the engine as the principal way of controlling your speed.” The gear is chosen at the top, not on the way down: “Shift the transmission to a low gear before starting down the grade,” and “Do not try to downshift after your speed has already built up,” because once the speed is there the driver may not get into a lower gear, or any gear, and all the engine braking is gone. The old rule was the same gear down as up; the manual says that newer trucks, with less friction and more power, may need a lower gear going down than the one that got them up, and that “You should know what is right for your vehicle.” The brakes are then used in short bites, not held: “Apply the brakes just hard enough to feel a definite slowdown,” release when the speed has dropped about five miles an hour below the safe speed, and repeat when it climbs back, a sequence the manual times at about three seconds per application. “Escape ramps are made to stop runaway vehicles safely without injuring drivers and passengers,” it adds, and “Escape ramps save lives, equipment and cargo” (CDL manual, Section 2.16). A driver who is in the right gear at the top of a hill is doing most of his braking with no brakes at all.

The adjustment. The heat does not land evenly. “Brake fade is also affected by adjustment,” the manual says: every brake on the truck has to carry its share, brakes out of adjustment stop working first, and the ones left take the heat for all of them and fade sooner, which is why “brake adjustment must be checked frequently” (CDL manual, Section 2.16). The law requires the whole set, with limited exceptions: “all brakes with which a motor vehicle is equipped must at all times be capable of operating” (49 CFR 393.48); “Each commercial motor vehicle must have brakes adequate to stop and hold the vehicle or combination of motor vehicles” (49 CFR 393.40); and on the road, “under any condition of loading,” a combination must be able to develop a braking force of 43.5 percent of its gross weight and stop from 20 miles an hour within 40 feet (49 CFR 393.52), the test The Air Brake describes on the flat, where the drums are cold. The carrier’s duty is to keep it that way: “Parts and accessories shall be in safe and proper operating condition at all times” (49 CFR 396.3). The federal crash study put a number on what happens when they are not. Among the 1,123 large trucks in its 963 crashes, “the brake problems associated factor is the most frequently coded (29 percent),” and “a truck with brake problems was 170 percent more likely to be coded with the critical reason for a crash” than one without them (FMCSA, Large Truck Crash Causation Study). A truck with one slack brake starts the hill with the others already carrying its load.

The ramp. When the brakes are gone, the only thing left that can take energy out of the truck is the road, and the state builds a piece of road for it. The Federal Highway Administration’s review describes escape ramps as “used by vehicles which have lost their braking capabilities and are out of control,” in six designs: “sandpile, gravity ramp, ascending grade arrester bed, horizontal grade arrester bed, descending grade arrester bed, and roadside arrester bed.” In an arrester bed “the rolling resistance offered by the material is the predominant means of decelerating the vehicle”; “The most common aggregates are pea gravel and loose gravel,” and the report tabulates their resistance as an equivalent uphill grade, loose gravel at 10 percent and pea gravel at 25 percent, which is to say a flat bed of pea gravel slows a truck like a hill it would have to climb at one foot up for every four along. The longest it found was “the 2480 ft horizontal grade arrester bed in Utah’s Parley’s Canyon on I-80” (FHWA, 1982). Texas now has a different kind. The mechanical ramp on El Paso’s Transmountain Road is “designed to safely stop a 90,000-pound truck traveling 90 miles an hour using a series of eight energy absorbing net stations bolted into reinforced concrete walls,” with a reset time “as short as four hours,” the fourth ramp on a road that already had three of gravel (TxDOT). Every one of them is the same statement in a different material: the engineers assume the brakes will fail, and they are paid to assume it.

The count. The 1989 report gathered what was known about how often they do. A survey it cites found “one in four drivers with mountain-driving experience had lost his brakes at least once during his career.” “One study of the number of annual runaways in the United States produced an estimate of 2,450,” and “Some 73 percent of the trucks involved in those runaway events carried loads heavier than 60,000 lb.” “Another study found that in one year, 16 percent of severe truck accidents nationwide involved runaways,” and “For one mountainous State that figure reached 41 percent.” The contributors that study named were “failure to downshift, defective brakes, inadequate signing, and inadequate driver information” (FHWA, 1989). The figures are decades old and the fleet has changed, but the list has not: the gear, the brakes, the sign and what the driver knew.

What the numbers do in court. Each of them has a job. The temperature and the engine rule set what the driver owed. The report’s whole premise is that drivers “can control speed and thus control brake temperature,” which makes fade a foreseeable consequence of a choice made at the summit rather than an act of God at the bottom; Texas asks for “a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the circumstances then existing” from no one, and tells a driver to slow when “approaching a hill crest” (Transportation Code Section 545.351), the duty of Reasonable and Prudent. A driver who rode the brakes down and lost them will reach for Sudden Emergency, and the instruction does not cover an emergency his own negligence produced. The adjustment and the maintenance rules set what the company owed, and breaking a federal safety regulation written to protect the people at the bottom of the hill is, unless excused, negligence in itself (Negligence Per Se); a brake the company knew was out of adjustment and sent down a mountain anyway is where gross negligence begins. The evidence is the engine recorder, which kept the speed and the gear; the drums and the linings, which an expert reads for the heat they took and the adjustment they were in; the maintenance file; the last roadside inspection; and the Logbook. All of it is in the company’s hands, and all of it can be repaired, sold or overwritten unless a letter stops it (Spoliation).

The science is settled. A brake stores heat it cannot shed, a lining has a temperature past which it lets go, and a truck in the wrong gear at the top of a hill arrives at the bottom with its brakes already spent. The question a Texas jury asks is whether the driver and the company sent it down as though they knew that.

The energy sums are arithmetic: kinetic energy is half the mass times the speed squared, with 80,000 pounds divided by 32.2 for the mass and 88 feet a second for 60 miles an hour; the energy of descent is the weight times the drop; a 6 percent grade at 35 miles an hour (51.3 feet a second) drops about 3.1 feet a second, and 80,000 pounds times 3.1 feet a second is about 246,000 foot-pounds a second, or about 450 horsepower at 550 foot-pounds a second per horsepower.
