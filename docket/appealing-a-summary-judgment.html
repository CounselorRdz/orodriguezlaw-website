Appealing a Summary Judgment

Leer esta página en español →

Part 3 of 3. A summary judgment ends a case without a trial. The judge rules that there is nothing for a jury to decide. When that ruling goes against an injured person, the appeal is the next chapter, and it is written almost entirely on paper.

This part follows one appeal, from the ruling in the trial court to the petition in the Supreme Court. Part 1 explained the courts; this is the road through them. Every step has its own rule and its own clock. It is general information, not legal advice, and the deadlines in an appeal are short. If you are facing one, talk to a lawyer now.

Know What You Lost

Texas has two kinds of summary judgment motions. A traditional motion argues “that no genuine issue of material fact exists.” A no-evidence motion argues “that there is no evidence of an essential element” of a claim or defense. One motion can combine both. A traditional motion must state its specific grounds, and a no-evidence motion must state the elements for which there is no evidence. Then the rule closes the field: “No judgment will be granted except on the grounds stated” (Rule of Civil Procedure 166a).

Since its 2026 rewrite, the rule runs on a tighter calendar, written to carry out a statute the Legislature passed. Unless the court allows otherwise or the parties agree, the response is due 21 days after the motion is filed and the reply 7 days after the response. The hearing or submission cannot be set within 35 days after the motion is filed, and it must be set within 60 days after filing, or 90 in the cases the rule lists. “No oral testimony will be received at a hearing on a summary judgment motion.” The court must sign a written ruling within 90 days after the hearing or submission date (Rule 166a; Government Code Section 23.303). The rewrite changed the deadlines; the comment to the rule says it was not meant to change the law otherwise.

Because no live testimony is taken, the evidence is what the parties put in writing: what was attached to the motion and the response, or pointed to specifically in the court’s file. The appeal is about that paper.

Is It Final?

An ordinary appeal to the court of appeals is taken from a final judgment (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 51.012). So read the order with one question in mind: did it end the whole case, every claim and every party? If the answer is not plainly yes, settle the question with a lawyer on the first day, not the thirtieth, because the deadline to file a notice of appeal runs from the day the judgment is signed (Rule of Appellate Procedure 26.1).

The Notice and the Clock

An appeal is perfected when a written notice of appeal is filed with the trial court clerk (Rule 25.1(a)). Among other things, the notice identifies the trial court and the case’s number and style, states the date of the judgment, states that the party desires to appeal, and names the court the appeal goes to (Rule 25.1(d)).

The notice is due within 30 days after the judgment is signed. If any party timely files a motion for new trial or a motion to modify the judgment, among the other filings the rule lists, the deadline becomes 90 days (Rule 26.1). Miss the deadline and the rule leaves a narrow opening: within 15 days after it, file the notice in the trial court and a motion for more time in the court of appeals (Rule 26.3). Promptly after filing the notice, the appellant files a docketing statement in the court of appeals (Rule 32.1).

The Record

The appellate record is the clerk’s record and, “if necessary to the appeal,” the reporter’s record (Rule 34.1). The clerk’s record includes, among other items, the docket sheet, the judgment being appealed and the notice of appeal (Rule 34.5(a)). In a summary judgment appeal, the papers that decided the case are the motion, the response, the evidence attached to each, any objections and the rulings on them, and the order. Several of them are not on the rule’s list of required items, so file a written designation with the trial court clerk for any that are missing (Rule 34.5). If something said at a recorded hearing matters, the appellant must ask in writing, at or before the time for perfecting the appeal, for the reporter’s record to be prepared (Rule 34.6(b)).

The record is due in the court of appeals within 60 days after the judgment is signed, or within 120 days when a post-judgment filing stretched the notice deadline to 90 (Rule 35.1).

The Brief

The appellant’s brief is due 30 days after the clerk’s record or the reporter’s record is filed, whichever is later (Rule 38.6(a)). It has eleven parts, in this order: identity of parties and counsel, table of contents, index of authorities, statement of the case, any statement regarding oral argument, issues presented, statement of facts, summary of the argument, argument, prayer and appendix (Rule 38.1).

Two lines in that rule deserve a second read. An issue “will be treated as covering every subsidiary question that is fairly included” (Rule 38.1(f)), so write issues broad enough to carry the whole argument. And the argument must be “clear and concise,” with citations “to authorities and to the record” (Rule 38.1(i)). A brief in the court of appeals may run 15,000 words if computer-generated, and a reply 7,500 (Rule 9.4(i)). The briefing rules are construed liberally, and substantial compliance is sufficient, though the court can order a deficient brief amended or redrawn (Rule 38.9).

The Standard

The court of appeals reviews a summary judgment de novo: it decides the question fresh, without deference to the trial judge. It takes “as true all evidence favorable to the nonmovant,” the party who opposed the motion (Provident Life & Accident Insurance Co. v. Knott, Supreme Court of Texas, 2003).

Then comes the rule that shapes every brief. When the trial court does not say why it granted the motion, the court of appeals “must affirm the summary judgment if any of the theories presented to the trial court” and preserved for review is meritorious (Knott). So the appellant answers every ground the motion raised. Leave one standing, and the judgment can stand on it.

For a no-evidence motion, the question is narrower: did the response produce summary judgment evidence raising a genuine issue of material fact? If it did not, the rule says the court must grant the motion (Rule 166a).

What Was Preserved

The court of appeals reviews what was asked for and decided below. A complaint is preserved when it was made to the trial court by a timely request, objection or motion, specific enough to make the court aware of it, and the court ruled on it, “either expressly or implicitly,” or refused to rule and the party objected to the refusal (Rule 33.1(a)). In a summary judgment fight, that matters most for objections to evidence. Read the objections and the rulings on them as carefully as the order itself (see Preserved).

Argument and Decision

A party who wants oral argument notes the request on the front cover of its first brief (Rule 9.4(g); Rule 39.7). The court may still decide argument is unnecessary, for example because the dispositive issue has been authoritatively decided, or because the facts and legal arguments are adequately presented in the briefs and record (Rule 39.1). A panel of three justices decides the appeal (Rule 41.1(a)) and writes an opinion that addresses every issue necessary to decide it (Rule 47.1).

After the Opinion

The losing side may move for rehearing within 15 days after the court of appeals’ judgment (Rule 49.1). The petition for review is due 45 days after that judgment, or after the court’s last ruling on a timely motion for rehearing or en banc reconsideration. Since January 1, 2026, the petition starts with an introduction, no more than 1,000 words if computer-generated, that identifies “the most persuasive grounds for the Court to grant review”; each issue presented must cite the record to show it was preserved; and briefs on the merits come only if the Court orders them (Misc. Docket No. 25-9104). The petition is limited to 6,500 words if computer-generated, introduction included; the count leaves out parts such as the table of contents and the index of authorities (Rule 9.4(i)).

That is the whole road: a motion decided on paper, a record made of paper, a brief, three justices, and maybe nine. The lesson that runs through every step is the one the trial court teaches first. On appeal, the case is the case you made below.
