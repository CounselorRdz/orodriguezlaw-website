The Left Turn

Leer este parte en español →

The most common way a car kills a motorcyclist is not a chase and not a blind spot. It is a left turn: a rider going straight, a driver turning across his path, and a gap that was never as wide as it looked.

The count. In 2024, 6,228 motorcyclists died on American roads, 16 percent of everyone killed in traffic, and mile for mile a motorcyclist was almost 27 times as likely to die as someone riding in a car. Of the 3,396 fatal crashes that year between a motorcycle and one other vehicle, in 45 percent the other vehicle was turning left while the motorcycle was going straight, passing or overtaking other vehicles (NHTSA). Texas counts two riders dead a day on average: 557 killed and 2,468 seriously injured in 2025, with 37 percent of the deaths at an intersection (TxDOT).

Why the driver does not see it. Often the driver was looking right at the motorcycle. The federal safety agency puts it plainly: most multi-vehicle motorcycle crashes happen because other drivers “simply didn’t see the motorcyclist” (NHTSA), and the Texas Department of Transportation runs a statewide campaign whose whole message is to look twice for motorcycles, especially before a left turn, because drivers “simply don’t see the motorcycle or misjudge its distance and speed” (TxDOT). Part of the reason is how the eye reads speed. The brain judges how fast something is closing mostly by how quickly its image grows, the same cue Looking, Not Seeing describes for a parked truck. A motorcycle coming head-on is a narrow image that grows slowly until the last moment, so a rider who is close and coming fast reads as farther away and slower than he is. Researchers measured it: drivers watching film of oncoming traffic judged that a motorcycle would reach them later than a car or a van arriving at the same instant, at 30 and 40 mph, by roughly half a second on average (Horswill, 2005). The authors attributed it to the size-arrival effect, and they tied it to the fact that drivers leave smaller safety margins when they pull out in front of a motorcycle than in front of a car. At 40 mph a motorcycle covers close to 60 feet a second, so half a second is two car lengths of gap that were never there; if the same half second holds at highway speed, where a motorcycle covers about 100 feet a second, it is more than three.

What the crash does to the body. The bike stops almost at once against the turning vehicle. The rider does not. He keeps going at close to his travel speed, over the bars and into the hood, the pillar or the pavement, often head first, which is what The Cage means when it says the rider is the crumple zone, and what Thrown Clear describes once the body is in the air. A helmet does not stop that. It buys time: the liner crushes, the stop takes a few milliseconds longer, and the peak force on the brain comes down. The federal estimate, carried by the CDC, is that helmets prevent 37 percent of deaths among riders and 41 percent among passengers (CDC; NHTSA). Of the motorcyclists killed in 2024 whose helmet use was known, 34 percent were not wearing one (NHTSA). That is a large share of the outcome, and it is not the cause of the crash.

Where the law comes in. The duty sits with the turning driver. To turn left at an intersection or into a driveway, a Texas driver “shall yield the right-of-way to a vehicle that is approaching from the opposite direction and that is in the intersection or in such proximity to the intersection as to be an immediate hazard” (Transportation Code Section 545.152). A motorcycle in plain view is that vehicle whether or not it was easy to judge, and the perception science cuts against the turning driver too: a driver who could not judge the gap had the option the state’s campaign recommends, which is to let the motorcycle pass. The rider’s speed is in the case as well, because the duty is measured against a vehicle close enough to be a hazard, and 37 percent of the riders in fatal crashes in 2024 were speeding (NHTSA); Failure to Yield explains how speed and sight lines move that question. The helmet is a separate question, and Texas keeps it separate. Riders 21 and older may go without one if they have completed a safety course or carry health insurance that covers a motorcycle crash (Section 661.003). When a rider was hurt without one, the rule the Texas Supreme Court wrote for seat belts is written broadly enough to reach it: a plaintiff’s “pre-occurrence, injury-causing conduct” can be weighed in the split of responsibility, but only on evidence that the nonuse “caused or contributed to cause” the injuries (Nabors Well Services v. Romero, the case The Belt explains). That is Comparative Fault at work: the rider’s share reduces the recovery, and it ends the claim only if that share passes 50 percent (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.001). Whether a rider wore a helmet goes to how badly he was hurt. It does not decide who turned.
