Stowers

Leer esta página en español →

In Texas a settlement offer can do more than ask for money. When an injured person offers to settle a covered claim within the policy limits, on terms a reasonable insurer would accept, an insurer that says no can end up answering for the whole judgment, including the part above its limits. The rule carries the name of a furniture company.

Stowers. The case was decided in 1929. A woman had sued a Texas furniture company for damages, and the company’s liability insurer took charge of the defense. The company later alleged that its insurer knew the case was “a very dangerous one.” According to the company, she offered to settle for less than the policy limit, and the insurer would not pay more than half of the limit. The case went to trial, the verdict came in well above the policy, and the furniture company paid the judgment and sued its insurer to recover it (G.A. Stowers Furniture Co. v. American Indemnity Co.). The case gave Texas a doctrine, which the Supreme Court of Texas has put in one sentence: “Under the doctrine established in G.A. Stowers Furniture Co. v. American Indemnity Co., an insured can sue his insurer for negligently failing to settle a third party’s claim against the insured” (Trinity Universal Insurance Co. v. Bleeker).

The rule today. In 2021 the Court stated it this way: “Under the Stowers doctrine, an insurer has a common-law duty to settle third-party claims against its insureds when it is reasonably prudent to do so.” And it listed when the duty arises: “The duty arises when (1) the third party’s claim against the insured is within the scope of coverage; (2) the settlement demand is within policy limits; and (3) the terms of the demand are such that an ordinarily prudent insurer would accept it, considering the likelihood and degree of the insured’s potential exposure to an excess judgment” (In re Farmers Texas County Mutual Insurance Co.).

The three conditions, in plain words. The claim has to be one the policy covers; the Court has said that “an insurer has no duty to settle a claim that is not covered under its policy” (as quoted in Farmers). The demand has to be within the limits; a demand above them does not start the duty. And the terms have to be ones a careful insurer would take, measured by how likely a larger verdict is and how large it could be. There is one more requirement in the cases: “a settlement demand must propose to release the insured fully in exchange for a stated sum of money” (Bleeker, quoting Texas Farmers Insurance Co. v. Soriano). In Bleeker the Court reversed a Stowers judgment because the claimants’ lawyer had never offered a full release, so the insured “cannot prove his Stowers case” (Bleeker).

Whose claim it is. The duty runs from the insurer to the person it insures, the driver or business that was sued. In the Court’s words, “an insurer, when defending its insured, acts as the insured’s agent and is bound to exercise ordinary care to protect the insured’s interests,” and “a Stowers claim sounds in tort” (Farmers). It is the insured who can sue the insurer for failing to settle, and in Bleeker the insured had assigned that claim to the people who were hurt (Bleeker).

What it changes. When the duty is breached and a judgment comes in above the limits, “the insurer is liable under the Stowers Doctrine for the entire amount of the judgment,” the Court said, quoting its own earlier opinion (Farmers). It also drew the line the other way: its precedent “has consistently recognized a Stowers cause of action only when the insured’s liability exceeds policy limits.” The Court has described the Stowers remedy, in words it quoted from 1994, as “shifting the risk of an excess judgment onto the insurer” (Farmers).

In a case. For a person hurt by a driver who carried a small policy, this is where the letter matters. A demand for a covered claim, within the limits, offering the insured a full release for a stated sum, on terms a careful insurer would accept, puts a choice in front of the insurer, and the law attaches consequences to that choice. A demand that is vague, conditional, or above the limits may not. The other side of a crash with too little insurance is your own coverage, which Consent to Settle covers. And none of it waits forever: the claim itself has a deadline (Limitations).

A policy limit is a number. Stowers is the reason that number is not always the end of the conversation.
