Enough

In Okinawa, home to some of the longest-lived communities on earth, they leave the table before they are full.

The practice has a name: hara hachi bu. Eat until you are eighty percent done, then stop. Not deprivation. Design. The body runs better below its limit, and the people who live this way tend to outlast everyone who does not.

Now listen to the most common complaint I hear from people who call me after leaving another firm. It is never about losing. It is this: “I never talked to my lawyer. They kept switching lawyers on me. Nobody ever called me back.” That is not a bad-apple problem. That is a full-stomach problem. A practice built on volume has to keep swallowing: more intake, more files, more inventory, until attention is the thing being rationed and the client can feel it through the phone.

The volume model works, in the way a buffet works. Speed, force, throughput. Settle it, stack it, next. Money comes in. Quality leaks out through a hundred small punctures: the deadline caught late, the record built thin, the client who becomes a file number and knows it. There is an old saying about appetite: pigs get fat, hogs get slaughtered. The line between the two is drawn in caseload.

The other model has a patron saint. Leonardo da Vinci left the world fewer than twenty paintings, and every one of them is guarded like a national border. He did not run out of commissions. He ran a standard: nothing leaves the shop unless it can stand forever. A legal practice can be built the same way. Fewer matters. Deeper preparation. Everything that carries your signature built to be impenetrable, because your signature is the product.

Enough is not a ceiling. Enough is a design specification. A caseload has its own hara hachi bu point, and past it you are no longer serving law. You are serving portions.

The move: define your eighty percent before the intake calls define it for you. Know the number of matters you can hold in your head at trial depth, stop one short of it, and let the word do the thing it was built for.

One word. The hardest one to say to new business, and the reason the work stays good.

Filed after midnight.
