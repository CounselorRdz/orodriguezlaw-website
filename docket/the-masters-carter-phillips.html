Carter Phillips

Leer esta página en español →

“I don’t do formal moot courts,” Carter Phillips told the Washingtonian in 2011. Later in the same answer he added, “I don’t need a dress rehearsal.” What he wants instead is a table of lawyers, some who worked on the case and some who did not, telling him what the questions will be. He is here for that roundtable.

He clerked for Judge Robert Sprecher of the federal appeals court in Chicago from 1977 to 1978 and for Chief Justice Warren E. Burger from 1978 to 1979. As an Assistant to the Solicitor General he argued nine cases in the Supreme Court for the United States. Then he joined Sidley Austin, where he is a partner, and the firm counts 82 more Supreme Court cases argued since, for a total of 91 oral arguments. In 2024 Northwestern University dedicated the Carter G. Phillips Center for Supreme Court and Appellate Advocacy and the Carter G. Phillips/Sidley Austin LLP Supreme Court Clinic.

The roundtable first. In place of a dress rehearsal he prefers, he said, less formal roundtable discussions with lawyers who did and did not work on the case, and he wants their reactions to what kinds of questions they think will matter at the argument. The fresh readers find what the team has stopped seeing. Underneath it is ordinary hard work: he reads the relevant cases and learns the record particularly well.

Then the questions. They have multiplied. “It’s gotten to the point now where in a 30-minute oral argument, you’ll get 70 questions,” he said in 2011. A lawyer cannot script that. He can only know the case well enough to answer whatever comes.

Then the rest. The night before an argument, he said, “I just stop at 5, try to do the best I can to not think about it.” And the day of: “I don’t get nervous the day of the arguments. You feel adrenaline, obviously. I get excited about it.” Before each argument he visits a statue of his mentor, Rex Lee, who called argument “the most fun thing you can do in the practice of law.” His own first argument, he said, “was actually the first time I’d ever appeared as a lawyer in a courtroom.”

Why he is here. Because a jury is a table of people who did not work on the case. Before trial, sit down with a few lawyers, and a few people who are not lawyers, who have never seen the file, tell them the story, and ask what they want to know; their questions are the jury’s questions (see Ted Wells on the story line). Because seventy questions in thirty minutes is a deposition too, and only a lawyer who knows the record can take them. Because Ross Guberman counts him in Point Made among the advocates he studied, beside Paul Clement and Seth Waxman. And because of three habits a trial lawyer can take from him. Find the questions before the court does, by asking people who come to the case fresh. Know the record better than anyone in the room. And stop the night before, so the lawyer who walks in is rested.

Most lawyers rehearse an answer. Phillips goes looking for the questions.
