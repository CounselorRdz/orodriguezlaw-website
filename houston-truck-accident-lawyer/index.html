What makes a truck case different

A loaded eighteen-wheeler weighs up to twenty times what a passenger car weighs. When it hits, the physics does not forgive: the energy grows with the square of the speed, and the body in the smaller vehicle absorbs almost all of it. That is why the injuries are so often catastrophic: brain injury, spinal cord damage, burns when the fuel ignites, and death.

And the truck runs under a manual a private car never has: the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. Those rules say how many hours a driver may drive, when he has to rest, what maintenance the truck must get, what history the company must check before hiring him, what drug and alcohol testing he faces, and what cargo he may carry. Every rule is a place where the company may have failed. Every failure is evidence.

The evidence that disappears if nobody asks

The trucking company does not wait to be sued before it starts moving. A rapid response team often reaches the scene within hours to photograph it, measure it, and talk to witnesses before anyone on your side has been retained. At the same time, the data that matters most has a short shelf life.

The electronic logging device can be overwritten. The black box data can be lost on a power cycle. Dash camera footage, if there is any, is gone once the unit goes back into service. The driver’s hiring and drug-testing file can be purged on the company’s own retention schedule. A preservation letter sent on the first day is what keeps that material alive. A lawyer who knows what to ask for is what makes the letter work. The black box and ELD page on this site explains what each record holds.

The truck cases I take

The crossing trailer

A truck pulled across the highway from a stop and its trailer blocked the lane. The law requires the one entering to yield to the one already coming, and there is still no federal side-guard rule.

The rear-end

A truck needs far more distance to stop. The driver who left too little room, or looked at a screen, answers for the impact. The rear guard on the trailer is part of the case too.

Rollover and lost cargo

A load never tied down, a tank half full and sloshing, a truck badly maintained. The rule and the defect are both in the case.

Driver fatigue

Hours of service violated, a logbook edited, an ELD manipulated. The FMCSA measures it and the ELD proves it.

Tanker and HazMat

Cases with fire or chemical exposure. The rules in 49 CFR Part 397 on hazardous materials come into the case.

The trucking company

Beyond the driver, the company answers for negligent hiring, thin training, and failed supervision. Its records are the proof.

Common Questions
Why is a truck accident different from a car crash?
A commercial truck weighs up to twenty times what a car weighs, it runs under the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations, and its company keeps hours-of-service, maintenance, and hiring records that a private car never has. The case is built on that evidence.
What evidence has to be preserved right away?
The ELD (electronic logging device), the truck's black box, the maintenance records, the driver's hiring and drug-testing file, the dash camera footage if there is one, and the dispatch logs. A preservation letter goes out on the first day.
How long do I have to file?
Generally two years from the date of the crash under Section 16.003 of the Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code. Against a governmental unit the clock is much shorter, often six months for written notice.
