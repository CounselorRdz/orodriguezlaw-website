Read the report.

The Texas crash report, box by box.

The crash report is the first official account of your crash, and insurance companies read it early. Read it as carefully as they do. It records what one officer saw and was told. It is not a verdict.

What it is

When a Texas officer investigates a crash that injured or killed someone, or did apparent damage of a thousand dollars or more to any one person’s property, the officer must file a written report with the Texas Department of Transportation, electronically, within ten days (Transportation Code Section 550.062). The form is the Texas Peace Officer’s Crash Report, form CR-3. TxDOT collects these reports from law enforcement agencies and calls itself “the custodian of record for the crash report only” (TxDOT). The officer’s agency wrote it.

How to get a copy

Buy it from TxDOT’s Crash Report Online Purchase System. A regular copy costs six dollars. A certified copy costs eight, and TxDOT says the certified copy is the one usually required for legal proceedings. Search by the name of a person involved or by a driver’s license number, and narrow it by county, city, date or plate. TxDOT says it can take up to fourteen days from the crash for a report to appear in the search; if it is still missing after two weeks, TxDOT points you to the agency that investigated (the purchase guide).

Who can get the full report. The law lets people directly concerned in the crash buy the unredacted report: a person involved, that person’s authorized representative, a driver, a driver’s employer, parent or guardian, the owner of damaged property, an insurer covering a vehicle or a person involved, a person who may sue over a death the crash caused, and others the statute lists (Transportation Code Section 550.065). To buy it you sign a certification under that section. Anyone else can buy a redacted copy, with names, license and plate numbers, addresses and similar details removed.

The boxes that matter

Unit 1
The report numbers each unit in the crash. TxDOT’s instructions to officers call it best practice to “List the motor vehicle which was the proximate cause of the crash as Unit 1” (CR-100, page 52). So notice which vehicle got the first number. It can be the officer’s first opinion about who caused the crash.
Injury severity
Each person gets a code. K means killed and N means not injured. A, B and C run from most to least serious; TxDOT’s own statistics call them suspected serious injury, suspected minor injury and possible injury, and older forms say incapacitating and non-incapacitating (code sheet; TxDOT). The code is the officer’s assessment, not a doctor’s diagnosis.
Contributing factors
The form gives officers a numbered list of factors, such as failed to control speed, driver inattention, followed too closely and unsafe speed. The instructions label this part of the form the investigator’s opinion, and they keep the factors the officer found contributing apart from the ones that may have contributed. A factor is where the investigation starts. Failed to Control Speed takes one of them apart.
Vehicle damage
The damage rating is a short code: a clock direction for where the force came from (1 to 12), letters describing the damage, and a severity number from 0 to 7. A low severity number is not proof of a low-force crash; Delta-V explains why the bumper is the wrong thing to measure.
Belts, airbags, ejection, alcohol and drugs, charges
The form has fields for whether each person was restrained, whether airbags deployed, whether anyone was ejected, alcohol and drug test results, and any charge or citation. Each one can matter later, the belt most of all. Each one can also be wrong.
Commercial vehicles
When a commercial motor vehicle is involved, the form has a section of its own for it. The truck’s own records go much further; Black Box and ELD Data explains them.
The narrative and the diagram
Last come the officer’s own words and sketch, in the boxes the instructions call the investigator’s narrative opinion of what happened and the field diagram, not to scale. The labels say what they are: an opinion, and a drawing that is not to scale.

What the report proves, and what it does not

A crash report can come into evidence. In a published opinion, a Texas court of appeals wrote that “Accident reports are admissible under Rule 803(8) as exceptions to the hearsay rule,” the exception for public records. The officer was not called to testify, and the report came in with his opinions about cause, because they rested on his investigation and nothing showed they were untrustworthy. In the same case the court said that “the question of causation was for the jury” (McRae v. Echols, Tex. App. Waco 2000).

An officer’s testimony is a separate question. In a 2011 case, a trooper who had not seen the crash testified about how it happened and who was at fault. The court held that the testimony was not admissible as an ordinary witness’s opinion, that the side offering it had to show the trooper was qualified as an expert, and that whether an officer is qualified “depends on the facts of each case”; it found the error harmless in that case (Lopez-Juarez v. Kelly, Tex. App. Texarkana 2011).

So read the report for what it is: an early record, often written by someone who did not see the crash. The vehicles, their data, the photographs and the witnesses carry the case. The report tells you where to start looking.

When something in it is wrong

If a name, a direction, a lane or a code is wrong, start with the agency whose officer wrote the report; TxDOT holds it only as the custodian of record. Ask early, in writing, and keep a copy. Your own photographs, the names of the witnesses, and the medical records made that day often say more than the report does.
