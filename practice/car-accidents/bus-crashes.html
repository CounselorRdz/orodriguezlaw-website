Bus crashes.

Passengers, drivers, and everyone on the road around a bus.

A bus crash puts many people into one record: the passengers, the bus driver, the drivers around the bus, and the company or public agency that runs it. Each of them has a different claim, every claim has a deadline, and a claim against a government can require notice within six months.

Who answers

Many bus crashes involve more than one driver, and Texas does not make an injured person pick one. The jury, or the judge if there is no jury, assigns a percentage of responsibility to each claimant, each defendant, each settling person and each designated responsible third party (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 33.003; see Comparative Fault). A driver who was on the job when the crash happened usually brings an employer into the case (see Respondeat Superior).

If you were a passenger

A bus line that offers rides to the general public, like a city transit system, is generally a common carrier, and Texas asks more of a common carrier toward its passengers than it asks of other drivers. In 2020 the Supreme Court of Texas held that San Antonio’s public transit authority “is a common carrier that owes its passengers the duty to exercise a high degree of care,” which means acting as “a very cautious and prudent person” would act under the same or similar circumstances. The duty is not a guarantee; it does not make carriers “strictly liable as insurers” (VIA Metropolitan Transit v. Meck; see Common Carrier).

The rule has been challenged. In Meck the authority asked the Court to overrule it and hold that carriers owe only ordinary care. The majority declined to take that up in that case, because applying the ordinary duty would not have changed the outcome, and a concurring opinion for four of the nine justices called the rule “an anachronism that Texas should abandon.” So a passenger’s case should name the high degree of care early, in the pleadings and in the charge, and build proof that would meet ordinary care too.

The higher duty is a duty to passengers. Toward everyone else on the road, the drivers, the cyclists and the people on foot, the measure is ordinary care, along with the traffic laws and safety rules that apply to buses.

When the bus belongs to a government

In Houston, METRO is a public transit authority operating under Chapter 451 of the Transportation Code. In a 2006 contract case, the Supreme Court of Texas held that the “sue and be sued” language in Section 451.054 “is not alone a clear and unambiguous waiver of immunity” (Metropolitan Transit Authority v. M.E.B. Engineering). For an injury, the way in is the Tort Claims Act. The same chapter makes a metropolitan rapid transit authority a governmental unit under that Act (Transportation Code Section 451.052(c)). The Act waives immunity for an injury caused by an employee’s negligence within the scope of employment when the injury arises from the operation or use of a motor-driven vehicle and the employee would be personally liable under Texas law (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.021). In Meck the Court held that the waiver reaches a common carrier’s negligence under the high-degree-of-care duty when that duty applies (see The One Door).

Six months
The Act requires notice of the claim not later than six months after the day of the incident, describing the injury, the time and place, and the incident. When the bus belongs to a city, the city’s charter or ordinances can set a shorter deadline (Section 101.101(b)). The formal notice is not required if the governmental unit had actual notice of the death, the injury or the property damage (Section 101.101(c); see Notice). Actual notice is something you may have to prove. A written notice, sent early, is proof you already have.
The cap
The Act caps the damages a governmental unit can be made to pay, and the amount depends on what kind of governmental unit it is (Section 101.023).

Your own coverage

If the driver who caused the crash carried too little insurance or none, your own auto policy may still answer. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage protects insureds who are legally entitled to recover from the owner or operator of an uninsured or underinsured motor vehicle (Insurance Code Section 1952.101; see Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims). Who counts as an insured is written in the policy, so read it before anyone tells you it does not reach a passenger.

If you drive the bus

A bus driver hurt on the job by someone else’s driving may pursue workers’ compensation benefits, if the employer carries that coverage, and, separately, damages from the third party who caused the injury, subject to the compensation carrier’s right of subrogation (Labor Code Section 417.001; see Third-Party Claims).

What to keep

The records
Ask every party, in writing and early, to keep what it has: any video, the vehicle’s data, the route and dispatch records, and the maintenance records. The record of a bus crash is spread among a transit agency or a bus company, the other drivers and their insurers, and it is easiest to keep before it is lost (see Spoliation).
Your own record
Write down the route, the bus number, the time, and where you were sitting or standing. Keep the names of other passengers and of witnesses. Take photographs. Keep the medical records from that day.
The crash report
If an officer investigated, the crash report is the first official account of the crash. Read it carefully, and read it for what it is (see Read the Report).
