Counties I take cases from

Maverick County (Eagle Pass); Webb County (Laredo); Starr County (Rio Grande City and Roma); Hidalgo County (McAllen, Edinburg, Mission, Weslaco, Mercedes); Cameron County (Brownsville and Harlingen); Zapata County (Zapata); Dimmit County (Carrizo Springs); La Salle County (Cotulla); Bexar County (San Antonio); and the Eagle Ford counties between them. If your county is not on the list, ask. The answer is usually yes.

The roads from Eagle Pass to Brownsville carry the Eagle Ford oilfield, the freight coming north out of Laredo, and the produce and tankers of the Valley, on highways that were laid out for pickups and farm trucks. When a family down here loses someone to a commercial truck, the company on the other side has a lawyer before the ambulance leaves. This page is for the families on those roads: what these wrecks are, what the law does with them, and how a case from here actually gets worked.

The Texas Department of Transportation counts crashes in the state’s energy regions separately, because those roads carry what the oilfield needs. In 2024, 1,023 people died in those five regions, one in four of all Texas traffic deaths; the Eagle Ford alone, which begins in Maverick and Webb counties at the border, saw 14,518 crashes and 173 deaths. TxDOT’s own explanation is a single sentence: oil and gas production puts large trucks and heavy machinery on rural roads, sharing them with local traffic. Add the truck freight crossing at Laredo, the port the Bureau of Transportation Statistics ranks first for trade with Mexico, and you have the most truck-dense corridor in the state running through some of its smallest towns.

The corridor

Interstate 35 from Laredo north through Cotulla and Pearsall to San Antonio, the freight spine, where a car that meets a tractor-trailer at highway speed meets it with the wrong part of the car. U.S. 57 from Eagle Pass east to I-35, the road TxDOT has studied for interstate designation because of what it already carries. U.S. 83 down the river from Laredo through Zapata, Roma, and Rio Grande City to McAllen, and on to Harlingen and Brownsville. U.S. 281 north out of Edinburg through the brush country. U.S. 77 and Interstate 69 up from Harlingen. And the farm-to-market roads and Military Highway between them, where a two-lane intersection with a stop sign meets a loaded truck at seven in the morning.

Each of those roads has its own pattern. The interstate produces the underride, the rear-end into a slowed truck, the wrong-way head-on after midnight. The farm roads produce the intersection crash, the T-bone, the failure-to-yield that a trooper writes on the first morning before anyone has pulled the truck’s data. The oilfield roads produce the rollover of a loaded water or sand truck, the crush at a lease gate, the fatigued driver at the end of a long haul. The Truck Country dispatch on this site follows those patterns week by week, without names.

Why a truck case here is not a bigger car case

A commercial truck carries its own witness: an engine control module, an electronic logging device, dash cameras, dispatch records, the driver’s hours, the maintenance file, and the carrier’s hiring file. All of it can be overwritten, lost, or explained away in the first weeks. The first thing I do on a truck case anywhere in South Texas is put the carrier on written notice to preserve it. The black box and ELD data page explains what is on the truck, and the trucking company liability page explains why the company, not just the driver, answers for hours, training, maintenance, and the decision to run a trailer with a thirty-mile-an-hour guard on a road where traffic runs seventy.

The physics is on this site too. Underride explains why a car that goes under a trailer never uses the front end it was built with. Guard explains what federal law requires of the bar on the back of the trailer, and why it requires nothing on the side. The Third Collision explains why the injuries in these wrecks are what they are even when the car looks intact.

How I work a case here from Houston

I am a Houston trial lawyer with one office, and I do not pretend otherwise. What that means in practice is this. The case is built on records, and the records are the same whether the crash was on Interstate 10 in Houston or on FM 491 south of Mercedes: the crash report, the truck’s data, the medical file, the reconstruction. I get those from wherever they are. The depositions happen where the witnesses live. I take them there. The trial happens in the county where the case belongs, and I try it there.

Where the case belongs is a question Texas law answers. Under the general venue rule, a suit may be filed in the county where all or a substantial part of the events occurred, or in the county where a corporate defendant keeps its principal office in Texas (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 15.002). For a family in Eagle Pass or Rio Grande City, that usually means the case can be filed at home, in the state district court for Maverick or Starr County, in front of a jury from that county. When a case belongs in federal court, the Southern District of Texas sits in Laredo, McAllen, and Brownsville, and I am admitted there.

Two clocks run from the day of the crash. The general limitations period for injury and wrongful death is two years (§ 16.003). When a claim is against a city, a county, or the state, because of the road, the barrier, or a government vehicle, a written notice must reach the governmental unit within six months (§ 101.101), and some cities require it sooner. Those deadlines do not move because the courthouse is a long drive from Houston.

The lawyer from out of town

Families here sometimes ask whether they should hire a lawyer from Houston or San Antonio instead of one down the street, and the honest answer is that it depends on the case. A small case belongs with a good local lawyer. A serious one, against a trucking company or an oilfield operator with a defense firm already assigned, belongs with whoever will actually try it. The insurance company knows which lawyers settle and which ones pick juries. That reputation sets the value of your case, not the address on the letterhead, and not the award badges and directory listings that lawyers pay for. You will not find those here.

What I bring is the same thing I bring in Harris County. One lawyer on the file. Every deposition, hearing, and argument taken personally. A caseload kept small enough that yours is never a number. A trial date treated as the goal, not a threat. And Spanish, which on this site is not a translation layer added afterward. Every page has a Spanish twin written for the people who read that way first, so a parent can read the same thing a son or daughter reads in English, and every conversation with me can happen in either language.

Common Questions
Do you take cases in Eagle Pass, Laredo, and the Rio Grande Valley?
Yes. I take serious injury, truck, and industrial cases from Maverick, Webb, Starr, Hidalgo, Cameron, and the surrounding counties, and from San Antonio. The office is in Houston; the work happens where the case is.
Where will my case be filed?
Usually in the county where the crash happened, under the Texas venue rule, which means a jury from your own county. A case against a trucking company can sometimes be filed where the company keeps its principal office. Federal cases from this region go to the Southern District of Texas in Laredo, McAllen, or Brownsville.
Do I have to travel to Houston?
No. The first conversation is a phone call or a video call, in English or Spanish. Depositions and hearings happen where the witnesses and the court are, and I travel to them. You will not be handed to a case manager.
How long do I have?
Two years from the injury or death for most claims. If the claim is against a city, county, or the state, a written notice is due within six months, and some cities require it sooner. Call before the first deadline, not after.
