More than sixty questions people ask after a crash, a work injury or a death in Texas, in seven groups. Each answer starts with the answer, names the law behind it, and links to the page that goes deeper.

These are general answers about Texas law, not advice about your case. Every case turns on its own facts, and the clock starts the day it happens. If your question is not here, ask me.

Car crashes

How long do I have to file a lawsuit after a car crash in Texas?

Usually two years. Texas gives most injury and death claims two years from the day the claim accrues (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.003). Some rules can delay or pause that clock. A claim against a city, a county or the state has a much shorter first deadline: notice of the claim, within six months or less. Do not wait to learn which rule is yours.Read: Limitations; The One Door.

Can I still recover if the crash was partly my fault?

Yes, if your share is fifty percent or less. Texas divides fault among everyone responsible and reduces your recovery by your percentage. If the jury puts you above fifty percent, you recover nothing (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Chapter 33). Many cases are fought over that one line.Read: Comparative Fault.

I wasn’t wearing a seat belt. Does that end my case?

No. Since 2015 a Texas jury may hear whether you were belted, but only if the other side shows that not wearing the belt actually caused or added to your injuries, and the judge decides that question first (Nabors Well Services v. Romero, Tex. 2015). Even then, the belt goes to how fault is divided. It can reduce what you recover; it does not erase what the other driver did.Read: The Belt.

The driver who hit me has no insurance. What now?

Look at your own policy. Texas insurers must offer uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, and it is part of your policy unless you rejected it in writing (Insurance Code Section 1952.101). It pays what the driver at fault should have paid, up to your limits. Many people carry it without knowing it.Read: Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims; Contact.

The driver who hit me drove away. Can I still recover?

Often, through your own uninsured motorist coverage. When the driver is never identified, a Texas policy requires actual physical contact between the unknown driver’s vehicle and you or your vehicle (Insurance Code Section 1952.104). The Texas Supreme Court has held that a detached axle is not contact with the vehicle itself (Nationwide v. Elchehimi, Tex. 2008). A driver in a crash that injures or kills someone must stop, find out whether anyone needs help, and stay; leaving is a crime (Transportation Code Section 550.021), and in the civil case, leaving is evidence.Read: Render Aid; Contact.

Does the other driver’s ticket prove they were at fault?

Not by itself. What matters is the rule the driver broke. Going over the posted limit is prima facie evidence that the speed was unreasonable (Transportation Code Section 545.352). But a rule that only tells drivers to act with care, like the rule on following distance, leaves the question to the jury (Louisiana-Pacific Corp. v. Knighten, Tex. 1998).Read: Negligence Per Se.

A car hit me while I was walking. Do I have a case?

Often, yes. Where no signal controls a crosswalk, a driver must stop and yield to a person crossing in it on the driver’s half of the road, or close enough from the other half to be in danger, and every driver must exercise due care to avoid hitting a pedestrian (Transportation Code Sections 552.003 and 552.008). A pedestrian’s own choices can reduce the recovery under comparative fault. They do not erase the driver’s duty.Read: On Foot; I Never Saw Them.

A city bus or a county vehicle hit me. Is that different?

Yes, and the first deadline is shorter. A Texas government can be sued for a crash only through the Tort Claims Act, which allows claims that arise from an employee’s operation or use of a motor vehicle (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.021). The government is entitled to notice of the claim within six months, and a city charter can set a shorter period (Section 101.101). Put it in writing. The Act also caps damages.Read: The One Door.

The driver was drunk. Can I sue the bar that served him?

Sometimes. Under the Texas Dram Shop Act, a seller can be liable if it was apparent when it served the person that he was obviously intoxicated to the point of being a clear danger to himself and others, and his intoxication was a proximate cause of the harm (Alcoholic Beverage Code Section 2.02). The proof is what the bar saw and did that night.Read: Who Served Him.

I was a passenger. Whose insurance pays?

Start with the driver at fault, who in Texas must carry at least thirty thousand dollars for one person’s injuries and sixty thousand for everyone hurt in the crash (Transportation Code Section 601.072). Personal injury protection on the car you rode in can pay its passengers’ medical bills and lost income no matter who caused the crash, unless it was rejected in writing (Insurance Code Section 1952.151 and following). Your own uninsured motorist coverage may add more.Read: The Passenger.

The driver who caused the crash died. Can I still make a claim?

Yes. In Texas a personal injury claim does not end because the person responsible died. It survives against that person and the person’s legal representatives, and the suit may go forward as if the person were alive (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 71.021).Read: The Pole.

Truck crashes

Who can be responsible for a truck crash besides the driver?

Often the trucking company, in two ways. It answers for a driver acting in the course and scope of the job. And it answers for its own failures: hiring a driver it should have screened out, failing to train, pressing illegal hours, skipping maintenance. A broker that picked the carrier carelessly (Crane v. Penske, 5th Cir. 2026), and the company that loaded the cargo, can be in the case too.Read: Trucking Company Liability; Crane v. Penske; Shed.

How many hours can a truck driver drive?

Up to 11 hours of driving, for a property-carrying driver in interstate trucking, inside a 14-hour window that opens only after 10 consecutive hours off duty. The driver must take a 30-minute break after 8 hours of driving and may not drive after 60 or 70 hours on duty in 7 or 8 days (49 CFR 395.3). The rule speaks to the company first: no motor carrier may permit or require a driver to break it.Read: The Clock; Logbook.

What records does a trucking company keep?

More than most people expect. The driver’s hours, now kept on electronic logging devices (49 CFR Part 395). The maintenance and driver qualification files, behind a duty to systematically inspect, repair and maintain the truck (49 CFR 396.3). And after certain crashes, drug and alcohol test results, or a written reason the test was not done (49 CFR 382.303). The truck’s engine module and cameras keep a record of their own. Ask for all of it early.Read: Logbook; Black Box and ELD Data; The Jackknife; The Test He Refused.

Does a truck have a black box?

In effect, yes. The engine control module logs speed, throttle and hard braking around a crash, and the electronic logging device records the driver’s hours. With dashcam video, telematics and the company’s files, that data can reconstruct what happened and whether the rules were followed. Data like this does not keep itself, which is why a preservation letter goes out early.Read: Black Box and ELD Data; Spoliation.

Does the trucking company have to drug and alcohol test the driver after a crash?

After a crash that took a life, yes. Federal rules require the motor carrier to test each surviving driver who was performing safety-sensitive functions, for alcohol and for controlled substances, as soon as practicable. After a crash with an injury treated away from the scene, or a vehicle towed away, the tests are required when the driver is cited for a moving violation arising from the crash. If the alcohol test is not given within two hours, the company must write down why (49 CFR 382.303).Read: The Test He Refused.

Do trailers have to have underride guards?

On the back, yes. On the sides, no federal rule requires them. Federal standards set the strength and the placement of rear guards on trailers of ten thousand pounds and up built since January 1998, with the bottom edge no more than about twenty-two inches off the ground, and the carrier must keep the guard in shape every day the trailer runs (49 CFR 571.223, 571.224 and 393.86). A 2023 federal notice said there were no federal requirements for side guards on trailers (88 Fed. Reg. 24535).Read: Guard; Underride.

Why does a loaded truck need so much more room to stop?

Weight, and the way air brakes work: the air takes a half second or more to reach the brakes. Under the federal standard, most loaded tractors must be able to stop from 60 miles an hour within 250 feet; a 2009 rule cut that from 355 feet for tractors built from 2011 or 2013 on (49 CFR 571.121). That is a test of a new truck on a test track, counted from the moment the brake control moves. The driver’s perception and reaction come on top, and brakes out of adjustment or hot from a long grade stop longer.Read: The Air Brake; Brake Fade.

Something fell off a truck and hit my car. Who is responsible?

Usually the trucking company, and sometimes whoever loaded the truck. If it was cargo, look at the loading and the tie-downs. Federal rules require cargo to be secured so it cannot leak, spill, blow or fall from the vehicle, and require the driver to check the load within the first 50 miles and again during the trip (49 CFR 393.100 and 392.9). Texas also requires a vehicle hauling loose material to be covered or enclosed so nothing blows or spills out (Transportation Code Section 725.021). If it was a part of the truck, the question is the carrier’s maintenance (49 CFR 396.3).Read: Securement; Shed; Contact.

Does a truck driver need a medical certificate?

Yes. A commercial driver must not drive unless medically certified as physically qualified, after an exam by a medical examiner listed on the federal National Registry (49 CFR 391.41 and 391.43). The agency that runs the registry says a DOT physical is valid for up to 24 months, and an examiner can certify a driver for less.Read: The Card.

What does “out of service” mean for a truck?

It means the truck may not go back on the road until it is fixed. Inspectors declare a vehicle out of service when its mechanical condition or loading would likely cause a crash or a breakdown, and no carrier may let anyone drive it until the repairs are done (49 CFR 396.9). A driver can be put out of service too, for hours-of-service violations (49 CFR 395.13).Read: Out of Service.

Work injuries and plant incidents

Can I sue my employer if I’m hurt at work in Texas?

It depends on whether your employer carries workers’ compensation. If it does, comp benefits are generally your only remedy against the employer (Labor Code Section 408.001). If it does not, it is a nonsubscriber, and you can generally sue it directly for an injury its negligence caused. Either way, other companies at the site can usually still be sued, unless one of them bought your comp coverage and so stands in your employer’s place.Read: Nonsubscriber Work Injuries; Third-Party Claims; When the Driver Dies; Statutory Employer.

What is a nonsubscriber, and why does it matter?

A Texas employer that chose not to carry workers’ compensation. When its injured worker sues, it cannot argue that the worker was contributorily negligent, that the worker assumed the risk, or that a fellow employee caused the injury (Labor Code Section 406.033). The worker still has to prove the employer’s negligence caused the injury. Texas employers must tell workers whether they carry comp.Read: Nonsubscriber Work Injuries.

My employer has workers’ comp. Can I sue anyone else?

Usually, yes. Comp may be your only remedy against your employer, but it leaves your claim against other companies whose negligence caused the injury: a general contractor, another subcontractor, an equipment maker, the property owner. A company that provided your comp coverage can claim your employer’s shield. You can pursue comp benefits and the claim against the third party at the same time (Labor Code Section 417.001).Read: Third-Party Claims; Statutory Employer; When the Driver Dies.

I work for a contractor and got hurt at someone else’s plant. Can I sue the owner?

Sometimes, but Texas sets a high bar. When a contractor’s employee is hurt while building, repairing, renovating or modifying an improvement to real property, the owner is not liable unless it exercised or kept some control over how the work was done, beyond the right to start or stop the work, inspect progress or receive reports, and it had actual knowledge of the danger and failed to warn adequately (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 95.003).Read: Chapter 95.

A worker died on the job. Can the family sue the employer?

Sometimes. If the employer carried comp, the family’s remedy against it is the death benefits, with one exception: the surviving spouse or the heirs of the body may recover exemplary damages when the death was caused by the employer’s intentional act or omission or its gross negligence (Labor Code Section 408.001). If the employer had no comp, the family can sue it directly. Claims against everyone else stay open.Read: When the Driver Dies; Gross Negligence.

Can I be fired for filing a workers’ comp claim?

Not for that reason. Texas law forbids firing or otherwise discriminating against an employee because the employee filed a workers’ compensation claim in good faith, hired a lawyer for the claim, started a proceeding, or testified in one (Labor Code Section 451.001). A worker fired for it can recover reasonable damages and is entitled to reinstatement; the worker carries the burden of proof (Section 451.002).Read: Si reclamo, ¿me corren? (in Spanish).

Who picks my doctor under workers’ comp?

You make the first choice. Outside an emergency, an injured worker chooses a treating doctor from the approved list (Labor Code Section 408.022), or, if the carrier uses a certified network, from the network’s treating doctors in that service area (Insurance Code Section 1305.103). A worker who wants a different doctor can ask the Division of Workers’ Compensation, in writing, with the reasons.Read: The Company Doctor.

I’m paid in cash. Am I still an employee?

How you are paid does not decide it. The Texas workers’ compensation law defines an employee as a person in the service of another under a contract of hire, express or implied, oral or written, and says nothing about how the person is paid (Labor Code Section 401.012). What decides it, the Texas Supreme Court has said, is who has the right to control how the work is done (Limestone Products Distribution v. McNamara, Tex. 2002).Read: Me pagan en efectivo (in Spanish).

Does an OSHA citation prove my employer was negligent?

No, and the lack of one does not prove the employer was careful. The federal safety law says it does not enlarge or diminish the rights of employers and employees under any law about workplace injuries (29 U.S.C. Section 653(b)(4)). A citation can still be evidence of the safety standard and of what the company knew. The claim itself stands on Texas law.Read: Serious Violation.

What does a shelter-in-place order mean?

Go inside and stay there until officials say it is safe. The CDC’s steps: close and lock all windows and doors; turn off the fan, the air conditioner and the furnace; close the fireplace damper; choose a room on the highest floor possible; and listen for instructions. It is advice about the air outside, meant for a limited time. Afterward, the order and its times are a dated public record.Read: Shelter in Place.

Why can a chemical exposure seem minor and turn serious hours later?

Some gases do their damage deep in the lungs. The federal medical guideline for phosgene warns that its irritation can be mild and delayed, and that a patient may be free of symptoms for 30 minutes to 48 hours before respiratory damage becomes evident. If you were exposed and get worse later, tell a doctor, and make sure the change is in your medical records.Read: Hours Later.

Wrongful death

Who can bring a wrongful death claim in Texas?

The surviving spouse, the children and the parents of the person who died (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 71.004). Adopted children are included; brothers, sisters and grandchildren are not. One or more of them can bring the claim for all. If none of them files within three months after the death, the estate’s executor or administrator must, unless all of them ask that it not be brought.Read: Wrongful Death; Familia en otro país (in Spanish).

How long does a family have to file a wrongful death case?

Generally two years from the date of death (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.003). If a government is responsible, notice of the claim is usually due within six months of the incident, and some cities require less. Work from the earliest date, not the latest.Read: Wrongful Death; Limitations.

What is the difference between a wrongful death claim and a survival claim?

They belong to different people. The wrongful death claim belongs to the spouse, the children and the parents, for their own losses (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 71.004). The survival claim is the claim the person who died would have had, kept alive for the heirs, legal representatives and estate (Section 71.021); it can include the pain and anguish the person consciously suffered before death. A death case can carry both.Read: Conscious; Wrongful Death.

Can the family recover for what the person went through before dying?

Yes, through the survival claim, for the conscious pain and the mental anguish the person suffered before death. The proof is the whole question. The Texas Supreme Court has made clear that time to see an injury coming is not proof that the person saw it coming (United Rentals North America v. Evans, Tex. 2023).Read: Conscious.

Our family lives in another country. Can we still bring the claim?

Yes. The wrongful death statute names the surviving spouse, children and parents and sets no requirement that they live in Texas or in the United States (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 71.004). Distance changes the logistics, not the right.Read: Familia en otro país (in Spanish).

Does a criminal conviction help the family’s civil case?

It can. A final felony conviction entered after a trial or a guilty plea, but not a no-contest plea, can be admitted in a later civil case to prove facts essential to the judgment, if no appeal is pending (Texas Rule of Evidence 803(22)). Intoxication manslaughter is a felony. The civil case still decides the damages and everyone else’s share.Read: The Second Case.

Can a family recover punitive damages?

Sometimes. Texas calls them exemplary damages. They require proof of fraud, malice or gross negligence by clear and convincing evidence, a higher bar than an ordinary claim (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Sections 41.001 and 41.003). The law caps them in most cases, with exceptions for certain crimes, including intoxication manslaughter (Section 41.008).Read: Gross Negligence; The Second Case.

How does a family get the autopsy or the medical examiner’s report?

Ask the office that did the examination. A Texas medical examiner must keep full records, and they may not be withheld except under the Public Information Act, with a carve-out for photographs and X-rays (Code of Criminal Procedure Article 49.25). In Harris County the request goes in writing to the Institute of Forensic Sciences, which emails a PDF of an autopsy report at no charge to a requester who does not need a certified copy.Read: Inquest.

Other injuries

I slipped and fell in a store. What do I have to prove?

Four things: that the store knew or should have known about the condition, that it posed an unreasonable risk of harm, that the store failed to use reasonable care to reduce or eliminate the risk, and that the failure caused your injury (Keetch v. Kroger, Tex. 1992). The store is charged with knowing what a reasonably careful inspection would reveal (Corbin v. Safeway Stores, Tex. 1983).Read: Invitee; Premises Liability.

How long does a spill have to be on the floor before the store is responsible?

Long enough that the store should have found it, and you have to prove how long. Unless the store caused the condition or actually knew about it, Texas law asks for evidence of how long it was there. In a 2026 unpublished opinion, the Fifth Circuit held that seventy-five minutes of video showing no change to the floor, employees walking past and another shopper slipping eight minutes earlier did not show how long the water had been there (Canales v. Walmart Texas, 5th Cir. 2026).Read: Canales v. Walmart; Invitee.

A product hurt me. What do I have to prove?

That it was defective, in one of three ways: made wrong, designed wrong, or sold without the warnings or instructions needed to use it safely. A design claim also requires proof that a safer alternative design existed and that the defect was a producing cause of the injury (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 82.005).Read: Product Liability; Crashworthiness.

I had a bad back before the crash. Can I still recover?

Yes. The person at fault takes you as he finds you, and answers for the harm done to you as you were, including a condition you already had that the crash made worse (Coates v. Whittington, Tex. 1988; Weidner v. Sanchez). The defense will try to blame the old injury, so your medical history matters.Read: Eggshell.

Is a landlord responsible if the smoke alarm didn’t work in a fire?

It can be. A Texas landlord must install smoke alarms where the statute requires and test them at the beginning of the tenant’s possession (Property Code Sections 92.255 and 92.258). During the lease, the duty to inspect and repair arises when the tenant gives notice of a malfunction or asks for an inspection. What the records show about that test and that notice often decides the case.Read: Smoke Alarm.

I was hurt on an amusement park ride. Who inspects these rides?

Not the state itself. Under Texas law the owner’s insurance company inspects each ride once a year, the state issues a compliance sticker, and operators must report every quarter the injuries that need medical treatment (Occupations Code Chapter 2151). Those inspection and injury records are where a ride case starts.Read: Airtime.

The car’s design made my injuries worse. Is that a separate claim?

It can be. A crashworthiness claim asks whether the vehicle protected the people inside as it should have, even though the defect did not cause the crash; Texas recognized it in Turner v. General Motors (Tex. 1979). It requires proof of a safer alternative design, and a maker that complied with the federal safety standards gets a presumption that it is not liable, which the evidence can rebut (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Sections 82.005 and 82.008).Read: Crashworthiness; The Roof and the Tank.

Insurance and money

What is uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage?

The part of your own auto policy that pays when the driver at fault has no insurance, or not enough. Texas insurers must offer it, and it stays on the policy unless you rejected it in writing (Insurance Code Section 1952.101). Underinsured coverage pays what you are legally entitled to recover, up to your limit, reduced by what the other driver’s insurer pays or owes (Section 1952.106).Read: Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims; Consent to Settle.

What is personal injury protection (PIP)?

Coverage on a Texas auto policy that pays necessary medical, hospital and funeral expenses, and lost income for a person who earns one, no matter who caused the crash. It is included unless rejected in writing, and it covers the named insured, the household, and authorized drivers and passengers of the insured car, including guests (Insurance Code Sections 1952.151 through 1952.155).Read: The Passenger.

What are the minimum insurance limits in Texas?

Thirty thousand dollars for injury to one person, sixty thousand for injury to everyone hurt in the same crash, and twenty-five thousand for property damage (Transportation Code Section 601.072). In a serious crash, the other driver’s policy is often the smallest number in the case, which is why your own underinsured coverage matters.Read: Houston Car Accident Lawyer; Car Accidents.

Do I need my own insurer’s permission to settle with the driver who hit me?

Often your policy says you do. Many policies say underinsured coverage does not apply if you settle without the insurer’s written consent. The Texas Supreme Court has held that the insurer can rely on that clause only if it was actually prejudiced by the settlement (Hernandez v. Gulf Group Lloyds, Tex. 1994). That still leaves room for a fight, so read your policy and ask in writing before you sign anything.Read: Consent to Settle.

What is a Stowers demand?

A settlement offer within the policy limits of the person at fault. If a reasonable insurer would accept it and the insurer refuses, the insurer can end up answering for the whole judgment, even the part above its limits. The duty arises when the claim is covered, the demand is within limits, and its terms are ones an ordinarily prudent insurer would accept (In re Farmers Texas County Mutual, Tex. 2021). The demand must also offer the insured a full release for a stated sum (Trinity Universal v. Bleeker, Tex. 1998).Read: Stowers.

Workers’ comp paid my benefits. Does it get paid back from my injury case?

Usually, yes, out of a recovery from a third party. The comp carrier steps into your place against that third party and has a right to be repaid from what is recovered, limited to the benefits it paid or assumed and reduced by any share of responsibility assigned to the employer (Labor Code Section 417.001).Read: When the Driver Dies.

The other driver carries only the minimum. Is that the end of it?

Not necessarily. Your own underinsured motorist coverage can pay what you are legally entitled to recover beyond the other driver’s policy, up to your limit. Other parties may share responsibility: an employer whose driver was on the job, a bar that served a driver who was obviously intoxicated, an owner who handed the keys to a driver it should not have.Read: Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Claims; Respondeat Superior; Negligent Entrustment.

Deadlines and evidence

What is spoliation, and why does a preservation letter go out so early?

Spoliation is the loss or destruction of evidence a party had a duty to keep. In Texas the judge decides whether it happened and what to do about it, and the strongest remedy, an instruction telling the jury it may presume the missing evidence would have hurt the side that lost it, generally requires intent (Brookshire Brothers v. Aldridge, Tex. 2014). A letter sent early makes the duty plain before the video, the data and the vehicle are gone.Read: Spoliation; The Phone.

What does a car’s event data recorder record?

If the vehicle has one, the federal standard requires it to log, among other things, the vehicle’s indicated speed and whether the service brake was on or off in the seconds before a crash (49 CFR 563.7). That record can contradict anyone’s memory, including the driver’s. Get it downloaded before the car is repaired, sold or crushed.Read: The Blackout; Spoliation.

How do I get a copy of my Texas crash report?

Buy it from TxDOT’s Crash Report Online Purchase System: six dollars for a regular copy, eight for a certified one. It can take up to fourteen days after the crash for the report to appear. People directly concerned in the crash, such as those involved, their representatives and their insurers, can buy the full report; anyone else gets a redacted copy (Transportation Code Section 550.065).Read: Read the Report.

Is the police crash report evidence in court?

It can be, but it does not decide fault. A Texas court of appeals upheld admitting a crash report as a public record, including the officer’s opinions about cause, and in the same case said that causation was for the jury (McRae v. Echols, Tex. App. 2000). Live testimony is different: when an officer who did not see the crash testifies about how it happened, the side calling him has to show he is qualified as an expert (Lopez-Juarez v. Kelly, Tex. App. 2011).Read: Read the Report; Failed to Control Speed.

What do the codes on my crash report mean?

Each person gets an injury code: K for killed; A, B and C from most to least serious; N for not injured. Drivers can be given contributing factors from a numbered list, such as failed to control speed or driver inattention, and the instructions to officers label that part of the form the investigator’s opinion. A code is where the investigation starts, not where it ends.Read: Read the Report.

What is the six-month notice for a claim against a city or county?

A Texas governmental unit is entitled to notice of a claim within six months after the incident, describing the injury, the time and place, and what happened (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.101). A city charter can set a shorter period. The notice is excused only if the government had actual notice of the death, the injury or the damage.Read: The One Door.

What is a statute of repose?

A second clock, on top of the two-year deadline, that can bar a product claim no matter when the injury happened. In Texas a products liability action against a manufacturer or seller generally must be brought within fifteen years of the date that defendant sold the product, with exceptions for a written warranty of a longer safe life and for certain diseases that show up late (Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.012).Read: Repose.

Can what someone said right after the crash be used in court?

Sometimes. Texas allows an excited utterance, a statement about a startling event made while the person was still under the stress of it, even though it was said out of court (Texas Rule of Evidence 803(2)). Words shouted at the scene can qualify. A calm, considered statement made later is a different thing.Read: Excited Utterance.

My crash report says I “failed to control speed.” Is that a verdict?

No. It is one of the officer’s contributing factors. It can come into evidence and a jury may credit it, but causation is for the jury (McRae v. Echols, Tex. App. 2000). The box says the officer thought speed played a part. It does not say what the conditions were, what the other driver did, or whether the road gave anyone a fair chance.Read: Failed to Control Speed; Read the Report.
